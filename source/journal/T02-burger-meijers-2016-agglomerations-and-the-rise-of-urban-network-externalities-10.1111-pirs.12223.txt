Abstract. This paper introduces the theme of the special issue ‘Agglomerations and the Rise of
Urban Network Externalities’. Urban network externalities are deﬁned as external economies
from which ﬁrms and households can beneﬁt by being located in agglomerations that are well
embedded in networks that connect with other agglomerations. The contributions focus on the
conceptualization of urban network externalities and their inﬂuence on urban performance.
Finally, a research agenda is presented, that should focus on multiplexity and heterogeneity in
networks and their impacts; interrelations between agglomerations and networks and their
dynamic and place-based nature; and, the policy implications of urban network externalities.

JEL classiﬁcation: O18, R11, R12

Key words: Agglomeration economies, network externalities


1 Introduction to the special issue

Urban and regional planners, geographers and economists alike are interested in the forces that
create, shape and maintain concentrations of economic activity. A large body of empirical
literature has emerged that addresses economic geography, regional science, and urban econom-
ics and investigates how spatial circumstances give rise to agglomeration externalities – external
economies from which ﬁrms can beneﬁt through co-location (e.g., Glaeser et al. 1992; Henderson
et al. 1995; Combes 2000; Rosenthal and Strange 2003).
     In most of the early empirical studies, agglomeration externalities are deﬁned as being
geographically constrained, and it is assumed that economic activities outside a certain area
have no effect on the economic activities within that area (Rosenthal and Strange 2004). At
the same time, it is now well known that agglomerations are not islands and that in the present
global economy, most cities interact at least to a certain degree (Fingleton 2003; McCann
and Acs 2011). The growing awareness in the theoretical spatial literature of the rise of urban

introduction of new terms, such as ‘urban network externalities’ (Capello 2000), ‘regional

currently, national and international urban network embeddedness is more important for urban
productivity than urban size, while according to Coe et al. (2004) and Yeung (2009), urban and
regional development is becoming a ‘globalizing’ phenomenon. Similarly, Florida (2008)
perceives globally interconnected cities as the real engines of economic growth.
     This discussion not only suggests that the embeddedness of cities in regional, national, and
international networks is important for their performance but also that small and medium-sized
cities could potentially internalize the beneﬁts of larger cities by being well-positioned in urban
networks. Unfortunately, relatively little is known about the relative importance of urban
network externalities vis-à-vis agglomeration externalities or which types of cities would proﬁt
from urban network externalities. This special issue of Papers of Regional Science focuses on
the rise of urban network externalities: external economies from which ﬁrms and households
can beneﬁt by being located in agglomerations that are well embedded or positioned in networks
that connect with other agglomerations.

1.1 The spatial and network dimension of external economies

Paramount to the study of agglomeration externalities is the discussion of what constitutes a city or
industrial agglomeration. Prior to the Industrial Revolution, it was relatively easy to deﬁne a city.
As a general rule, a city could be identiﬁed as an agglomeration of people and businesses, separated
from the countryside by fortiﬁcations, gates, and towers. This characterization is not only reﬂected
in the Greek term polis (‘surrounding wall’) and the Latin word urbs (‘walled city’) but also in
the English ‘town’ (‘circular palisade’), the German zaun (‘hedge’), and the Spanish ciudad
(‘stronghold’) (Kerbat 1995). However, what primarily distinguished cities from rural areas in
medieval times were their speciﬁc legal status (notably ‘city rights’) and their political identity.
Medieval towns were usually autonomous, being free from feudal bondage and having their
own jurisdiction (Hohenberg and Lees 1995). Perhaps this was the moment in history when the
beneﬁts of cities could truly be labelled ‘agglomeration economies’ because in spatial terms, they
coincided perfectly with agglomerations and could not be enjoyed outside the city walls.
     Since the Industrial Revolution, we have witnessed the increasing spatial disintegration of
‘agglomeration’ and the ‘beneﬁts and costs’ known as ‘agglomeration economies and agglom-
erations diseconomies’. Indeed, cities have shifted their functional borders as mobility has
steadily increased and communication technology has developed further. Trams, trains, and
the telegraph in the nineteenth century and telephones, automobiles, planes, and the internet
in the twentieth century are examples of technology that have affected the spatial extent of
the city (Anas et al. 1998). Paralleling the ever-growing suburbanization in the early twentieth
century, the focus on urban research gradually shifted from the internal structure of cities
(‘city as a system’) to the external relations of cities, also referred to as ‘systems of cities’
or ‘urban systems’. The notion of urban systems would be formalized in the interbellum in
central place theory (Christaller 1933; Lösch 1944), which would later gain popularity in
the ﬁelds of economics and geography. Although Christaller’s and Lösch’s works predom-
inantly focused on explaining the distribution, size and number of cities and towns and the
location of economic activity, a common key element in their theories was that not all
places can be self-sufﬁcient and places are inherently interdependent.
     The academic literature that developed after the Second World War that would build on the
work of Christaller (1933) and Lösch (1944) followed two paths (McPherson 1981). On the one
hand, a number of regional science and urban economic studies have extended and modiﬁed the

formal theoretical model to arrive at a more general and realistic model of urban systems (Berry
et al. 1988). In particular, the regional science movement combined the work of Christaller and
Lösch with insights about empirical regularities in spatial distributions. These insights included
the rank-size rule and the distance-decay functions and gravity models as well as general
equilibrium models. Contemporary work on the external relations of cities that builds on this
literature includes urban economic models on polycentric cities and suburbanization (Fujita
and Ogawa 1982) and the evolution of city-systems (Mori 1997; Fujita et al. 1999) as well as
the complex cities and networks literature (e.g., Wilson 2000; Batty 2008; Reggiani and
Nijkamp 2009; Roth et al. 2011).1
     On the other hand, empirical research, mostly originating from the urban systems school
(see, e.g., Berry 1964; Pred 1977; Bourne and Simmons 1978), has empirically studied the
organization of urban systems and the nature of urban networks. In this literature, urban systems
have been generally deﬁned as a set of regionally, nationally or globally linked and interdependent
urban areas (Bourne and Simmons 1978). To date, this strand of research can be found in the
literature on world cities and global production networks (e.g., Taylor 2001; Derudder et al.
2010; Jacobs et al. 2010; Rozenblat 2010; Wall and Van der Knaap 2011)2 as well as the literature
on urban networks and regional spatial structure (e.g., Camagni 1993; Camagni and Salone 1993;
Batten 1995; Van Oort et al. 2010; Burger and Meijers 2012).3
     Despite the attention that has been given to the external relations of cities over the past
70 years, the literature on agglomeration externalities in the late twentieth century that has built
on the work of Marshall (1890) and Hoover (1948) predominantly focuses on the economics of
physical proximity and not on the economics of the external relations of cities. The seminal
work by Glaeser et al. (1992) and Henderson et al. (1995) that placed agglomeration externali-
ties in the centre of the economic debate linked economic geography to the endogenous growth
theory of Romer (1986) and Lucas (1988).4 The large empirical literature that has emerged
focuses on how ﬁrms are inﬂuenced by co-location and which spatial conditions cause greater
than proportional growth in productivity and economic activity. Most notably, one of the key
debates in this literature has been whether diversity (urbanization economies) or specialization
(localization economies) best promotes growth,5 where more recent contributions in the ﬁeld of re-
gional science and urban economics have also paid attention to ﬁrm heterogeneity (e.g., Henderson
2003; Baldwin et al. 2008; Ottaviano 2011; Stavropoulos and Skuras 2016) as well as examining
the channels of sharing, matching and learning through which agglomeration externalities occur
(Duranton and Puga 2004; Puga 2010).
     At the same time, discussions of the geographical scale of agglomeration externalities and
urban network externalities have been relatively scarce (Parr 2002; Johansson and Quigley 2003;
Phelps and Ozawa 2003; Burger et al. 2009; Camagni et al. 2015). However, it is unfair to say
that ideas on urban networks are completely absent in the literature on agglomeration externalities.
Already in 1931, the British economist Robinson introduced the distinction between immobile
external economies and mobile external economies. Focusing on the Marshallian-type of localization
externalities, Robinson (1931) argued that immobile external economies depended on the size of an
industry in a locality, while mobile external economies depended on the size of the industry in a
country or the world as a whole and can be shared by ﬁrms outside the locality from which they arise.

    In the 1970s, Alonso (1973, p. 200) would introduce the related concept of ‘borrowed size’,
by which he meant that ‘a small city or metropolitan area exhibits some of the characteristics of
a larger one if it is near other population concentrations’. More precisely, he suggested that
smaller urban areas ‘borrow’ some of the agglomeration beneﬁts of their larger neighbours,
while avoiding their agglomeration costs. The phenomenon of ‘borrowed size’ demands a
network perspective because accessibility and network connectivity to large city agglomeration
externalities are prerequisites for borrowed size to occur. Although Alonso predominantly took
a regional perspective, Meijers and Burger (2016) recently argued that the concept can be easily
generalized to higher spatial scales.

economies and urban growth, two strands of research are now gradually taking root.
These strands of research both suggest that agglomeration externalities are spatially
transforming into a sort of ‘agglomeration externality ﬁelds’, or into ‘urban network externalities’

the strict sense of the word. When agglomeration externalities are no longer nodal, but zonal, it
would make sense to refer to them as ‘externality ﬁelds’ (Phelps et al. 2001) or ‘regional
externalities’ (Parr 2002), and some authors have suggested distinguishing the traditional
agglomeration externalities from these ‘regional externalities’ (Parr 2002). Alternatively,
several studies have examined the spatial extent of agglomeration externalities using – among
others – spatial econometric techniques. In a recent overview provided by Drucker (2012), it
was however noted that the empirical evidence obtained so far is quite inconsistent in that some
studies ﬁnd that agglomeration externalities rapidly decay with distance (e.g., Rosenthal and
Strange 2003; Van Soest et al. 2006; Baldwin et al. 2008), while other studies conclude that
agglomeration externalities may well extend over 100 kilometres (e.g., Partridge et al. 2007;
Renski 2011). Most likely, distance decay depends on the industry, agglomeration type, and the
type of externality that is examined (Drucker 2012). Further research is needed but is beyond the
scope of this special issue.

1.3 Urban network externalities

Second, urban networks seem to provide a substitute for the beneﬁts of agglomeration. In this
regard, the performance of cities may become increasingly dependent on their position in urban
networks that can have a national, continental or worldwide scope, where external economies
become increasingly mobile. Capello (2000) introduced the term ‘urban network externalities’,8
referring to the beneﬁts originating from the functional relationships between places, where the
presence of functional networks between cities could create synergies and complementarities
(see also Camagni 1993; Boix and Trullén 2007; Van Oort et al. 2010). Related to the discussion
on urban network externalities has been the re-introduction of Alonso’s concept of borrowed size
(see Phelps et al. 2001; Burger et al. 2015; Meijers and Burger 2016). Borrowed size processes take
place when a city displays performance levels normally associated with larger cities. This is
enabled through interactions in networks of cities across multiple spatial scales that serve as a
substitute for the beneﬁts of agglomeration (Meijers and Burger 2016). Accordingly, small and
medium-sized cities could potentially internalize the beneﬁts of larger cities by being
well-positioned in urban networks. At the same time, it should be noted that network connectivity
can also have a negative effect on the performance of cities due to competition processes and not
every city proﬁts equally from high levels of network embeddedness.


2 Contents of the special issue

This special issue brings together a multidisciplinary team of authors with backgrounds in urban
economics, regional science, economic geography, and urban planning, providing different
perspectives and approaches in the study of networks and agglomerations. Broadly speaking, the
papers address three issues. First, the term ‘urban networks’ suffers from a lack of speciﬁcity,
and hence, the conceptualization of urban network externalities is not always clear. Second, some
provide evidence on the extent to which urban network externalities inﬂuence urban performance
and how they compare to traditional agglomeration externalities. Third, the heterogeneous
relationship between urban network externalities and urban performance is addressed because
not all places might proﬁt from network connectivity. Several of the papers address multiple
issues. Their main contributions to the debate on the rise of urban network externalities are highlighted
below.

     Glaeser et al. (2016) build a model of endogenous amenities and recombinant growth to
answer the urgent policy question of how to accommodate future urbanization: Would it be
more beneﬁcial to develop urban networks (groups of cities enabling the exchange of goods,
people and ideas) or foster the rise of larger or denser mega-cities? Their theoretical model
suggests that the answer to this question depends on the trade-off between local returns to scale
in innovation, housing supply elasticity, and the importance of local amenities. Furthermore,
historical development pathways and place-speciﬁc conditions need to be taken into account.
     Van Meeteren et al. (2016) call for prudence in the debate on agglomeration and networks,
as both are fuzzy concepts and need to be disentangled before one can arrive at conclusions re-
garding their signiﬁcance in explaining the performance of cities and regions. This disentangling
is less straightforward than it may seem as the concepts of agglomeration and network are inter-
related. Their conceptual paper provides a review of the concepts of agglomeration externalities
and urban network externalities and presents a combinatorial typology of agglomeration
and networks derived from three disciplinary perspectives (industrial organization, spatial-
economic, and geometrical). Subsequently, they demonstrate how their conceptual approach
can be adopted in empirical research on globalized producer services ﬁrms.
     One type of network is made up of international supply chains. Fujita and Hamaguchi
(2016) emphasize the beneﬁts of such networks in terms of increasing the efﬁciency of human
resources, productivity and welfare, and the potential for inclusive globalization. However,
longer and more complex networks come with a cost, as uncertainties and risks increase.
Volatility is intrinsic to international supply chains. Fujita and Hamaguchi document the process
of supply chain internationalization in East Asia, speciﬁcally focusing on its spatial implications,
and show how idiosyncratic supply shocks triggered by natural disasters affected trade patterns.
     The paper by Duranton (2016) explains city growth in Colombia. He includes a more com-
prehensive set of determinants than previous research, providing an elaborate review of a wide
variety of factors that are essential to understand urban growth, and simultaneously sketches
how these factors can best be empirically explored from the modelling point of view. A
thorough empirical analysis of these determinants is provided. Road connectivity and market
access are examples of factors associated with urban network externalities. Road connectivity
in Colombia primarily facilitates travel within cities and fosters growth, as it has done for more
than a century. However, somewhat mixed and scarcely signiﬁcant results are found for the im-
portance of being able to access other cities and their market potential in the Colombian context.
     The paper by Camagni et al. (2016) questions the too simplistic reasoning that further
agglomeration leads to growth, suggesting that further urbanization of large mega cities is not
the key to welfare increases, especially not in recent years. While larger cities have higher
productivity, urban growth does not necessarily imply increases in productivity. They call for
such a dynamic perspective, which demands: (i) a focus on net agglomeration beneﬁts rather
than gross beneﬁts as is typical for many studies; (ii) breaking down the factor ‘size’ into
multiple determinants, including network embeddedness; and (iii) the explicit introduction of
the time dimension in theory and empirical analysis. They ﬁnd that large and small cities have
the same opportunity to grow, which suggests that other factors than size drive urban growth.
These other factors include among others the increase in the presence of urban functions – in
the city itself but also in nearby places (‘borrowed functions’).
     Thissen et al. (2016) decompose the growth of European regions into demand-led growth
due to growing export markets and structural growth due to growing export market shares in
those export markets. The vast majority (80%) of regional growth (and possibly decline) is
explained by demand-led growth in export markets. As structural growth is mostly affected
by regionally varying characteristics, it can be inferred that these characteristics – including
regional policies – are of limited importance for the growth opportunities of regions. At the
same time, the authors show that regions operating on similar markets are affected by the same

type of externalities because they operate on the same markets in a trade network. In this regard,
these results suggest that recently advocated place-based development strategies of European
regions should be complemented with network strategies.
    Finally, the study by Meijers et al. (2016) argues that urban network externalities make it
possible to ‘borrow size’ in order to explain the performance of small and medium-sized cities
in Western Europe. They establish that the importance of agglomeration (size) and network con-
nectivity differs across metropolitan functions. Sometimes network connectivity is more impor-
tant than size in explaining the presence of certain metropolitan functions in cities, suggesting
that networks can indeed substitute for size. It is also shown that small cities proﬁt more from
an increase in size, whereas larger cities proﬁt more from an increase in network connectivity.


3 Towards a research agenda

Based on a synthesis of contributions to this special issue and the existing literature, the contours
of a research agenda on the rise of urban network externalities become apparent. To advance the
debate on agglomeration and networks, we identify ﬁve urgent research issues related to the
conceptualization of network externalities, the trade-off between agglomeration and urban
network externalities and the heterogeneous impact of urban network externalities:

1. multiplexity and heterogeneity in networks and their impacts;
2. interrelations between agglomerations and networks;
3. a dynamic perspective on agglomeration and networks;
4. a place-based perspective on agglomeration and networks; and
5. policy implications of a focus on networks.


3.1 Multiplexity and heterogeneity in networks and their impacts

Camagni et al (2016) show that small cities manage to proﬁt relatively more from a developed
network of co-operative relationships with other cities than do large cities. Meijers et al. (2016),
using a rather different measure of networks, arrive at the opposite, ﬁnding that smaller cities are
less able to proﬁt from their networks. Likewise, larger cities seem to be more dependent on
international networks, whereas smaller cities perhaps beneﬁt more from regional networks
(Burger et al. 2015; Glaeser et al. 2016; Meijers et al. 2016). This issue calls for future research
to address multiplexity and heterogeneity: The effects of different types of networks
(multiplexity) may be different for different types (sizes) of cities (heterogeneity). Glaeser
et al. (2016) identify another type of heterogeneity that requires exploration, suggesting that
the balance between the importance of networks and agglomeration varies from region to
region, which depends, for instance, on the urban structure. In addition, Thissen et al. (2016)
show that connectivity is important for some sectors, but certainly not all. In sum, different types
of networks will have different impacts on cities and ﬁrms or may substitute for some agglomeration
externalities but certainly not all. Further research is required to disentangle different types of
networks and their impact, which may vary according to the city and ﬁrm.

3.2 Interrelations between agglomerations and networks

In addition, the more general issue is how agglomeration and networks are interrelated and
how they can be meaningfully disentangled despite their interrelatedness, both conceptually
and in empirical frameworks. The urgency and relevance of this question is highlighted in

Van Meeteren et al. (2016), who draw attention to the causalities between the development
of agglomerations and networks between them and the change over time of this relationship.
However, this relationship is also crucial in supply chain internationalization, as regional
development depends largely on the ability to couple speciﬁc local production assets with
the global production networking strategies of ﬁrms, as Fujita and Hamaguchi (2016) mention.
In addition, investment in particular agglomeration-related regional assets can only pay off
when this leads to an enhanced market position in a distant geographical market as a result
of competitive trade relations (Thissen et al. 2016). Duranton (2016) addresses a potential
estimation bias when he suggests that urban growth and other urban outcomes may cause
rather than follow network embeddedness, while these outcomes are also determined by many
factors related to agglomeration. Camagni et al (2016) suggest that while size is often used as
an explanatory variable of urban efﬁciency because it captures many factors correlated with
size, this may not be correct as some of these factors, in particular the capacity to establish
and maintain networks with close-by and distant cities, are increasingly less correlated with size.
Thus, we need a better understanding of the relationship between agglomeration and networks.

3.3 A dynamic perspective on agglomeration and networks

Glaeser et al (2016) also rightly call attention to the time dimension, suggesting that urban net-
works may perhaps be more efﬁcient at present in Europe, but this may in the long run lead to
less growth due to a lower level of new ﬁrm creation. The long-run beneﬁts of agglomeration
and networks may be quite different from the short-run beneﬁts. That such a dynamic perspec-
tive is enlightening is further demonstrated by Camagni et al. 2016, and demands attention.

3.4 A place-based perspective on agglomeration and networks

The study by Duranton (2016) warns us that the typical determinants of urban growth in the de-
veloped world are not necessarily the same in the developing world, even in a quite advanced
developing nation as Colombia. This also holds for the determinants for network embeddedness
that take central stage in this special issue. The importance of networks found in Meijers et al.
(2016) and Thissen et al. (2016) for Europe is not demonstrated in the Colombian context.
Similarly, Glaeser et al. (2016) state that whether a focus on developing urban networks or
megacities is preferable is place-dependent. The mismatch between agglomeration theory and
urban dynamics speciﬁcally in Europe (except Eastern Europe) is documented in Camagni et al.
(2016) and Meijers et al (2016). More generally, this calls for prudence in generalizing research
ﬁndings and incorporating place-speciﬁc contextual determinants in the analysis. The importance
of local factors in mediating the role of urban networks needs to be further explored.

3.5 Policy implications of a focus on networks

In the future, attention should also be devoted to the policy implications of a rise in urban net-
work externalities. Some of the ﬁndings presented in this special issue suggest a quite drastic
reorientation of policies. Thissen et al. (2016) indicate that both location and network-speciﬁc
policies are needed, while the current focus is predominantly on place-based development
(e.g., ‘smart specialization’). Such network policies can focus on enhancing the position of
a region or place in a wide variety of networks, ranging from networks fostering broad connectivity
to dedicated networks of knowledge exchange, etc. Given the potential substitution effects of net-
works, it would seem that huge investments in large (capital) regions to stimulate further urbaniza-
tion of large cities and hence agglomeration beneﬁts are not necessarily more beneﬁcial in fostering
productivity than stimulating network development of these and other (also smaller) cities. Such

issues are addressed in a theoretical mode by Glaeser et al. (2016), but they emphasize that
empirical estimations of their theoretical model are needed before ﬁnal conclusions can be drawn
on the trade-off between urban networks and megacities. Doing so involves solving a number of
methodological issues related to the connection between urban size and amenities, in particular
the endogeneity of size, and requires a better understanding of housing supply elasticities and
evidence on returns to scale in idea creation. Stimulating integration between nearby places to
strengthen regional urban systems seems a no-regret option and is particularly beneﬁcial for
medium-sized or ‘second’ cities (Cardoso and Meijers 2016). However, as Fujita and Hamaguchi
(2016) show, there are also negative network externalities, and policies should also try to reduce
them. However, the extent to which negative network externalities outweigh the positive ones
should also be addressed in future research.

Resumen. Este artículo presenta el tema de la edición especial ‘Aglomeraciones y la aparición
de las externalidades de red urbanas’. Las externalidades de red urbanas se definen como las
economías externas de las que se pueden beneficiar las empresas y los hogares situados en
aglomeraciones que están bien arraigadas en redes que conectan con otras aglomeraciones.
Las contribuciones se centran en la conceptualización de las externalidades de red urbana y su
inf luencia en el desempeño urbano. Por último, se presenta un programa de investigación, que
debería centrarse en: la multiplicidad y heterogeneidad de las redes y sus impactos; las interrela-
ciones entre las aglomeraciones y las redes, y su naturaleza dinámica y basada en la localización;
y las implicaciones políticas de las externalidades de red urbanas.
