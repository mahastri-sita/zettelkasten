                                                                ABSTRACT Even a decade after the concept of polycentric development became popular and
                                                                increasingly widespread in Europe as a normative policy stance allegedly leading to cohesion
                                                                and competitiveness, its empirical basis is still rather weak. This is partly due to a lack of
                                                                conceptual clearness, which makes its measurement difficult. This research briefing synthesises
                                                                the results of two recent ESPON projects that aim to create a quantitative measurement of the
                                                                extent of polycentricity of national urban systems, as well as the links they find between
                                                                polycentricity and economic and social objectives. Both approaches have their limits and have
                                                                been, and can be criticized from various perspectives. Some suggestions on how to proceed with
                                                                this research agenda are presented.


                                                                Introduction
                                                                The concept of polycentric development has gained widespread currency in planning and
                                                                territorial development strategies, though it remains a rather fuzzy concept as it seems to
                                                                mean different things to different actors and on different scales (Kloosterman & Musterd,
                                                                2001; Davoudi, 2003; Hague & Kirk, 2003). The scale on which the concept is applied
                                                                ranges from individual cities (e.g. Roberts et al., 1999) to city regions (e.g. Hall &
                                                                Pain, 2006; Parr, 2004; Meijers, 2007a), to nation-states (Waterhout et al., 2005) and,
                                                                finally, to the European scale (see CEC, 1999). Davoudi (2003) observes that as the
                                                                scale increases, the concept of polycentricity becomes gradually less analytical, and
                                                                instead becomes the expression of a normative agenda. Indeed, polycentricity is not just
                                                                a descriptive term, but also a policy stance prescribing a means to promote and equalize
                                                                economic growth (Hague & Kirk, 2003). Its main promise is that it would link the
                                                                seemingly conflicting goals of greater cohesion and competitiveness (Waterhout et al.,
                                                                2005). The theoretical basis for this, however, is weak, as for instance regional growth

                                                                   The European Spatial Development Perspective (ESDP) presents as a major objective
                                                                the development of a balanced and polycentric urban system, as the economic fabric of
                                                                Europe is dominated by one large metropolitan system mainly located in the northwest
                                                                corner of the continent: the pentagon (CEC, 1999; Krätke, 2001; Copus, 2001). It is
                                                                argued that “the economic potential of all regions of the EU can only be utilized
                                                                through the further development of a more polycentric European settlement structure”
                                                                (CEC, 1999, p. 20). The objective of polycentric development is currently being taken
                                                                further in the current debate about a European territorial cohesion policy (Faludi, 2005,
                                                                2006). The EU member states recently agreed on a “Territorial Agenda of the European
                                                                Union” (EU Ministers responsible for Spatial Development, 2007), which again underlines
                                                                the importance of strengthening polycentric development. The substance of the concept

                                                                has not changed much since the ESDP, as it is still about the development of multiple
                                                                core areas next to one existing core. However, it appears that polycentric development
                                                                is now relatively more concerned with cohesion rather than competitiveness objectives.
                                                                   Alongside the European scale, many European countries also individually pursue a
                                                                polycentric development policy, often addressing the dominance of their prime city to
                                                                diminish regional disparities (see Waterhout et al., 2005 for an overview). A few examples
                                                                include France, which has a long tradition of combating the dominance of Paris (see
                                                                Guigou, 2000; Baudelle & Peyrony, 2005), while Ireland tries to develop “gateway
                                                                cities” in lagging regions in order to overcome its overdependence on Dublin (DELG,
                                                                2002; Davoudi & Wishardt, 2005). This does not mean that in all countries where the poly-
                                                                centricity concept has been used it has become influential in the sense of directing the
                                                                application of concrete policy instruments. What can be seen, however, are remarkable
                                                                examples of application in spatial planning documents—spatial visions and perspec-
                                                                tives—although at the same time implementation is often a weak point.
                                                                   Within the ESPON (European Spatial Planning Observation Network) framework, there
                                                                have also been attempts recently to measure polycentricity on the scale of countries and
                                                                the EU, thus implementing a more analytical than normative approach to polycentric
                                                                development on these scales (ESPON 1.1.1, 2002– 2004; ESPON 1.4.3, 2006). This is
                                                                of great importance as it allows for an empirical assessment of the many claims that
                                                                have been made for the concept of polycentricity on these scales. The empirical assess-
                                                                ment of the polycentricity concept has become all the more urgent now that polycentricity
                                                                has won itself this prominent place in many national and European spatial development
                                                                strategies, which often involve substantial budgets (most notably the European structural
                                                                and cohesion funds). The possible effectiveness of such policies is also determined by the
                                                                accuracy of the assumptions on which they build. Therefore, it is both surprising and
                                                                alarming that a key concept such as polycentric development and its promises of cohesion
                                                                and competitiveness has so far hardly been assessed empirically on these higher spatial
                                                                scales.
                                                                   Part of the explanation for this empirical deficit is the lack of a common and shared
                                                                understanding of what polycentricity is and how it should be properly measured. One of
                                                                the major reasons why the concept of polycentricity is still rather vague is that it is
                                                                sometimes associated with both morphological aspects and functional relationships
                                                                between cities. As will be explained further on, it is highly advisable to reserve the term
                                                                for morphological issues and to distinguish it from functional networks between cities. So

                                                                far, the lack of a shared understanding stands in the way of an assessment of its relation
                                                                with aspects of cohesion and competitiveness. However, two research projects carried
                                                                out within the ESPON framework have tried to shed light on these matters. The first is
                                                                the ESPON 1.1.1 project on “Urban areas as nodes in a polycentric development”. The
                                                                second is the ESPON 1.4.3 project, “Study on Urban Functions” that aims to improve
                                                                the ESPON 1.1.1 findings. As is common for this early stage of exploration, it appears
                                                                that researchers have so far not reached consensus on the right approach to measure
                                                                polycentricity.
                                                                   In this paper, we first observe the outcomes of both projects. This refers both to their
                                                                analysis of the extent of polycentricity of European countries, as well as the relationships
                                                                they have explored in terms of economic competitiveness, cohesion and sustainability.
                                                                These outcomes are strikingly different, and the section ‘Explaining Differences’ provides
                                                                explanations for this. Finally, based on the pros and cons of both approaches to measuring

                                                                research agenda.

                                                                Measuring Polycentricity and its Promises
                                                                The ESPON research programme aspires to achieve a better understanding of spatial
                                                                trends, problems and opportunities on a European scale, to monitor them using newly
                                                                developed indicators and mapping techniques and to provide tools and instruments for a
                                                                better territorial governance at EU level. The ESPON 1.1.1 project was among the first
                                                                projects that started in 2002, and focused on “the role, specific situation and potentials
                                                                of urban areas as nodes in a polycentric development”. Within this comprehensive
                                                                project an analysis of the extent of polycentricity and monocentricity of countries was
                                                                made. The project was led by Nordregio, while by the end of the project (2004), 14
                                                                research organizations from across Europe were involved as main or consultative partners.
                                                                Carried out at a later stage (2006), the ESPON 1.4.3 project aimed to take forward the
                                                                analysis of project 1.1.1, in particular the delimitation of functional urban areas (FUAs)
                                                                and the analysis of polycentricity building on that delimitation. The project was led by
                                                                IGEAT/Free University of Brussels and the team consisted of two other partners and
                                                                one other expert. The final report was published in March 2007.
                                                                   Table 1 presents the results of ESPON 1.1.1’s and ESPON 1.4.3’s analysis of the
                                                                extent of mono- or polycentricity (both are considered extremes on the same scale) of
                                                                European countries. Higher scores indicate more polycentricity. There are remarkable
                                                                differences between their outcomes. In fact, they do not even statistically correlate.
                                                                For instance, according to ESPON 1.4.3, countries such as Germany, Spain, Italy,
                                                                the Netherlands, Lithuania, Poland, Romania, the Slovak Republic and most of the
                                                                Nordic countries are much more polycentric than according to ESPON 1.1.1, while
                                                                Cyprus, Denmark, Greece, Ireland, Latvia and Slovenia turn out to be much more
                                                                monocentric.

                                                                sustainability. Proxies for these were GDP/capita, the gini coefficient of the GDP

                                                                Explaining Differences
                                                                These differences raise questions about the value that should be attached to the outcomes.
                                                                How is it possible that two research projects that aim to measure a similar feature of urban
                                                                systems and its effects arrive at such different conclusions? Here, we provide two main
                                                                explanations. The first concerns the way polycentricity is defined. The second concerns
                                                                the different emphasis that is placed on the elements that define polycentricity.


                                                                The Definition of the Concept of Polycentricity
                                                                ESPON 1.1.1 departs from the observation that polycentricity is an ambiguous, multi-
                                                                scalar and ill-defined normative concept. ESPON 1.1.1 stresses that two structural
                                                                elements are of particular relevance to polycentricity: morphological elements laying




Downloaded by [Dalhousie University] at 04:36 04 October 2014
                                                                out the size and territorial distribution of urban areas in a given territory, and relational
                                                                elements, based on the networks of flows and co-operation between urban areas on differ-
                                                                ent scales. Both elements are strongly linked: relations between cities are crucial for poly-
                                                                centricity, as nodes without relations would not form a polycentric system (ESPON 1.1.1,
                                                                2004, p. 45). A national urban system is considered more polycentric when the FUAs
                                                                are more equally sized (see column “size index” in Table 1 for scores), distributed
                                                                more evenly across the nation’s territory (see column “location index”) and when the
                                                                accessibility of the FUAs is more equal (see column “connectivity index”). The ESPON
                                                                1.4.3 team does not depart from an explicit definition of polycentricity, other than empha-
                                                                sizing that according to them, polycentricity is a purely morphological issue. This implies
                                                                that both projects depart from a substantially different perspective. The ESPON 1.1.1
                                                                project includes the aspect of relationships or flows between the FUAs of the urban
                                                                system, next to morphological indicators such as the size and spacing of the FUAs. The
                                                                ESPON 1.4.3 focuses solely on the size distribution of FUAs. Not surprisingly, there is
                                                                a strong correlation between the size index of ESPON 1.1.1 and the outcomes of the
                                                                ESPON 1.4.3 project.

                                                                indicators on which both projects based their measurement of polycentricity. In brackets is
                                                                the weight given to each indicator in calculating the overall polycentricity score.

                                                                distribution. There is a difference in the number of FUAs that are included in both ana-
                                                                lyses. In ESPON 1.1.1 all FUAs with over 50,000 inhabitants, or even just 20,000 inhabi-
                                                                tants for some countries, have been included when calculating the rank-size distribution
                                                                and primacy for each country. ESPON 1.4.3 presents some more differentiation in

                                                                Towards an Improved Measurement of Polycentricity and its Promises
                                                                Both ESPON 1.1.1 and ESPON 1.4.3 can be credited and criticized for several reasons.
                                                                ESPON 1.1.1 has been ground-breaking in terms of exploring for the first time the issue
                                                                of polycentricity in quantitative terms. Moreover, it departs from a clear, theoretically
                                                                founded definition of polycentricity. However, it has been criticized for its outcomes.
                                                                It appears that most commentators feel that the outcomes for several countries do not
                                                                                                          Measuring Polycentricity and its Promises        1319

                                                                correspond to their tentative impression of the extent of mono- or polycentricity in these
                                                                cases (see Biot, 2005). Ireland for instance is a case in point. This country came out as one
                                                                of the most polycentric in ESPON 1.1.1, while many consider it the example par excel-
                                                                lence of a monocentric country. These concerns were partly the reason to start the
                                                                ESPON 1.4.3 project. Its outcomes appear more tentatively correct. On the other hand,
                                                                the project lacks a clear definition of polycentricity and is not very specific about the ratio-
                                                                nales for using their indicators and their weighting. They also ignore the aspect of the
                                                                territorial distribution of cities across a nation’s territory. The main questions in measuring
                                                                polycentricity are whether or not this is just a morphological issue and, if so, whether it is
                                                                only concerned with size or also with the territorial distribution.
                                                                   Below, some suggestions are presented which aim to arrive at an improved measure-
                                                                ment of polycentricity, and subsequently, the more accurate assessment of its promises
                                                                of cohesion and competition.

                                                                A Polycentric Urban System is not a Networked Urban System
                                                                Basically, polycentricity refers to the plurality of centres in a given area and is thus by
                                                                nature a morphological issue. In a synthesis of the defining conditions of a polycentric
                                                                urban region, Parr (2004) points to, among others, the separation of cities and the size dis-
                                                                tribution of cities. It appears that morphological characteristics such as the size and
                                                                spacing of cities are determining factors in establishing whether or not any given area
                                                                is polycentric or the opposite, monocentric. Following the ESDP terminology and those
                                                                of many countries’ policies, a country can be considered more polycentric when:

                                                                . there is less hierarchy in the size of its cities (FUAs), in other words, when the cities are
                                                                  more equally sized and no city dominates the others;
                                                                . the cities are more evenly distributed across a country’s territory.


                                                                In ESPON 1.1.1, the non-morphological dimension of connectivity in terms of potential
                                                                accessibility was also included. However, a polycentric urban system cannot be equated
                                                                with a networked urban system. Elsewhere, I have argued that this is also the case on
                                                                the regional scale, where a polycentric urban region is not necessarily an urban network
                                                                (Meijers, 2007b). The latter would be the case when features of what has been labelled
                                                                the network model of spatial organization (see Batten, 1995; Camagni & Salone, 1993;
                                                                Meijers, 2007c) are present. This includes for instance the features of complementarity
                                                                between FUAs, a relative disconnection between size and function of FUAs and also
                                                                the criss-cross orientation of flows. In fact, the extent to which a polycentric system func-
                                                                tions as a networked system is a crucial explanation of the performance of this polycentric
                                                                system. Thus, polycentricity is about morphological features, more particularly the distri-
                                                                bution of sizes of FUAs and their territorial distribution. This perspective implies that the
                                                                ESPON 1.1.1 approach was too broad and the ESPON 1.4.3 approach too narrow.

                                                                An important explanation for the reason that the ESPON 1.1.1 results, even when ignoring
                                                                the connectivity aspect, do not seem to correspond to general, tentative impressions of the
                                                                extent of polycentricity of countries, is that the results are based on too many FUAs.
                                                                In particular in the larger countries, the calculations for the “flatness” of the urban hierarchy

                                                                Final Remarks
                                                                Polycentric development has rapidly become the buzzword amongst spatial planners in
                                                                Europe, both in European policies and at the level of European countries. As is generally
                                                                the case with new concepts, after the initial policy enthusiasm, the concept of polycentric
                                                                development needs to be tested on its usefulness and validity if it is to be sustained. This
                                                                requires a clear definition of polycentricity, subsequently allowing for a proper measure-
                                                                ment of the extent of polycentricity. By reviewing and synthesizing approaches to these

                                                                the discussion on this important matter. It appears that there are at present no conclusive
                                                                answers regarding the question of whether polycentricity is instrumental to achieving
                                                                economic competitiveness, territorial cohesion and environmental sustainability. Such
                                                                empirical assessments would provide a welcome contribution to this debate.
