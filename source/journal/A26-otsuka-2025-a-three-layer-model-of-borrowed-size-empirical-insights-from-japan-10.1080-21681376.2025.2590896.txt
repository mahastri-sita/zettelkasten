1. Introduction
In recent years, the concentration of economic activity in the Greater Tokyo Area has shown signs of gra­
dual transformation. Notably, the widespread adoption of teleworking in the wake of the COVID-19 pan­
demic has prompted a reevaluation of the urban-centric lifestyle, opening new perspectives on the
residential viability of peripheral regions. However, the structural challenges of population decline and
industrial hollowing-out remain acute, calling into question the autonomy and resilience of regional econ­
omies. Under such conditions, the conventional explanatory framework based on external economies of
agglomeration is becoming increasingly inadequate. The critical issue today is how smaller regions can sus­
tain and develop economic activity by benefiting from scale economies without relying on physical
agglomeration.
    This study re-examines the concept of ‘borrowed size’ as a theoretical framework to address this ques­
tion. Borrowed size refers to the phenomenon wherein small-scale regions indirectly benefit from extern­
alities concentrated in large metropolitan areas through various forms of networked or relational proximity.
A typical example is a peripheral region that accesses cultural facilities, consumer markets and employment
opportunities in a nearby metropolis while maintaining a low-density and cost-effective living environment.
Such configurations deviate from the assumptions of the traditional agglomeration theory, which posits that
productivity and efficiency depend directly on population density and market size, and instead highlight the
importance of networked and relational structures.
    The notion of borrowed size also serves as a critical response to theoretical models in the new economic
geography literature, such as those developed by Fujita and Tabuchi (1997), who depicted increasing inter-

research findings from Japan indicate that, while economic concentration in major metropolitan areas con­

simultaneous occurrence of sustained agglomeration and declining inequality cannot be sufficiently
explained by the existing agglomeration or equilibrium dispersion models. In contrast, the borrowed size
perspective suggests that small-scale regions can enjoy metropolitan externalities through networks and
that this structure may function as a spatial counterbalance to metropolitan congestion and saturation.
Against this backdrop, the central research question of this study was as follows:
      How can smaller or peripheral urban regions access the benefits of scale economies without undergoing physical
      agglomeration, and what institutional and relational mechanisms enable such access?

This question lies at the intersection of urban theory, spatial economics and regional policy and challenges
conventional assumptions that equate functional capacity with size or density. By addressing this question,
this study aims to contribute to a theoretical reorientation that emphasises networked interdependence,
institutional embeddedness and nonmaterial linkages to understand regional development.
    The concept of borrowed size transcends the binary opposition between spatial concentration and dis­
persion in urban economics, and can be a mediator when theorising structures in which spatial dispersion
and functional integration co-occur. In contemporary regional economies, spatial proximity is not a prere­
quisite for access to externalities. Instead, non-physical elements, such as institutional connectivity and rela­
tional embeddedness, increasingly shape functional scales (Amin & Thrift, 2002; Boschma, 2005;
Rodríguez-Pose, 2013). The theoretical framework proposed in this study explicitly incorporates such
non-physical conditions of agglomeration and seeks to extend spatial economic models by integrating insti­
tutional and relational dimensions.
    When discussing borrowed size, it is essential to move beyond the passive reception of spatial spillovers
or economic externalities and focus on the institutional and relational foundations that actively mediate
such processes. This necessitates an enquiry into the institutional context – policies, regulations and sub­
sidies – and the forms of social capital–inter-firm collaboration, inter-regional networks and human mobi­
lity – that facilitate the realisation of borrowed size. This study redefines the concept as a three-layered
structure as follows:

(1) The benefits of scale economies as externalities.
(2) The mediating function of network connectivity.
(3) Institutional and relational foundations enabling sustained access to these externalities.

   This theoretical framework captures the non-linear and multilayered dynamics of regional growth.
   Specifically, this study conceptualises borrowed size as a multilevel phenomenon that operates simul­
taneously as a state, mechanism and process. First, as a state, borrowed size refers to the observable outcomes
of functional integration situations in which regions perform beyond what would be expected, given their
population or economic scale. Second, as a mechanism, it captures the causal linkages through which per­
ipheral or smaller regions access agglomeration benefits through inter-regional connectivity, institutional
arrangements and knowledge flows. Third, as a process, it represents the dynamic evolution of interactions
and adaptations between metropolitan and non-metropolitan regions. In the proposed three-layer frame­
work, these dimensions are hierarchically related; the state emerges as the outcome of mechanisms that
evolve through continuous processes of adjustment and institutional mediation. This definitional hierarchy
allows for a coherent understanding of borrowed size as both a measurable phenomenon and a theoretical
construct.
   To underpin this theoretical reconstruction with a descriptive grounding, this study focuses on Japan’s
economic structure at the prefecture level. The term ‘region’ refers to prefectural jurisdictions, which are
fundamental administrative and statistical units in Japan’s spatial policy and economic data systems.
This choice was guided by the availability of consistent long-term statistics and the fact that prefectures
often act as the primary units of governance for economic and infrastructure planning. However, the theor­
etical scope of borrowed size in this study extends beyond administrative boundaries to encompass

functional economic areas and inter-regional network systems, particularly in cases where the flows of
people, goods and information transcend prefectural lines. Accordingly, prefecture acts as a proxy for a
broad set of relational and functional geographies.
   This study presents an evidence-based analysis of the trends in real gross value-added (GVA) disparities,
spatial proximity and industrial configurations relative to the Greater Tokyo Area, Japan’s core region. This
study examines the structural conditions under which borrowed size manifests in practice. Rather than
seeking a causal inference, the empirical component supplements the theoretical model by illuminating
the structural diversity of regional growth that has been overlooked in prior theories.
   This study makes three main contributions to the literature. First, it reframes borrowed size as a theory
incorporating both institutional thickness and relational connectivity. Second, it grounds this framework in
the empirical context of the Japanese regional economy while offering the potential for generalisation to
other Asian settings. Third, it proposes an alternative to an agglomeration-centred regional policy by high­
lighting the possibility of distributed regional growth through network-oriented institutional design and
relational integration. In doing so, it seeks to reposition borrowed size as a central theoretical pillar in a
new regional growth model that embraces spatial integration and institutional diversity.
   The remainder of this paper is organised as follows. Section 2 reviews the existing literature on borrowed
size and identifies the theoretical gaps. Section 3 presents the proposed theoretical framework. Section 4
provides empirical evidence, using Japanese prefectural data to assess the applicability of the model. Section
5 explores the policy implications of the framework, and Section 6 concludes the paper and suggests the
scope for future research.


2. Rethinking borrowed size: literature review and theoretical gaps
Alonso (1973) introduced the concept of borrowed size in the context of zero urban population growth,
referring to the idea that small cities may indirectly benefit from the cultural and economic functions of
neighbouring large cities. In the 2010s, Meijers and Burger (2010) re-theorised the notion of embedding
it within urban systems theory, particularly with reference to polycentricity and the functional urban region.
They redefined borrowed size as a theoretical concept wherein the physical scale of a city does not necess­
arily align with its functional influence, and where the benefits of agglomeration may be accessed through
inter-city networks.
    The empirical applications of this framework include a study by Phelps et al. (2001), who quantitatively
examined how borrowed size affected the growth and locational dynamics of small firms in the UK. Simi­
larly, Meijers et al. (2016) conducted a multidimensional analysis of European cities to explore the links
between functional inter-urban connectivity and economic externalities conferred by borrowed size.
These studies demonstrate that borrowed size is not merely a theoretical hypothesis but a concept that
can be both observed and measured within actual urban and regional structures.
    This perspective resonates with the notion of the ‘functional urban region’, as advanced by Parr (2007),
who emphasises how urban areas may extend their socioeconomic influence through networked relation­
ships, transcending administrative boundaries and physical scale. In this context, borrowed size offers a
reconceptualisation of scale that moves beyond static indicators, such as population or GVA, towards a
functional reach mediated by inter-urban ties.
    Studies such as those by Burger et al. (2015) and Meijers and Burger (2017) further expanded the analyti­
cal scope of borrowed size by examining its manifestation through various socioeconomic dimensions,
including housing markets, transport networks and industrial structures. These studies validate the concep­
tual utility of borrowed size and redirect attention toward the latent functional roles of small- and medium-
sized cities, long overshadowed in conventional agglomeration theory.
    Nevertheless, a substantial portion of the literature continues to frame the mechanisms of borrowed size
primarily in terms of spatial proximity and physical distance. The institutional and relational foundations
through which borrowed size is mediated remain theoretically underexplored. Questions such as why some
regions successfully leverage borrowed size while others do not, and what conditions enable the emergence
and persistence of mediating structures, have yet to be systematically addressed.
    Furthermore, an understanding of the emergence, evolution and potential dissolution of borrowed size is
lacking. Spatial proximity alone does not guarantee access to externalities. However, regions geographically
978      A. OTSUKA


distant from urban cores may benefit from agglomeration effects if they are underpinned by robust relational
capital and institutional complementarity (Camagni et al., 2015). This suggests the need to reconceptualise the
mechanisms of spatial externalities through a lens that incorporates institutional and relational structures.
    Rodríguez-Pose (2013) highlighted the decisive role of institutional quality and spatial configuration in
shaping regional development outcomes, arguing that institutional disparities are the key determinants of
the persistence or convergence of territorial inequalities. Rodríguez-Pose and Storper (2006) stressed that
the efficacy of institutional change does not depend solely on formal rules but also on their coherence
with local social foundations. Similarly, Storper and Scott (2016) identified the fragmented treatment of
institutions, cultures and networks as a critical challenge for contemporary urban theory, reinforcing the
theoretical value of framing borrowed size within an institutional mediation structure.
    The function of borrowed size should be examined concerning its potential antithesis–negative extern­
alities, particularly agglomeration shadows. Krugman (1991) and Fujita et al. (1999), within the context of
new economic geography, modelled the centripetal forces of agglomeration that draw resources away from
peripheral regions, leading to economic hollowing-out. Tabuchi (1998) synthesised Alonso’s land-use the­
ory using Krugman’s agglomeration framework to quantitatively map the spatial distribution of such effects.
Brakman et al. (2004) empirically identified the long-term imprint of agglomeration shadows on post-war
urban growth using the natural experiment on wartime bombing in Germany.
    Borrowed size and agglomeration shadow are the ‘light and shadow’ of spatial externalities. In territories
lacking a sufficient network structure or institutional mediation, agglomeration benefits are unlikely to
diffuse, borrowed-size effects are marginalised, and agglomeration shadow becomes dominant. These struc­
tural bifurcations are essential for any reconsideration of spatial configuration and redesign of regional
policy.
    In Japan, where a clear urban hierarchy persists and decades of infrastructure development and insti­
tutional engineering have shaped territorial dynamics, it is reasonable to assume that borrowed size is gov­
erned less by physical distance and more by institutional proximity and relational connectedness. This view
is consistent with the theoretical frameworks of Amin and Thrift (2002) and Storper (1997); they empha­
sised the importance of cultural and institutional ties in shaping inter-urban relationships.
    Against this background, this study seeks to reconstruct a theoretical framework of borrowed size.
Specifically, it conceptualises its mechanisms through a threefold structure:

(1) The benefits of scale economies as externalities.
(2) Mediating function of network connectivity.
(3) institutional and relational foundations provide sustained access to these externalities.

    First, scale-related externalities, such as productivity gains, labour market thickening and knowledge
spillovers, have traditionally been attributed to population size and spatial concentration. However, this
study suggests that such effects may be transmitted across broader spatial extents via networks without
necessitating physical contiguity (Boschma, 2005; Camagni et al., 2015).
    Second, network-mediated connectivity encompasses spatial distance, institutional and social links that
facilitate the flow of information, labour and capital. Functional connectivity among regions depends on
physical infrastructure, such as transport or information and communications technology (ICT), and on
trust-based relationships and institutional agreements (Bathelt et al., 2004; Storper & Scott, 2016).
    Third, institutional and relational foundations refer to policy frameworks, inter-regional coordination
mechanisms and support schemes that structurally sustain network operations. Such institutional arrange­
ments transform borrowed size from a transitory effect into a constitutive element of regional resilience
(Aoki, 2001; Rodríguez-Pose, 2013).
    By redefining borrowed size as a three-layer model, this study offers a theoretical framework that explains
the coexistence of persistent agglomeration and narrowing spatial inequality – an empirical paradox that
conventional spatial economics has struggled to resolve.
    Although Camagni et al. (2015) and Boschma (2005) discussed the theoretical foundations of borrowed
size, the present framework advances this discussion in two important directions. First, unlike Camagni et
al.’s focus on agglomeration-related interdependencies within functional urban areas, this study builds a mul­
tilayered framework incorporating functional, symbolic and institutional dimensions across a wider territorial
                                                                           REGIONAL STUDIES, REGIONAL SCIENCE   979


scale. Second, while Boschma emphasises proximity and relatedness in knowledge flows, the present frame­
work integrates these concepts into explicit territorial governance mechanisms, particularly relevant in non-
metropolitan contexts, where institutional borrowing is more salient than economic clustering.


3. A three-layer model of borrowed size
This section presents a theoretical reconstruction of the borrowed size concept by clarifying its multilayered
structure. While previous studies have predominantly understood borrowed size in terms of functional
inter-urban linkages, often emphasising spatial proximity and the visible structure of networks, they
have paid less attention to the underlying mechanisms by which the economic benefits of scale are trans­
ferred to smaller regions. To elucidate how such benefits reach less populated areas, borrowed size must be
conceptualised as a three-layered structure comprising the source of externalities, transmission mechanisms
and the institutional foundations that support them.
   Figure 1 shows the conceptual framework of borrowed size as a three-layered structure integrating scale-
related externalities, network connectivity and institutional-relational foundations. This framework illus­
trates how small or peripheral regions can access and sustain the benefits of metropolitan externalities
through mediated and embedded mechanisms.
   These three layers do not function in isolation. Instead, they are mutually interdependent and co-evolve
within territorial configurations. This co-evolutionary process manifests borrowed size as an operative force
within real-world regional dynamics.
   Figure 2 presents the hierarchical logic of the borrowed size to further clarify the conceptual architecture
of the proposed framework. The state denotes the measurable outcomes of regional integration, where per­
ipheral or smaller regions perform beyond their expected size. The mechanism represents the causal and
institutional linkages enabling these outcomes, and the process captures the dynamic interactions through
which such mechanisms evolve. Figure 1 shows the empirical configuration of the three layers, and Figure 2
focuses on their theoretical interdependencies.
   In this hierarchy, processes denote the ongoing dynamics through which inter-regional interactions,
institutional adjustments and learning mechanisms evolve. These processes give rise to mechanisms – net­
work-mediated, relational and institutional linkages that enable the peripheral regions to access the benefits

3.1. Scale economies as externalities
The first element of borrowed size is the benefit of scale, that is, the external economies arising from
agglomeration. These include thick labour markets, diversified industrial clusters, knowledge and techno­
logical spillovers, and the capacity to accommodate heterogeneous consumer preferences. Metropolitan
regions are particularly adept at internalising such externalities, functioning as engines of economic
efficiency and innovation.
    Traditionally, these externalities have been understood to be an outcome of spatial concentration. How­
ever, in today’s context, geographical distance no longer represents a principal constraint. Thus, the benefits
of scale are not confined to physically agglomerated areas. Instead, they can be functionally reconstituted
through extensive networks and institutional linkages. This perspective necessitates a redefinition of the
concept of scale, repositioning borrowed size from a notion grounded in a substantive scale to one based
on a relational scale (Camagni et al., 2015).
    Here, the ‘substantive scale’ refers to the economic scale determined by physical attributes such as popu­
lation size and spatial extent. In contrast, the term ‘relational scale’ denotes a functional economic space
characterised by nonmaterial forms of connectivity, including inter-urban networks and institutional
ties. Mediating network structures are essential for reconfiguring economies of scale in a manner that
does not rely on physical proximity.


3.2. Mediating structures of network connectivity
The second element of borrowed size concerns the mediating structures through which externalities are
transmitted from metropolitan centres to peripheral regions, namely, the connectivity of inter-regional
                                                                        REGIONAL STUDIES, REGIONAL SCIENCE     981


networks. It involves spatial proximity and structural linkages that facilitate the movement of people, goods
and information. Transport infrastructure, ICTs, supply chains and inter-institutional collaborations, par­
ticularly among universities and research institutes, constitute essential conditions for the realisation of bor­
rowed size (Phelps et al., 2001).
    The extent to which such networks are institutionally embedded and underpinned by relational trust. For
instance, in interdependent industrial clusters, where labour mobility and interfirm transactions are inten­
sive, informal conventions and mutual trust, rather than formal contracts, are conduits for the transmission
of externalities.
    Bathelt et al. (2004) distinguished between ‘local buzz’ – the informal circulation of knowledge within
regions – and ‘global pipelines’ – structured connections to actors beyond the region. These dual mechan­
isms operate complementarily to generate innovation and foster growth. Similarly, the mediating structures
of borrowed size rely on this dual architecture, in which the density of local networks and the quality of
external linkages together constitute the critical determinants of regional development.
    This second layer, centred on physical and functional connectivity, is a critical channel through which
peripheral regions can tap into the core’s market size and concentration-driven externalities that constitute
the first layer. However, network connectivity should be understood not only in its technical or physical
sense, but also as a socially constructed and institutionally negotiated framework through which borrowed
size can materialise. Although these mediating structures can transmit agglomeration benefits from the core
to the periphery, they can also serve as conduits for the reverse flow of economic gains back into the core.
Without mechanisms for financial and resource circulation back to the periphery, these benefits may leak,
limiting local multiplier effects, a phenomenon often referred to as the agglomeration shadow or ‘leaky
bucket’ problem in regional development (New Economics Foundation, 2002). Therefore, the second
layer alone is insufficient to ensure sustainable regional growth. Third, an institutional layer, encompassing
fiscal transfers, revenue-sharing schemes and other systemic arrangements, is necessary to retain and
reinvest these flows in peripheral economies. Overcoming such negative externalities requires institutional
complementarity as a prescription, marking a conceptual shift beyond infrastructural provisions toward the
qualitative dimensions of network architecture as a core principle in contemporary spatial economic theory.
    Accordingly, network connectivity must be understood not only in terms of its technical or physical
properties, but also as a socially constructed and institutionally negotiated framework through which bor­
rowed size can materialise. This perspective calls for a conceptual shift beyond infrastructural provisions,
emphasising the qualitative dimensions of network architecture as a central tenet of contemporary spatial
economic theory.


3.3. Institutional and relational foundations
The third element of borrowed size is the institutional and relational underpinnings that ensure the stability
and functionality of inter-regional networks. Borrowed size must not be conceived merely as a temporary
appropriation of externalities but rather as a sustainable and reproducible regional configuration. Non-mar­
ket factors such as institutional frameworks (comprising policy agreements, fiscal transfers and regulatory
flexibility) and social embeddedness (including social capital, cultural affinity and normative trust) play a
decisive role (Amin & Thrift, 2002).
   A rigorous theoretical understanding of these institutional and relational conditions draws on insti­
tutional economics literature. First, as North (1990) emphasised, institutions are path-dependent constructs
formed through the accumulation of historical decisions, and institutional change occurs within historically
contingent frameworks. Second, Rodrik (2007) argued for complementarity between institutions and pol­
icies, suggesting that institutional arrangements conducive to economic development must exhibit insti­
tutional coherence; that is, they must align with the socioeconomic context of a given region or nation.
   Finally, Rodríguez-Pose (2013) argued that it is not the abstract quality or content of institutions per se that
determines regional development outcomes, but rather their institutional fit – how well they are embedded
and functionally integrated within the specific context of the local society. This insight cautions against the
wholesale transplantation of institutional models and calls for a context-sensitive institutional design.
   Aoki (2001) conceptualised institutions as systems of mutually complementary components. The coher­
ence of expectations and the sharing of rules across these components guide institutional stability and
982      A. OTSUKA


evolutionary change. His notion of institutional complementarities offers a robust conceptual foundation for
understanding the ‘interoperability’ among the institutional arrangements necessary to sustain borrowed
size.
    Together, these perspectives affirm that borrowed size is not merely a by-product of spatial networks but
a phenomenon actively mediated and sustained through institutional configurations. The durability of net­
works and their capacity to convey externalities fundamentally depend on the design and complementarity
of the institutional infrastructure upon which they rest. Without such institutional arrangements, the
benefits transmitted through the first (scale economies) and second (network connectivity) layers are
prone to leak back to the core, diminishing their impact on peripheral economies. This dynamic is captured
by the leaky bucket analogy of regional development, wherein the income generated through integration is
rapidly lost through outflows, undermining the local multiplier effects. This phenomenon is also linked to
the concept of an agglomeration shadow, such as the absorption of regional resources into core regions.
    In the Japanese context, the Local Allocation Tax historically sought to correct such disparities through
fiscal transfers. Local allocation tax is a transfer of funds from the central government to local governments.
This is the revenue that local governments can use freely, and it plays an important role in local government
finances. In addition to the local allocation tax, the Furusato Nozei programme (hometown tax donation),
introduced in 2008, provides a more voluntary, demand-driven channel for resource circulation, enabling
urban residents to redirect part of their tax payments to rural municipalities (Ministry of Internal Affairs
and Communications, n.d.). These schemes exemplify the institutional devices that can counterbalance
the outflows caused by agglomeration shadows and reinforce the sustainability of borrowed-size effects.
    In this regard, institutions should not be treated as passive background conditions but as enabling mech­
anisms – structural devices that render the circulation of externalities feasible and effective. Scott and Stor­
per (2003) advanced this view by underscoring regional development’s critical interplay among institutions,
cultures and economic systems. In the face of intensifying global competition, they contended that insti­
tutional embeddedness is a key determinant of regional resilience. This reinforces the proposition that
the institutional-relational foundations of borrowed size are indispensable for both its emergence and
long-term viability.
    Peripheral actors typically pursue normative objectives such as balanced development, service accessibil­
ity, demographic retention and resilience. Realising these aims depends on concrete institutional capacities
– fiscal space and transfer design, administrative coordination capability and network-brokerage compe­
tences – through which cooperative arrangements can be sustained and conflicts (e.g., inter-jurisdictional
tax competition) are managed over time.


3.4. Conceptual model: multi-scalar borrowed size mechanism
By integrating the three constituent elements outlined above, the borrowed size mechanism can be concep­
tualised as a multilayered structure encompassing the following components:

(A) Source (scale economies): Concentration of economic and intellectual externalities in metropolitan
    cores.
(B) Mediation structure (network): The transmission of these externalities to surrounding regions through
    physical, institutional and relational connectivity.
(C) Reception base (institutions and relations): Institutional, cultural and trust-based foundations are
    necessary for the stable absorption and utilisation of these externalities.

    The integration of these three layers is grounded in the theory of institutional complementarity (Aoki,
2001; North, 1990), which posits that the productivity of one component is enhanced when combined with
other mutually reinforcing components. In the context of regional growth, physical agglomeration econom­
ies (Layer 1) and network connectivity (Layer 2) can generate short-term gains. However, without govern­
ance and relational capacity (Layer 3), such gains are prone to dissipation due to failures in coordination,
fiscal leakage and inter-jurisdictional competition. This complementarity implies that the absence of any
layer reduces the marginal returns of the others, thereby establishing a theoretical rationale for treating
them as an inseparable framework.
                                                                                           REGIONAL STUDIES, REGIONAL SCIENCE                983


   These three layers do not function in isolation; rather, they operate in an interdependent and co-evol­
utionary manner such that the borrowed size emerges only when all the components align and reinforce
one another. In the absence of institutional support for the mediating structure, scale benefits may fail to
reach peripheral regions. Worse still, as Brakman et al. (2004) demonstrated, such asymmetries may lead
to the dominance of negative externalities, manifested as an agglomeration shadow. In such cases, the
same inter-regional interdependencies that could generate positive borrowed-size effects instead manifest
as agglomeration shadows: negative net externalities flowing toward the core at the expense of the periphery
(Otsuka, 2024b, 2025). Borrowed size and agglomeration shadow are therefore not independent phenomena
but represent two possible outcomes of a single mechanism, differentiated by the balance of inflows and
outflows of externalities. Strong institutional and relational capacities, such as equitable fiscal transfer
schemes, reinvestment of locally generated revenues and governance arrangements fostering mutual
trust, shift this balance toward sustained positive effects. Conversely, weak or asymmetric institutional
frameworks increase the likelihood of dominating the agglomeration shadow effects.
   Ultimately, borrowed size should be redefined not merely as a concept within urban network theory, but
as an integrative theoretical device that links institutions with spatial configurations. Assessing how spatial
and institutional linkages within the Greater Tokyo Area, along with industrial complementarities, shape
the dynamics of regional growth will offer important insights into the empirical validity and relevance of
the model to policy.


4. Regional structures in Japan: empirical patterns and descriptive evidence
This section presents a descriptive analysis of the inter-regional economic structures in Japan to generate
empirical insights into the three-layer framework of borrowed size. Particular attention is given to trends
in economic disparity at the prefectural level, as measured by the GVA, along with spatial distance from the
Greater Tokyo Area and patterns of industrial specialisation. These observations aim to shed light on the
seemingly paradoxical coexistence of sustained agglomeration and the narrowing of regional disparities,
an outcome that may be interpreted through the lens of the borrowed size mechanism. It is important to
note that the purpose of this descriptive enquiry is not to establish causal relationships per se but rather
to complement the preceding theoretical discussion by identifying the structural characteristics and quali­
tative trends that signal the applicability and relevance of the conceptual framework.

apparent paradox, it is necessary to examine how the first layer (scale economies) interacts with the second
layer (mediating networks) and the third layer (institutional and relational foundations) of the borrowed
size mechanism.



4.2. Spatial proximity and asymmetric connectivity
Japan’s high-speed transport infrastructure has developed into a radial network centred on the Greater
Tokyo Area. Transport systems, including highways, Shinkansen (bullet trains) and air routes, have been
systematically developed, with Tokyo as the national hub. Since the 1990s, this network has expanded
rapidly, substantially reducing the travel times between Tokyo and many regional cities. These reductions
in temporal distance represent not only physical improvements, but also a functional expansion of the
metropolitan economic sphere, thereby enhancing the potential for borrowed-size effects in peripheral
regions.

4.3. Industrial specialisation and relational complementarities
An examination of the industrial composition of Japan’s regions reveals that many peripheral areas experi­
encing narrowing economic disparities have undergone industrial specialisation, particularly in manufac­
turing. This specialisation acts as an ‘absorptive interface’ through which external scale economies can
be internalised, aligning with the reception base described in the conceptual model.
   Figure 7 illustrates the strong positive correlation between the degree of specialisation in manufacturing
and the rate of labour productivity growth. For instance, prefectures such as Shiga, Tokushima, Gunma and
Ibaraki exhibit high levels of manufacturing agglomeration and above-average productivity growth despite
not being part of the major metropolitan areas. This pattern suggests that peripheral regions can achieve
productivity catch-up when sectoral advantages allow them to effectively channel and amplify metropolitan
spillovers.
   Such trends can be attributed to the technological characteristics and market structures of individual sec­
tors. Manufacturing, being capital-intensive and well-aligned with ICT investment and process innovation,
tends to facilitate productivity enhancement even in regions with declining populations. In contrast, service

   Importantly, this pattern of regional manufacturing specialisation complements the industrial structures

   Camagni et al.’s (2015) analysis of the conditions under which second-ranked cities can benefit from
agglomeration economies is particularly instructive. They demonstrated that such cities could harness
the advantages of scale by maintaining functional connectivity in large urban regions and institutional foun­
dations. This finding supports the theoretical framework of borrowed size, particularly the dual emphasis on
relational complementarity and institutional mediation.
   Capello (2009) argued that traditional spatial economic theories tend to underestimate the role of insti­
tutional frameworks and network connectivity in explaining regional growth. From this perspective, the
three-layer structure of the borrowed size model supplements the existing growth models with institutional
depth.
   Crucially, these relationships are not confined to market transactions but also rely on institutional and
organisational linkages. Collaborative arrangements between academia, industry and the government in
research and development (R&D), inter-regional economic agreements and sustained support from
regional financial institutions all serve as the foundational components of institutional mediation (Bristow
& Healy, 2015). These mechanisms stabilise production networks and provide governance structures that
secure the continuous circulation of externalities, thereby operationalising the third layer of the borrowed
size mechanism.
   In Japan, the institutional and relational foundations for inter-regional collaboration are embedded in
the National Spatial Strategy (NSS), which is a statutory nationwide plan formulated under the National
Land Planning Act. As a long-term comprehensive spatial plan for the use, development and conservation
of land at the national level, the NSS provides a framework that specifies spatial policies, infrastructure

priorities and governance arrangements across prefectures and metropolitan areas, thereby functioning as a
formalised platform for realising relational externalities beyond individual administrative boundaries.
    Since the first comprehensive national development plan of 1962, national spatial planning in Japan has
been periodically revised to address emerging challenges, evolving from infrastructure-led growth to net­
work-oriented, resilience-focused strategies. The current NSS (Ministry of Land, Infrastructure, Transport
and Tourism, n.d.), with a ten-year planning horizon, defines its core concept as the creation of a ‘country
that promotes interaction’, where ‘interaction’ refers to the inter-regional movement of people, goods, capi­
tal and information arising from cooperative relationships. These interactions, mediated by inter-regional
networks, are considered essential for sustainable regional development. In the context of population
decline, the NSS emphasises maintaining the regional market size through networking, which requires pre­
serving the individuality of each region as an economic entity. By refining their distinct local resources,
regions can generate mutually beneficial exchanges that enhance national productivity and efficiency. A
notable example is the planned Chuo Shinkansen, a 438 km Maglev line linking Tokyo, Nagoya and
Osaka. Once operational (Tokyo–Nagoya in 2037, Nagoya–Osaka in 2045), travel times between these
major metropolitan areas will be reduced to approximately one hour, allowing face-to-face collaboration,
joint research and innovation across an integrated ‘super-mega-region’. This illustrates how institutional
frameworks (Layer 3) and physical connectivity (Layer 2) can act in concert to strengthen the overall spatial
economy.
    In addition to the NSS, Japan’s institutional foundations for inter-regional collaboration are reinforced by
two long-standing statutory mechanisms that directly shape the absorptive capacity of peripheral regions.
    First, intergovernmental fiscal transfer systems such as the Local Allocation Tax function as formalised
redistribution mechanisms that equalise fiscal capacity across municipalities. Figure 8 shows the change in
the CV of the per capita GVA before and after fiscal transfers. The figure clearly shows a downward shift
after fiscal transfers, indicating a narrowing of regional disparities. By securing a stable revenue base for less-
affluent jurisdictions, these transfers enable the continued provision of public services and strategic invest­
ments in local infrastructure. This institutional support sustains the economic viability of peripheral areas
and enhances their ability to participate in inter-regional production networks, thereby increasing the like­
lihood that external-scale economies from metropolitan areas will be effectively absorbed and retained.
    Second, the NSS framework facilitates large-scale social capital investments, including transport, com­
munication and logistics infrastructure. As documented in the post-war NSS, infrastructure development
under these plans has improved market accessibility, reduced transaction costs and supported the formation
of regional industrial hubs (Otsuka, 2021, pp. 30–33). Such investments illustrate how Layer 3 (institutional

4.4. Linking empirical patterns to theoretical insights
The preceding descriptive analyses do not merely catalogue empirical facts but serve to substantiate the
theoretical coherence of the three-layer borrowed size framework. Specifically:

.   The gradual convergence of GVA across regions suggests that, although the source of external economies
    remains concentrated in core areas such as Tokyo, a structural mechanism for their redistribution via
    transport networks and industrial linkages is empirically observable.
.   The reduction in the time distance and improvements in functional connectivity underscore the central
    role of network connectivity as a mediating structure in shaping the growth potential of peripheral regions.
.   The presence of complementary industrial linkages and institutional support mechanisms demonstrates
    that institutional and relational foundations transform the concept of borrowed size from a passive receipt
    of externalities to a structurally sustainable process, especially when such institutional arrangements are
    combined with physical and relational connectivity to form a mutually reinforcing growth system.

   Together, these empirical patterns reinforce the explanatory power and policy relevance of the borrowed
size framework for capturing the complexities of regional development in contemporary Japan. They high­
light that scale-related benefits are not simply ‘spilled over’ but are actively filtered, shaped and amplified
through spatial networks and institutional capacities. They also provide a conceptual bridge for rethinking
growth theory in spatial economics, particularly in terms of how scale-related benefits are distributed across
space through non-linear multi-scalar mechanisms.
   In the next section, we build on this integration of theory and empirical observations to explore the
implications of borrowed size for regional policy design and theoretical advancement.


5. Theoretical and policy implications
5.1. Rethinking spatial scale: from physical to relational constructs
The three-layer model of borrowed size offers both theoretical and practical implications for spatial econ­
omics. Its core contribution lies in reconceptualising the spatial scale, traditionally understood in purely
physical terms, as a relational construct shaped by institutional and network-mediated channels.
   Classical models in new economic geography (Krugman, 1991; Fujita et al., 1999) explain growth
through the self-reinforcing effects of agglomeration: dense populations and large markets generate centri­
petal forces, often increasing interurban disparities. This powerful logic assumes that external economies
diffuse primarily through geographical proximity.
   However, contemporary Japan shows that sustained agglomeration can coexist with narrowing regional
disparities. This pattern suggests that scale economies are redistributed through institutional arrangements
and inter-regional networks, not solely through physical closeness. This corresponds to the first layer of the
model, scale-related externalities, illustrated by the long-term convergence in per-capita GVA in Japan
despite persistent metropolitan concentration, as discussed in Section 4.1. Therefore, scale should be con­
ceptualised as relational, with its benefits depending on the presence of mediating structures that link the
core and peripheral regions.

   Incorporating institutional mediation into the spatial economic theory expands its explanatory scope,
allowing for a more integrated understanding of how agglomeration effects diffuse under specific structural
conditions. This reconceptualisation moves beyond the geography of distance toward the geography of
institutional connectivity.


5.2. Integrating institutions into spatial economic theory
Redefining spatial scale as relational also requires shifting the analytical focus from whether institutions
exist to how they function within specific regional contexts. Rodríguez-Pose (2013) emphasised that insti­
tutional fit – the alignment of institutions with local conditions – is more important than generic notions of
‘good institutions’. This directly resonates with the borrowed size framework, which assumes that the diffu­
sion of scale economies depends on institutions tailored to the territories they serve.
   Rodríguez-Pose and Storper (2006) contended that institutional effectiveness stems not only from formal
design but also from embeddedness in informal rules, such as social trust, relational networks and cultural
norms. This aligns with Aoki’s (2001) concept of institutional complementarities, where mutually reinfor­
cing institutions create coherent expectations and collective actions. In the borrowed-size framework, these
complementarities are critical for sustaining spillovers over time.
   Storper and Scott (2016) called for a theory that integrates institutions, networks and space. Borrowed
size can act as a nexus, linking physical and relational scales to institutional depth. This corresponds to
the third layer of the model – institutional and relational foundations – and in the Japanese context,
examples include the NSS and the fiscal transfer system noted in Section 4.3. By embedding institutional
analysis into spatial economic theory, borrowed size extends existing growth models (Capello & Nijkamp,
2009), providing a more comprehensive account of the structural mechanisms that shape regional develop­
ment in contemporary economies.


5.3. Conceptualising agglomeration shadow and borrowed size as dual outcomes
Borrowed size and agglomeration shadow can be understood as dual interrelated outcomes of inter-urban
networks. Agglomeration processes often generate centripetal forces that boost growth in urban cores but
suppress development in peripheral areas – what Fujita et al. (1999) and Krugman (1991) describe as the
‘shadow effect’.
   This study proposes that institutional and relational networks can counterbalance this shadow. When
such mediating structures are present, the benefits of scale, normally concentrated in the core, can be redis­
tributed to peripheral regions, thus enabling borrowed size. In this sense, borrowed size is not merely an
incidental by-product of agglomeration but an institutional counterweight to its negative spillovers. This
duality connects the second layer (network-mediated connectivity) and the third layer (institutional foun­
dations), both of which must operate together to offset shadow effects.
   Framing these phenomena as structurally intertwined, yet conditionally distinct, clarifies that spatial
inequalities are not inevitable outcomes of market forces. Instead, they depend on the presence or absence
of mediating institutional arrangements. This perspective implies that policy must intentionally design
scale through coordination, network planning and institutional bridging to transform disparities into
functional complementarities. By adopting this dual-outcome lens, spatial policy can move beyond reac­
tive redistribution toward proactive integration, supporting both spatial justice and the resilience of inter-
urban systems.


5.4. Four strategic pillars of policy design
The three-layer borrowed size model demonstrates that regions can capture scale benefits without relying
on physical agglomeration, thus offering a framework for reimagining regional development beyond core-
centred growth. Instead of concentrating resources on metropolitan hubs, policy can leverage institutional
reinforcement and relational connectivity to foster distributed and resilient growth. Each of the four stra­
tegic pillars is mapped to at least one layer of the model to ensure a direct link between theory and policy
design. Four strategic pillars emerge for policy design:
992      A. OTSUKA


5.4.1. Ensure institutional interoperability (Layer 3)
Harmonise regulations, improve interjurisdictional governance and strengthen administrative coordi­
nation. These measures reduce the friction between municipalities and provide a structural foundation
for functional integration.

5.4.2. Cultivate relational capital (Layers 2 and 3)
Promote human mobility, support inter-regional academic–industrial networks and institutionalise trust-
based partnerships. Relational capital deepens the institutional complementarity and enhances the adapta­
bility of networked regions.

5.4.3. Redesign functional scales (Layers 1 and 2)
Move beyond administrative boundaries toward policy frameworks aligned with actual economic and living
spheres. This recalibration allows institutional configurations to match territorial realities.

5.4.4. Enhance mediating structures (Layer 2)
Strengthen intermediary organisations, regional hubs and public–private partnerships that enable inno­
vation and coordinate cross-regional cooperation. These entities serve as operational enablers for borrowed
size.
   In operationalising these pillars, Boschma’s (2005) five proximities – geographical, organisational, insti­
tutional, social and cognitive – offer an analytical lens for assembling diverse forms of proximity into coher­
ent regional strategies.
   This approach contrasts with traditional agglomeration-focused policy, which entrenches dependency on
peripheral areas. By prioritising institutional, relational and functional spatial logic, a policy based on the
borrowed size framework can transform spatial disparities into complementarities, advancing both equity
and efficiency in regional systems. As shown in Sections 4.2 and 4.3, this approach is particularly relevant in
Japan, where high-speed transport connectivity and industrial specialisation provide a foundation for net­
worked growth. Although grounded in the Japanese context, the conceptual and policy insights derived
from the borrowed size framework hold broader applicability for analysing distributed growth and net­
work-mediated externalities in other national and regional settings, particularly in countries facing similar
demographic and spatial restructuring.


6. Conclusion and future research agenda
This study proposes a theoretical reconceptualisation of borrowed size as a three-layered construct encom­
passing scale-related externalities, mediating network structures and institutional-relational foundations.
This multi-scalar, non-linear framework offers an integrated interpretation of the seemingly paradoxical
coexistence of sustained agglomeration and declining inter-regional disparities observed in Japan, thereby
suggesting a new direction for regional policy paradigms.
   By shifting the analytical focus from agglomeration intensity to inter-municipal and institutional inter­
dependence, this study extends the borrowed size theory beyond the interpretations of Camagni et al. (2015)
and Boschma (2005). Descriptive evidence from Japan underscores how peripheral regions can benefit from
institutional coordination, shared governance platforms and relational capital even in the absence of phys­
ical proximity or functional dominance. These findings contribute to the growing recognition that regional
resilience and growth are increasingly contingent on nonmaterial forms of connectivity.
   The policy implications of this study should be interpreted in light of a process-oriented understanding of
borrowed size. Regional resilience is not a static condition but an outcome of continuous institutional
mediation and network adaptation. Therefore, effective regional policy should focus less on reproducing
metropolitan agglomeration externally and more on facilitating dynamic processes such as institutional
cooperation, governance coordination and knowledge circulation that enable regions to sustain functional
integration over time. Empirical illustrations from Japanese prefectures support this process-oriented
interpretation of borrowed size and demonstrate how institutional coordination enables functional inte­
gration beyond physical agglomeration.

   However, this study has three primary limitations. First, its high level of theoretical abstraction raises
questions regarding balancing conceptual clarity and empirical precision. Second, the operationalisation
and measurement of the institutional and relational components of borrowed size remain methodologically
underdeveloped, requiring further advancement in quantitative approaches. Third, the scope is confined to
prefecture-level cross-sectional indicators, which may not fully capture microscale interactions or the tem­
poral dynamics of institutional change. This study does not aim to provide causal inference or formal vali­
dation but rather to construct a conceptual foundation and illustrate its contextual relevance in the Japanese
setting.
   Future studies should address these limitations. First, dynamic modelling using evolutionary spatial net­
work simulations and longitudinal datasets is warranted to capture the formation, deepening and dissol­
ution of borrowed size. Second, a multi-scalar analysis spanning municipalities, metropolitan regions
and wider functional areas may provide insight into the scope and scale dependence of the mechanism.
Third, comparative institutional studies across diverse national and regional contexts would clarify the uni­
versality or context-specificity of borrowed size, particularly in light of the socially and historically contin­
gent nature of institutions. Such cross-national perspectives are especially relevant for countries undergoing
demographic transition, metropolitan concentration and spatial restructuring, where network-mediated
spillovers may function to counterbalance spatial polarisation. Fourth, new relational indicators such as
mobility trajectories, supply chain linkages and university–industry collaborations are needed to refine
the empirical analyses.
   This three-layered model contributes to the reconfiguration of contemporary urban and regional theory
by situating borrowed size as a mediating structure at the intersection of institutions, culture and space. As
Rodríguez-Pose and Storper (2006) contended, institutions are not merely formal rules but social constructs
embedded in norms and trust networks that shape regional development. This perspective reinforces the
theoretical relevance of conceptualising borrowed size as a regionally constructed and institutionally
mediated growth mechanism. Although this study focuses on Japan, the proposed three-layer framework
may be applicable to other countries with metropolitan concentration and emerging inter-regional linkages.
By acknowledging its methodological boundaries and outlining the pathways for empirical expansion, this
framework provides a robust platform for cumulative research across spatial and institutional contexts.
   In conclusion, the borrowed size offers a novel theoretical foundation for understanding growth mech­
anisms beyond physical agglomeration. Harnessing institutional and relational networks provides a new
perspective for designing strategies for regional resilience and inclusive development. Future research
should continue to refine this framework as both a theoretical cornerstone and practical model for spatial
policy grounded in institutional resilience.
