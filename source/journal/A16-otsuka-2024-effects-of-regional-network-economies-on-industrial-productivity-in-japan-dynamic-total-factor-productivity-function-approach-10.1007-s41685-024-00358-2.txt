Abstract
Improving productivity performance is essential for achieving regional economic
sustainability. With the advent of the network society, regional economic agents
have benefited not only from local within-region interactions but also from exter-
nal out-of-region economies. However, it remains unclear whether localized exter-
nal economies or spillovers of externalities through inter-regional networks are more
significant in improving productivity performance. Therefore, this study examined
the relationships between productivity performance and geographical externalities
from an industry perspective using regional economic data for Japan and employed
a dynamic total factor productivity function approach. It also assessed the impact
of high-quality transportation infrastructure improvements on productivity catch-
up. The results revealed the long-run effects of inter-regional networks on the pro-
ductivity performance of industries. Furthermore, it revealed that, as a geographi-
cal externality, the “borrowed size” effect contributed significantly to productivity
improvements in both manufacturing and non-manufacturing industries. These find-
ings suggest that enhancing both high-quality transportation infrastructure and
inter-regional transportation networks is likely to improve industry productivity
performance.

1 Introduction

This study explores the role of geographical externalities as determinants of the pro-
ductivity performance of regional economies and statistically identifies their effects.
    Advances in technology and networked societies have altered inter-regional
exchange mechanisms and significantly affected the productivity performance of
economic agents (Castells 1996). Economic agents in a region not only benefit
from local, within-region interactions but also from external economies outside
the region. Consequently, regional economies shift from being spatially inde-
pendent areas to network hubs and region–region interactions affect the produc-
tivity performance of regional economies (Cooke et al. 2011).
    Traditional arguments on economies of agglomeration have suggested that
the geographical agglomeration of economic agents affects the productivity per-
formance of the regional economy as external economies (Combes and Gobil-
lon 2015; Rosenthal and Strange 2004). Several empirical studies (e.g., Acs et al.
2002; Carlino and Kerr 2015; Varga 1999) have shown that innovative activities
responsible for productivity performance improvements tend to be spatially clus-
tered and localized. Knowledge spillover depends on various factors, including
tacit dimensions of knowledge, proximity of different types, and labor mobility
(Grillitsch and Nilsson 2015). Tacit knowledge has been considered a localized
factor, as knowledge transfer over long distances is difficult (Lam 2000).
    However, non-geographically bound forms of knowledge spillovers exist, such
as those based on cognitive, technological, and social proximities, through which
several studies have indicated the possibility of knowledge exchange at a distance.
For example, Asheim and Coenen (2006) and Trippl (2011) argue that acquiring
localized knowledge is insufficient for innovation activities and that combining
and complementing localized knowledge with knowledge acquired from distant
sources is crucial. That is, collaboration between distant locations is considered
an important factor for successful productivity performance improvement, and in
this sense, traditional geographic proximity is neither a necessary nor a sufficient
condition for productivity improvements (Boschma 2005).
    The “borrowed size” concept—a theory on geographical externalities (Burger
et al. 2015) proposed by William Alonso (1973)—theoretically explains the
impact of such distant collaboration on the productivity performance of regional
economies. According to this concept, regions adjacent to a central region can
benefit from the latter’s external economies by borrowing the economies of scale
generated therein (Meijers and Burger 2017). Inter-regional networks allow
regions to acquire complementary capabilities through collaboration and bene-
fit from economies of scale as external advantages from other regions (Capello
2000; Phelps et al. 2001). As a result, the economic growth of regions is influ-
enced not only by the traditional significance of geographical closeness but also
by their capacity to form connections with major urban centers (Meijers et al.
2016). Several studies (e.g., Camagni et al. 2015, 2016; Johansson and Quigley
2003; Melo et al. 2017; Otsuka 2018, 2020a, b, 2024a) have provided empirical
evidence on these effects from a regional economic system perspective.
Asia-Pacific Journal of Regional Science (2024) 8:1111–1134                          1113


    However, previous studies have not provided sufficient empirical evidence on
regional industrial activity. Regional industries can be classified into export-oriented
(core) and intra-regional (non-core) industries. In many cases, manufacturing indus-
tries represent export-oriented industries, which form an efficient production sys-
tem in a wide-area network to meet demand from outside their regions. For exam-
ple, the supply chains of advanced industries, such as the semiconductor sector, are
constructed outside their regions, and inter-regional divisions of labor in produc-
tion develop, likely improving inter-regional collaboration in industrial innovation
activities. Intra-regional industries, however, include non-manufacturing industries,
mainly service sectors. In these industries, the construction of production systems
is limited to the region to efficiently respond to demand within the region. In other
words, the production bases of these industries are localized; hence, the industries’
innovation activities are likely to be localized as well. To clarify the effects of inter-
regional networks that differ across industries, this study quantitatively evaluates
the factors of geographical externalities that affect productivity performance using
industry data.
    Using Japanese industry-specific data, this study significantly contributes to eval-
uating regional industrial policy in Japan. In the 2000s, the period under observa-
tion, regional policies were continuously implemented to promote industrial location
and strengthen inter-regional networks. In 2001, the Ministry of Economy, Trade
and Industry (METI) launched the “Industrial Cluster Plan,” followed by the Min-
istry of Education, Culture, Sports, Science and Technology (MEXT) initiating the
“Knowledge Cluster Creation Project” in 2002. These initiatives were designed to
enhance regional economic productivity by fostering connections among businesses,
academic institutions, and research organizations across traditional administrative
boundaries.
    Such policies may improve productivity by enabling new technological and prod-
uct developments through network formation, even in regions with scarce man-
agement resources. A research study conducted in France demonstrated that firms
within competitive industrial clusters exhibited comparatively higher total fac-
tor productivity (TFP) than those outside such clusters (Abdesslem and Chiappini
2019). In other words, assuming Japan’s regional policies have been as successful
as policymakers anticipated, the overall productivity of the area may have improved,
with less productive regions potentially catching up to their more economically
advanced counterparts. Therefore, to verify the validity of these policies, it is crucial
to examine the effects of inter-regional networks on productivity performance.
    This study empirically analyzes geographical externalities by industry based on
2000–2018 data from 47 prefectures. The analysis method utilizes dynamic panel
analysis based on dynamic TFP functions, allowing us to capture both short- and
long-term effects of inter-regional networks on productivity performance. That is,
it is possible to assess whether the formation of inter-regional networks has helped
low-productivity regions to catch up with high-productivity regions over different
timescales.
    The structure of this paper is as follows. The connection between productivity
performance and geographical externalities is explored in Sect. 2. Section 3 outlines
the analytical approach, including the definition of the TFP indicator and a detailed
1114                                      Asia-Pacific Journal of Regional Science (2024) 8:1111–1134


explanation of the analytical model and data. Section 4 discusses the findings from
the estimation and provides an interpretation of the analytical outcomes. The study
concludes in Sect. 5, summarizing the research and addressing unresolved issues.


2 Conceptualization

2.1 Productivity

Productivity is defined as the ratio of inputs to outputs. The higher the productivity,
the more output is obtained from a given input, or the less input is required to pro-
duce a given amount of output. Generally, input is a production factor, such as labor
or capital, and output is the value added.
    Labor productivity, which is commonly used as a measure of productivity, is
defined as the output per unit of labor. It is also simply referred to as factor produc-
tivity because it considers only labor as an input. This indicator is effective when the
composition ratio of input goods is almost the same among economic agents. How-
ever, in regional economies, the composition ratio of input goods differs depend-
ing on the industrial structure of each region. In this case, labor productivity can-
not accurately evaluate the productivity performance of the regional economy. For
example, the labor productivity of economic agents that use capital-intensive tech-
nology tends to be higher; therefore, regions specializing in capital-intensive indus-
tries tend to have higher labor productivity. This raises the concern that it is not
possible to distinguish whether this is because industries that use labor resources
efficiently are concentrated in the region or because industries with a high capi-
tal–labor ratio are concentrated in the region.
    In contrast, TFP is defined as the ratio of aggregate inputs to aggregate outputs
and encompasses both labor productivity and capital productivity. Therefore, it does
not face concerns that arise with factor productivity (Otsuka and Goto 2016).

   Indirect technological progress includes technological progress embodied in capi-
tal and labor. For example, when old capital stock is replaced with new capital stock
through capital investment, even if the total amount of capital stock remains the
same, the new capital stock can potentially produce more output than the old capi-
tal stock due to the addition of new functions and the increase in capital expertise.
Similarly, when a more skilled worker replaces one worker, more outputs can be
generated even if the number of workers remains the same. In addition, if economies
of scale exist, productivity performance will improve due to the benefits of mass
production. Productivity performance will also improve if managerial efficiency
increases—that is, if operational efficiency improves due to a review and accelera-
tion of the decision-making process. Furthermore, if industries are geographically
connected, geographical externalities will be strengthened due to agglomeration
economies or inter-regional network economies. Consequently, productivity perfor-
mance in the regional economy will improve.


2.2 Borrowed size

The concept of borrowed size integrates population, function, and critical mass
effects for both demand and supply, proposing that smaller urban areas can benefit
from nearby larger agglomerations without experiencing their drawbacks, such as
congestion. This theory suggests that the advantages of urban clustering are not con-
fined to a city’s physical limits but can extend to surrounding regions. This frame-
work explains why smaller cities might operate more efficiently than their larger
counterparts and why rural areas can maintain effective urban structures by “bor-
rowing” agglomeration economies from the broader urban network (Parr 2002).
   As urban agglomeration externalities broaden their reach, their influence extends
beyond traditional city limits. This expansion suggests that the notion of borrowed
size could potentially elucidate the growth of regional economies on a larger scale,
encompassing areas well outside urban centers. That is, the borrowed size concept
can overcome the limitations of the conventional geographical approach that only
cities adjacent to a metropolis can experience increased efficiency, and argues that
scale is not the only determinant of productivity and economies of agglomeration.
Incorporating long-distance cooperation agreements throughout the urban network,
along with the presence of advanced functions, could increase urban productivity
despite the limited size of cities (Boix and Trullén 2007; Camagni 1993; Capello
2000; Camagni and Capello 2004; Hall and Pain 2006; Huang et al. 2020).
1116                                    Asia-Pacific Journal of Regional Science (2024) 8:1111–1134


   Establishing efficient transportation systems enhances these borrowed-size effects
by reducing travel expenses for innovators and expanding opportunities for eco-
nomic interactions. A well-developed transportation infrastructure not only boosts
the efficiency of its direct users but also benefits related parties by accelerating and
increasing the frequency of information exchange. This means that the geographical
reach of externalities grows as knowledge spreads beyond regional boundaries. Con-
sequently, creating an advanced transportation network amplifies network externali-
ties by enabling innovators to move more freely between the regions (Ma and Liu
2022; Tian et al. 2022).


2.3 Agglomeration shadow

Nevertheless, some scholars contend that establishing regional networks may not
always benefit urban development (Hesse 2016). The rivalry among cities that
emerges from these networks can result in a “siphoning effect,” where larger urban
centers extract resources from their smaller neighbors, impeding the growth of these
surrounding cities. Consequently, this inter-urban competition may decrease the
overall efficiency of urban development processes (Dijkstra et al. 2013).
   Fujita et al. (1999) theoretically explained this phenomenon as the “agglomera-
tion shadow.” An agglomeration shadow is a phenomenon in which large cities and
regions with strong economic agglomeration have an economic impact on neighbor-
ing smaller regions (Fujita and Thisse 2002). Specifically, it refers to a situation in
which the economic activities of small cities and suburbs near large cities are sup-
pressed or inhibited from growth due to large cities absorbing resources, including
human resources, from those neighboring regions. The agglomeration shadow effect
and the borrowed size effect are related and affect the economic dynamics between
regions (Meijers 2005; Phelps and Ozawa 2003; Burger and Meijers 2016).
   The two concepts may have a complementary relationship. Small cities and sub-
urban areas located near large cities can be overwhelmed by the economic activities
of large cities, but they can also grow using the resources and infrastructure of the
large cities. In other words, while some economic activities are suppressed, others
can benefit.
   However, it is also possible that a competitive relationship will arise between the
two concepts. On the one hand, if the borrowed size effect is fully demonstrated, the
impact of the agglomeration shadow will weaken, and the surrounding areas may
achieve more independent economic growth. On the other hand, if the agglomera-
tion shadow has a strong effect, the borrowed size effect will be canceled out, and
the growth of the surrounding areas will be severely restricted.
   These relationships are an important point of discussion in formulating regional
policies and urban planning; however, as discussed by Hornbeck et al. (2004), the cir-
cumstances under which the agglomeration shadow becomes dominant have not been
fully clarified owing to the complexity of the analytical approach. Nevertheless, a com-
plex relationship exists between the borrowed size effect and agglomeration shadow. In
other words, the borrowed size effect can be strengthened or weakened by the agglom-
eration shadow. To avoid the difficulty of interpreting agglomeration shadows, this
Asia-Pacific Journal of Regional Science (2024) 8:1111–1134                                                  1117


study does not explicitly consider agglomeration shadows but instead focuses on the net
effect of geographical externalities from a regional macroperspective and statistically
examines the impact of the borrowed size effect on the productivity performance of
regional economies.

2.4 Research focus

This study statistically clarifies the effects of inter-regional network formation on the
productivity performance of regional economies relative to conventional geographical
externalities.
   Research has shown conflicting results regarding the relationship between inter-
regional network externalities and traditional geographical externalities. Some studies
indicate that these two types of externalities complement each other (Boix and Trul-
lén 2007; Tian et al. 2022; Wetwitoo and Kato 2017). Conversely, other research sug-
gests that network externalities can substitute conventional geographical externalities
(Fu et al. 2023; Huang et al. 2020; McCann and Acs 2011; Tang et al. 2021). Notably,
recent investigations in China have provided substantial evidence that network exter-
nalities exert a greater influence on innovation activities within special economic zones
compared to traditional geographical externalities (Jiang et al. 2022; Liu et al. 2023;
Yang et al. 2022; Zheng et al. 2023).
   To contribute to the discussion of previous research, this study clarifies the effects of
borrowed size on productivity performance improvements from an industrial perspec-
tive. In particular, using a dynamic production function approach clarifies the short-
and long-term effects of geographical network economies on the productivity perfor-
mance of industries.

attributable to these density-related economic benefits.

infrastructure, encompassing the social capital stock of roads, ports, and air-

from reduced travel times, lower transportation expenses, and increased shipping
capacities (Aschauer 1989). This infrastructure development enhances industries’
productive potential, allowing them to source inputs more efficiently and opti-

onstrated that areas with high-quality social capital stocks exhibit greater TFP or
the Solow residual (Bröcker et al. 2019; Eberts and McMilen 1999; Otsuka and

3.3 Estimation model

There are endogeneity concerns with the estimation of Eq. (2) (Graham 2009). In
the field of geographical externalities, researchers often assume that the error terms
in the equation are independent of the regression coefficients. However, this assump-
tion becomes invalid when dealing with endogenous regression coefficients. The
factors involved in geographical externalities are linked to productivity and may
themselves be endogenous variables.
   Many previous studies have addressed this concern through two-step least squares
methods with instrumental variables. However, some have reported that the esti-
mates are unlikely to be biased even if geographical externalities are endogenous.
For example, Ciccone (2002) and Ciccone and Hall (1996) have shown that esti-
mating geographical externalities with instrumental variables slightly differs from
estimating them with the least squares method. Studies by Henderson (2003) and
Rosenthal and Strange (2004) have also determined that endogenous regression
coefficients have a minimal effect. However, Combes and Gobillon (2015) have
pointed out that the issue should be effectively addressed even though solid evidence
that bias arises from endogeneity is lacking.
   In this study, we address this concern using dynamic panel estimation, in which
lags of the explained variable are introduced to the explanatory variables (Arellano
and Bond 1991). This method addresses the concern that the explanatory variables
and the error terms will be correlated and inconsistent in the usual ordinary least
squares estimation (Cameron and Trivedi 2009). This analytical approach is well
suited for numerous individual effects and limited time-series data scenarios. It
particularly applies to our research, given that our dataset encompasses a relatively
brief time frame (18 years) and a wide array of cross-sectional units (47 in total).
   The estimation model for dynamic panel estimation for Eq. (2) is as follows:
   ΔlnTFPjt = 𝛼ΔlnTFPjt−1 + 𝛽ΔlnBSjt + 𝛾ΔlnDSjt + 𝛿ΔlnTCjt + 𝜆ΔlnHCjt + Δ𝜀jt ,

the equation are the estimated parameters.
   If the effect of borrowed size is significant, the sign of β is positive. When the
effect of population density is manifest, the sign of γ is positive, but when the

negative external effect associated with overcrowding is manifest, the sign of γ is
negative. The sign of δ is positive because traffic capital, as a geographical external-
ity, raises the productivity of the industry. However, when negative external effects
such as traffic congestion are large, the sign of δ is negative because transportation
capital overcrowding reduces the industry’s productivity. The sign of λ is positive
because an increase in the educational capacity of laborers increases the industry’s
productivity performance.
    Short- and long-run elasticities are obtained from the estimated results of Eq. (3).
We assume that short-run TFP is less responsive to geographical externalities than
long-run TFP because, while some changes in the production system, such as the
operation of plants and equipment, can be adjusted immediately, others, such as
adjustments in the amount of employment or location, cannot. That is, we cannot
expect the production system to change quickly in response to geographical exter-
nalities. Therefore, short-term output may deviate from the optimal output in the
long run. The production system will not adjust immediately to the long-run equi-
librium but should converge to the optimal level over time if producers are rational.
    Traditional TFP functions typically omit the lag variable for TFP in their estima-
tions due to the absence of an adjustment process for productivity performance. This
approach assumes that production at different time points is unrelated, resulting in
no lag in the adjustment process. The standard TFP function presumes that expec-
tations do not incur costs that influence current decisions, which is not a realistic
assumption. In contrast, the dynamic TFP function model incorporates the adjust-
ment process of productivity performance, thus including the lag variable of TFP
in its estimation. This model allows for a gradual adjustment between the optimal
long-term productivity level and the short-term productivity level. Consequently,
researchers can derive both short-term and long-term elasticities by utilizing the
dynamic TFP function model, such as Eq. (3).


3.4 Data

The study employs panel data from 2000 to 2018, examining all industries, manu-
facturing and non-manufacturing industries. The analysis covers 47 prefectures, as
illustrated in Fig. 1. All original data in this study are accessible to the public and
can be obtained at no cost.
    To measure TFP, the study utilizes the Regional-Level Japan Industrial Produc-
tivity Database (R-JIP), which is published by the Research Institute of Economy,
Trade and Industry (RIETI). In Japan, few TFP analyses target industries (e.g., Goto

The estimation results for Eq. (3) are presented in Table 3. The table displays data
for three categories: ALL, encompassing all industries; MANU, representing the
manufacturing industry; and NMANU, denoting the non-manufacturing industry.
When performing dynamic panel estimation, ensuring that the error terms do not
exhibit serial correlation is crucial. A reliable estimator can only be achieved when
this requirement is met. Therefore, an Arellano–Bond test must be performed, and
while the first-order autocorrelations are allowed to be significant, the second-order
autocorrelations are not. The results show that the estimation satisfies this condition
for all models. Furthermore, we conduct a Sargan–Hansen test, an exogenous test for
instrumental variables; the results indicate that the number of instrumental variables
is not excessive, so the conditions for dynamic panel estimation are satisfied. The
calculated coefficients represent elasticities since both the independent and depend-
ent variables are expressed in logarithmic form. Consequently, a higher estimated
coefficient value indicates a stronger influence of the independent variable on the
dependent variable.

5 Conclusion

To assess the effect of inter-regional networks on industries’ productivity perfor-
mance, this study statistically evaluates geographical externalities affecting TFP
using a dynamic TFP function approach. In particular, this study examines the
importance of localized and inter-regional spillover externalities in determining
Industry-based TFP. The results indicate that the borrowed size effect influences
the TFP of both manufacturing and non-manufacturing industries. In particular, the
long-run effect of borrowed size was larger for manufacturing industries, indicating
that geographical externalities interact across regional boundaries in the long run
as high-quality transportation networks expand the geographic scope of production
activities.
   The study also finds that the contribution of borrowed size effects to TFP
improvement differs significantly across regions, with the impact of these
effects being greater in less productive regions than in more productive ones. In
other words, low-productivity regions may improve their TFP by connecting to

inter-regional networks and strengthening their relationships with other regions,
allowing the externalities of other regions to spill over inward through increased
opportunities for interactions among regions.
   Furthermore, this study confirms the importance of localized externalities for
both manufacturing and non-manufacturing industries. The impact of localized
external economies on TFP performance is particularly large in the non-manufac-
turing industry; therefore, this reflects how the non-manufacturing market is limited
within the region. Thus, we conclude that the borrowed size effect complements the
localized external economy within the region.
   The results of this study suggest that to effectively improve the productivity
performance of industries, both strategically strengthening individual regions and

developing a high-quality transportation network that connects regions are essen-
tial. Connecting clusters with a high-quality rapid transit network and strengthening
inter-regional linkages will result in significant between-region externality spillo-
vers, which were previously region-bound. A high-quality transportation network
can expand externalities over a wider area through enhanced borrowed size effects.
   Furthermore, many developed countries aim to enhance economic sustainability
by promoting regional innovation. If the desired growth strategy is formulated from
the perspective of the location environment, the development of skilled laborers will
likely increase industrial competitiveness across all industries. The results of this
study confirm that improving labor skills contributes to improving the productiv-
ity performance of industries. Policy support for regional universities and research
institutes can effectively increase an industry’s productivity performance.
   While this study provides unique insights, we could not fully discuss the possibil-
ity of spatial autocorrelation; nevertheless, spatial interactions exist in the produc-
tion activities of economic agents (Rice et al. 2006). A credible, dynamic panel esti-
mation that considers spatial autocorrelation has not been fully developed, making it
difficult to statistically address the possibility of spatial autocorrelation in this study.
This issue should be addressed by developing analytical methods in the future.
