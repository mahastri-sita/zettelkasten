Introduction
A polycentric structure oﬀers beneﬁts for a more desirable form of urban development. Its
proponents argue that polycentricity produces a better balance between the positive and
negative aspects of agglomeration (Faludi 2004; Meijers 2007; Parr 2004). Polycentric
metropolitan areas are relatively larger than monocentric ones and have a higher popu-
lation density, greater per capita income, lower poverty rates (Arribas-Bel and Sanz-
Gracia 2014), and higher labor productivity (Meijers and Burger 2010; Veneri and Burga-
lassi 2012). Moreover, the polycentric urban structure could help to increase individual
travel pattern eﬃciency (Li et al. 2018; Lin, Allan, and Cui 2015). Much evidence has
shown that polycentricity could reduce exposure to risk and enhance urban resilience
(Shariﬁ 2019). Therefore, the study of polycentricity has received great attention
through theoretical (see Wheaton 1979; Griﬃth 1981; Fujita and Ogawa 1982; Berry
and Kim 1993; Lucas and Rossi-Hansberg 2002) and empirical explorations (i.e. McDo-
nald 1987; Giuliano and Small 1991; McDonald and Prather 1994; McMillen and McDo-
nald 1998; Antipova and Ozdenerol 2013; Arribas-Bel and Sanz-Gracia 2014; Antipova
2015; Huang, Liu, and Zhao 2015; Antipova, Skryzhevska, and Kung 2017).

    Despite these positive features, other researchers have linked polycentricity to a “way of
seeing” urban space and as a desired development strategy that promotes social, economic,
and environmental goals to problems and limitation. Critics point out that higher GDP
per capita in polycentric countries is more likely to be achieved on the national rather
than regional level (Brezzi and Veneri 2015), which explains the increasing disparities
between regions (Meijers and Sandberg 2008; Veneri and Burgalassi 2012). Polycentricity
causes lower levels of urban productivity, although this impact appears to weaken as a
city’s population increases (Li and Liu 2018). Meanwhile, the eﬀect of the polycentric
structure in reducing urban traﬃc congestion in China weakens as the metropolitan popu-
lation size exceeds a certain threshold (Li, Xiong, and Wang 2019). The beneﬁt of short
commutes in polycentric cities could only be realized through a better balance between
jobs and resident workers in each population center (Li et al. 2018). Thus, the eﬀectiveness
of the polycentric urban structure in reducing traﬃc and promoting sustainable develop-
ment depends on many aspects.
    Regardless of these mixed ﬁndings, the polycentricity discourse tends to shift from an
analytical tool into a normative agenda in the form of a spatial policy (Davoudi 2003).
Various places in Europe, the United States, and Asia have applied normative practices
of polycentricity. In Europe, the concept has been adopted at the regional and national
scale (Meijers 2008). Several locations in the United States have implemented the policy
of polycentricity at the metropolitan scale, including Portland, Sacramento, Washington,
DC, and Chicago. The adoption of this spatial structure is reﬂected in the long-term plans
prescribing a polycentric hierarchy for these metropolitan areas (Knaap et al. 2016). Since
the early 1980s, China made attempts to implement a “decentralized cluster style” (fensan
zutuan shi) of development in Beijing through comprehensive city planning (Huang, Liu,
and Zhao 2015, 11,633). However, the success of these eﬀorts has been limited despite
changes in Beijing’s spatial structure, including its residential suburbanization and devel-
opment of several subcenters in the suburbs. Notably, the overall spatial distribution of
employment still largely follows a monocentric form with jobs concentrated in the city
center accounting for almost 60% of the city’s total employment.
    Recent studies found evidence that the polycentric structure is an emerging urban
development trend in East Asia (Phelps and Ohashi 2018). However, little is known
about the dynamics of polycentricity in the context of Southeast Asia. While Southeast
Asian cities have been recognized as being diﬀerent from industrialized Western cities,
they have a similar historical engagement with global capital ﬂows. For example, the Neth-
erlands East Indies was a destination for foreign investment since the end of the nineteenth
century and produced a vast amount of wealth for the Dutch colonial government
(Gordon 2014). Interaction between cities in the Southeast Asian region and the more
advanced cities in the western countries, those so-called “metropolitan countries”, resulted
in diﬀerent phases of urban development with both convergence towards and divergence
from the western model (Dick and Rimmer 1998, 2306). Since the 1970s, academic views
of urban development in Southeast Asia shifted from the third world cities to world cities,
to desakota, and lastly volatile globalization (Rimmer and Dick 2018, 11). Consequently,
the current urban form is inseparable from its global context. However, contemporary
studies have failed to address the causes and consequences of polycentric urban spatial
development in Southeast Asia as their focus was on the transition of human settlements
rather than to capital accumulation and the system of production.

    This paper explores the current metropolitan spatial development in Indonesia. The
Indonesian spatial pattern of urbanization reﬂects regional disparity and the country’s
economic development policy is biased towards urban areas (Firman 2004). The resulting
combination of high population density and lagging investments in infrastructure has led to
congestion, pollution, and an increased risk of disasters in many urban areas (World Bank
2016). A better understanding of urban spatial organization might contribute to appropriate
land planning strategies to address these issues (Antipova and Ozdenerol 2013). In this
respect, several attempts have been made to identify the metropolitan spatial structure of
the Jakarta Metropolitan Area (JMA), resulting in diﬀerent conclusions. While Hakim
and Parolin (2009) found that the JMA’s spatial structure matches neither the monocentric
nor the polycentric model, Hudalah et al. (2013) concluded that it is shifting from a scattered
spatial arrangement towards a polycentric region. Sadewo, Syabri, and Pradono (2018) sup-
ported the latter argument, emphasizing that the JMA’s spatial form has transformed from a
multifunctional to a mono-functional polycentric structure. The focus of these studies was
limited to the JMA, while the contrasting conclusions primarily resulted from their diﬀerent
ways of measuring polycentricity as none of those studies used a combination of morpho-
logical and functional aspects in analyzing the urban spatial form.
    For Indraprahasta and Derudder (2019), a long-term evolutionary process with
diﬀerent phases has shaped the JMA’s urban character and function in the global
economy. Nevertheless, only little is known about the relationship between the economic
function and the urban form of the JMA and other metropolitan areas in Indonesia. In
polycentric urban regions, key facets and dimensions such as internal dynamics, territorial
context and path-dependencies should be considered as the source of development vari-
ations (Schmitt et al. 2015). By comparing the strikingly distinct Medan Metropolitan
Area (MMA) and Denpasar Metropolitan Area (DMA) to JMA, this study argues that
contemporary urban form is also path-dependent to the contextual development base.
Thus, this study uses Robinson’s (2011) individualizing strategies rather than applying a
universalizing comparison approach to compare diﬀerent metropolises. It discusses the
historical aspects of each metropolitan area to explain the diﬀerentiated process leading
towards contemporary urban form.
    This objective is achieved in three ways, i.e. identify the subcenters for each metropo-
litan area, compare the level of polycentricity in those areas, and discuss how diﬀerent
growth trajectories within a similar planning system could shape urban spatial develop-
ment in unique ways. The paper contributes to the body of literature by providing rare
empirical evidence of urban polycentric development in the Southeast Asian context.
    The paper comprises seven parts. The introduction provides the research background
and the second section gives a brief overview of the study areas. The third section reviews
previous studies on urban subcenter identiﬁcation and polycentric measurement, while
the fourth section discusses data and methodology. The ﬁfth section presents the main
ﬁndings, the sixth section discusses the historical and contextual development of the
case studies, while the last section contains the conclusions.


An overview of the case studies areas
The Indonesian government has designated 14 metropolitan development areas in its
2015–2019 National Mid-Term Development Plan (RPJMN). In 2017, these areas were
4      E. SADEWO ET AL.


home to almost 34% of the national population, with an annual growth average of 1.68%
between 2010 and 2018. These metropolises are unevenly distributed: three located in
Sumatra, ﬁve in Java, one in Kalimantan, two in Sulawesi, and two in Bali and Nusa Teng-
gara. The examination of location quotient (LQ) and export value of 14 metropolitan areas
in Indonesia has revealed that the MMA has a plantation economic base and is the largest
metropolitan area in terms of agricultural export value. The JMA’s economy is strongly
supported by commercial and business activities, although their largest export sector is
manufacturing (Table 1). Meanwhile, the DMA has developed global-scale tourism-
related service activities. Thus, we selected the MMA, the JMA, and the DMA as the
case studies because these metropolitan areas represented the strongest linkage to the
global network of capital accumulation in the sectors of agriculture, manufacturing, and
service, respectively.
    The MMA is located on the northeastern coast of Sumatra, with easy access to the busy
Malacca Strait shipping lanes and closeness to several neighboring countries (Figure 1).
Owing to this strategic geographic location, it plays an important role in the Indonesia-
Malaysia-Thailand Growth Triangle. The MMA consists of 52 sub-districts covering an
area of 3016.97 km2. It is also known as Mebidangro, abbreviated from the names of
the places that compose the area: Medan City as its metropolitan core is surrounded by
Binjai City, Deli Serdang Regency, and parts of Karo Regency. Medan is the capital of
North Sumatra Province, the fourth most populous city in Indonesia, and the largest
city outside Java (Tarigan et al. 2017). Karo Regency was excluded from the study as it
is located farthest from the metropolitan core and only parts of it are included in the
MMA. Therefore, in this paper, the term MMA refers to Mebidang rather than to the
entire Mebidangro region.
    Located on the northwestern coast of Java, the JMA comprises 183 sub-districts across
the three provinces of West Java, Banten and the special province of DKI Jakarta. DKI
Jakarta is the metropolitan core and is surrounded by the peripheral municipalities of
Bogor, Depok, Bekasi, and Tangerang. The JMA is also known as Jabodetabek, a combi-
nation of the initial letters of each city it is composed of. Serving as the national capital, the
JMA plays a prominent role in the development of Indonesia in many aspects, i.e. social,
economic, and political (Rustiadi et al. 2015). As the main gateway for passengers and
goods into Indonesia, it has experienced rapid urbanization and evolved into an increas-
ingly multicultural and cosmopolitan area. With an area of nearly 6392 km2 and a popu-
lation of 30.97 million in 2015, the JMA is the largest metropolitan area in Indonesia, and
the second largest in the world (Sadewo, Syabri, and Pradono 2018). The region contrib-
utes 25% of the national GDP, mainly from manufacturing and high-order services
activities.
    The third study area represents an example of small island development. The DMA is
composed of 27 sub-districts and four regency-level administrative areas, covering 1753.63
km2. It comprises the core city of Denpasar and its surrounding Badung, Gianyar and
Tabanan Regencies, and is abbreviated as Sarbagita. Strategically located in the southern
part of Bali Island, the metropolitan area is renowned for its strong cultural identity and a
beautiful landscape that provides the basis for tourism. It has a population of 2.5 million
and is ranked third for annual population growth between 2010 and 2018 after the JMA
and the Banjarmasin Metropolitan Areas.

Identiﬁcation of subcenters and measuring polycentricity
Studies on polycentricity have used multiple and complex perspectives. A polycentric
urban structure is formed when one or more employment subcenters develop beyond
the Central Business District (CBD) (McMillen 2001, 448). An example of a polycentric
urban form is a metropolis consisting of several smaller, speciﬁc, and relatively adjacent
activity centers, as a natural form generated by the evolutionary process of a city (Hall
and Pain 2006). It is driven by the decentralization of economic activities, increased mobi-
lity, complex cross-commuting, and fragmented spatial distribution of activities (Davoudi
2003). Focused on intra-metropolitan areas, Hall (1999, 178) classiﬁed the polycentric
urban structure into six main activity centers: a traditional business core; secondary
business core; tertiary business core or inner edge city; outer edge city; outermost city;
and scattered specialized concentration of activities. Yet, the concept can also be
applied at larger diﬀerent geographic scales such as the inter-urban and inter-regional
scales (Davoudi 2003).
    Polycentric models of urban development have been developed to address the deﬁcit of
the monocentric model in adequately explaining the expanding urban land use, which
encourages the formation of multiple smaller centers within urban commuting areas.
Built upon empirical ﬁndings, the mainstream research on polycentricity regards these
subcenters as major employment locations (Alegría 2016). Cladera, Marmolejo Duarte,
and Moix (2009) classiﬁed the employment density-based subcenter identiﬁcation meth-
odology into ﬁve main groups: identiﬁcation of local peaks in employment density; use of
thresholds reference or cut-oﬀ values in the densities and absolute employment values;
parametric methods that analyze residuals in a regression equation of employment
density; nonparametric methods commonly using locally weighted regression models to
                                                                    ASIAN GEOGRAPHER        7


identify local employment density peaks; and some other variants such as a cubic spline
density function. However, several other methods can also identify subcenters, such as
proportional approaches and spatial statistics (Alidadi and Dadashpoor 2017).
    The employment density peaks (EDP) approach identiﬁes subcenters as job locations of
a certain density (McDonald and McMillen 1990). This method is criticized for its lack of
theoretical foundation and objectivity. Meanwhile, the proportional approach compares
the absolute number of workers in a location to the entire metropolitan area (Alegría
2016; Shearmur and Coﬀey 2002). It uses the employment-to-residential workers ratio
(E/R ratio) to estimate the net in-commuting and employment attraction. Both
methods use arbitrary measures but the latter is considered more accurate. The EDP
approach is also considered to be static and incapable of showing economic agglomeration
because it excludes the distance between ﬁrms. The threshold reference approach, which is
based on the number of jobs per acre and the absolute number of employment (Giuliano
and Small 1991), is a combination of the EDP and proportional methods. Coﬀey and
Shearmur (2001) have shown that the use of a combination of approaches produces
more eﬃcient measurements.
    The identiﬁcation of subcenters in parametric and nonparametric methods is based on
the residual analysis of the density function. Both methods compare the residuals between
real and predicted values and consider observations with the residuals that are statistically
diﬀerent from zero as candidate subcenters. The parametric method is simpler, yet it pro-
vides rigorous results. However, the parametric method only portrays the metropolitan area
one-dimensionally based on the assessed location distance to the CBD, while disregarding
the complex three-dimensional geographical nature of urban systems, which can be better
understood by analyzing historical development (Cladera, Marmolejo Duarte, and Moix
2009, 2843). A nonparametric approach improves the parametric model’s eﬃciency in iden-
tifying local maximums of employment density as a result of historical urban development
processes. Several nonparametric models have been proposed, such as a two-stage pro-
cedure involving a smoothed employment density function in the ﬁrst stage and a semipara-
metric estimation in the second stage (McMillen 2001), and quantile smoothing splines
(Cladera, Marmolejo Duarte, and Moix 2009). Meanwhile, the Exploratory Spatial Data
Analysis (ESDA) approach identiﬁes subcenters by considering spatial autocorrelation
and spatial heterogeneity (Baumont, Ertur, and Gallo 2004; Hakim and Parolin 2009).
The advantages of the method include ﬂexibility in scaling, avoidance of arbitrary cut-oﬀ
values, and ease of operation, making it preferable when studying a large number of
areas (Arribas-Bel and Sanz-Gracia 2014; Li and Liu 2018; Li, Xiong, and Wang 2019).
Nevertheless, the use of this method requires complete supporting data such as maps and
aggregate location data. Another disadvantage of the nonparametric method is the
diﬃculty to determine whether the identiﬁed “peaks” are true subcenters that correspond
to the spatial reality or random peaks in the metropolitan employment pattern (Cladera,
Marmolejo Duarte, and Moix 2009, 2844).
    While many statistical approaches are more appropriate for the analysis of individual
urban areas, most comparative urban studies use index values. Comparative polycentricity
considers the morphological aspect of the urban system and the interrelated relationships
between urban centers (Green 2007; Meijers 2008). The polycentricity does not only refer
to urban center plurality of a given delineation of the physical dimensions of the urban
system, but is also closely associated with the balance of urban center distribution
8      E. SADEWO ET AL.


(Burger and Meijers 2012). Nevertheless, the polycentric urban region should fulﬁll not
only the condition of separation between centers, but also the distribution, specialization,
and interaction between them (Parr 2004). Thus, the subcenters in a balanced multi-urban
center system should be functionally inter-connected (Green 2007). Empirical evidence
has shown that the diﬀerences between morphological and functional polycentricity
could occur to some degree (Burger and Meijers 2012). Hence, it is important to consider
both aspects in spatial structure measurements of diﬀerent regions for comparison.
   Meijers and Sandberg (2008) suggested that an area should not be seen as monocentric
or polycentric per se. For comparative purposes, polycentricity should be measured by
some value ranging from fully monocentric to fully polycentric. Several frameworks
have been developed to measure morphological polycentricity, whereas obtaining the
value of the rank-size distribution from a regression slope is the most popular (Burgalassi
2010; Burger and Meijers 2012; Veneri and Burgalassi 2012; Li and Phelps 2018). This
method is practical and straightforward (Meijers 2008), and provides accurate information
about the hierarchy of a center as a proxy of each city’s importance (Parr 2004).
   From the functional perspective, a polycentric structure requires a certain density and a
balance of interaction ﬂows between its nodes of activity. Theoretical discourses suggest
that a close relationship between commuting patterns and urban structures leads to poly-
centricity (Gordon, Kumar, and Richardson 1989; Helminen et al. 2012; Lin, Allan, and
Cui 2015). Therefore, studies widely use commuting patterns to assess the functional
aspect of polycentrism, although the functional relation could appear in various forms,
from physical ﬂows of goods to informational exchange. Green (2007) proposed social
network analysis (SNA) to identify the two requirements of functional polycentricity.
This approach is versatile and scalable, and applicable from individual to regional to
national levels. It allows to compare polycentricity functionally across scales and among
diﬀerent functions. The method has been used not only for identiﬁcation of functional
polycentricity (Liu, Derudder, and Wu 2016; Mu and Yeh 2016), but also for comparison
of morphological polycentricity (Hall and Pain 2006; Vasanen 2013; Liu et al. 2019; Yue
et al. 2019). However, it still has drawbacks of determining the maximum value limits,
which has an element of subjectivity.


Methods and data
This study adopts the identiﬁcation of subcenters approach following the classical mono-
centric standard density function hypothesis, which mainly comes in negative exponential
forms, to compare the polycentricity of the MMA, the JMA and the DMA. In this regard,
this study applies a conventional statistical approach using parametric modeling. This
involves estimating parameters generated by the models of each sub-district’s employment
density, as a function of accessibility to the CBD of each metropolitan area. However, since
the data of the actual distance between sub-districts were not available, the Euclidean dis-
tance between each sub-district centroid is used as a proxy. We obtained the distances to
the CBD in two stages. First, we arbitrarily selected CBD points within the traditional city
center known as “alun-alun” in Indonesia. An alun-alun in modern-day Indonesian cities
represents a large, central square used for recreation. It is typically surrounded by impor-
tant buildings such as mayor’s houses, oﬃces, and a central mosque. Speciﬁcally, we used
Merdeka Square in West Medan sub-district in the MMA, Monas Square in Gambir in the
                                                                    ASIAN GEOGRAPHER        9


JMA, and Puputan-Badung Square in West Denpasar in the DMA to identify the CBD
location. Second, we determined a centroid point for each sub-district. Then, we calculated
the distance between each centroid to the traditional CBD as a straight-line distance.
    Using the parametric model also allows for a comparison of the numbers and location
of subcenters obtained by previous studies. To relax a normality assumption and obtain
the best model, we tested ﬁve parametric models using diﬀerent functions of employment
density: a linear model (LIN), a negative exponential model (EXP), a logarithmic version
of the negative exponential model (also called a standard model, STD), an inverse poten-
tial or a gravitational model (GRAV), and a logarithmic version of the gravitational model
(Log–Log). We selected the best model by evaluating its coeﬃcient of determination (R2)
as a relative measure of ﬁt. The spatial symmetry breaking by creating disaggregated para-
metric models following the major transportation axes was also considered if the initial
result is dissatisfactory. For a more detailed discussion of the parametric methods used
for subcenter identiﬁcation, the reader may refer to Cladera, Marmolejo Duarte, and
Moix (2009). Since some models could produce similar R 2 values, the Root Mean
Square Error (RMSE) as an absolute measure of ﬁt was also employed for best model selec-
tion. We obtained the subcenter based on the value of the positive residuals from the best
model with the best goodness-of-ﬁt that exceed a threshold of employment density. Giu-
liano and Small (1991) proposed a threshold of 1000 jobs or a minimum density of 5,000
jobs per square mile for this threshold. However, we relaxed the threshold in this study to
1000 employment/km2 because we limit our analysis in this study to Medium and Large
Establishment (MLE) data for sub-districts.
    The decision to limit our analysis to MLEs was to compare our ﬁndings with the earlier
research (see Hudalah et al. 2013 and Sadewo, Syabri, and Pradono 2018). This study uses
similar aggregate data at the sub-district level as Hudalah et al. (2013). Nevertheless, this
study uses complete non-agricultural MLE activity data from the 2016 Economic Census,
as in Sadewo, Syabri, and Pradono (2018), to avoid reaching a partial conclusion which
may result from the assessment of the spatial distribution of a single type of economic
activities such as manufacturing (Hudalah et al. 2013). The use of MLE data is statistically
representative because, despite being small, non-agricultural MLEs contribute signiﬁcantly
to employment. To illustrate, in 2016, 2.61% of MLEs accounted for 30.92% of the total
employment in the MMA, while 4.61% of MLEs accounted for 47.64% of the employment
in the JMA, and 4.09% of MLEs represented 34.94% of the employment in the DMA (see
Table 2).
    There is a large body of previous research comparing polycentricity between regions,
but only a few studies use a combination of morphological and functional approaches
(Burgalassi 2010; Cladera, Marmolejo Duarte, and Moix 2009; Hall and Pain 2006;
Vasanen 2013; Liu et al. 2019; Yue et al. 2019). This study uses both approaches to
obtain a more comprehensive conclusion. For morphological identiﬁcation, this study
follows Burger and Meijers (2012) to estimate the regression slope of rank-size distri-
bution. Here, the size refers to the number of employment in the delineated municipal-
ity/regency and the nodes refer to the center of each municipality or regency within the
metropolitan area. Consequently, there would be three urban regions with contrasting
node numbers. Since including a greater number of nodes tends to produce a greater poly-
centric value, we chose a sample of a ﬁxed number of nodes to estimate the slope (Meijers
and Burger 2010). We created a regression model where we used a diﬀerent number (2, 3,

polycentric value. A ﬂatter slope, or a value closer to 0 indicates a more polycentric metro-
politan area. We adjusted the number of places in the MMA by splitting the Deli Serdang
Regency into two sections because MMA had the smallest number of nodes. We set the
sub-districts of areas mostly located east of 98,65o longitude as Eastern (E) Deli
Serdang and their counterparts as Western (W) Deli Serdang. This division creates 12
equal sub-districts in both areas.
   To identify the functional aspect of polycentricity, we used Green’s (2007) SNA
approach by comparing the General Functional Polycentricity (Pgf) index of the study
areas with other Pgf index benchmarks. While the index value is theoretically between
0 and 1, empirical evidence from Europe shows that a functionally notable polycentric
area has a maximum index value of 0.19 (Hall and Pain 2006, 52). We derived the data
used in this method from the result of commuter surveys, published by Indonesia’s
Central Statistics Agency (BPS). The surveys were conducted in two phases: in the JMA
in 2014, and in the MMA and DMA in 2015. The commuter survey in the JMA covers
13,120 household samples, while surveys conducted in the MMA and DMA comprise
2,400 household samples each. This survey uses a slightly diﬀerent deﬁnition of commut-
ing. The United States and several continental European countries deﬁne commuting as a
recurring regular travel activity for the purposes of work or school, regardless of its desti-
nation. Meanwhile, commuting in Indonesia and several Scandinavian countries is deﬁned
as a daily travel activity that passes the municipal administrative boundary of their resi-
dential area. Thus, the location of employment which we used to compose the origin-des-
tination (OD) matrix is based on the regency-level boundaries.

Bogor City as a potential subcenter. This study, in contrast, uses the distance decay as a
weight of the parametric method and can better capture the actual economic development
patterns.


The comparative polycentricity
The location of employment centers within the three metropolises reﬂects their contem-
porary urban structure. With respect to the average rank-size regression slope of diﬀerent
numbers of incorporated places (2, 3, and 4), the three metropolises exhibit diﬀerent spec-
trums of polycentricity. Based on the measurement used by Meijers and Sandberg (2008),
a morphological value of −0.44 for the JMA suggests that this metropolis is very poly-
centric, as Tangerang City and Tangerang Regency, the two locations that ranked
second and third in employment, are located outside the JMA’s urban core. Meanwhile,
the morphological values of −1.12 for the DMA and −2.21 for the MMA indicate
average polycentric and very monocentric structures, respectively. The shift of the employ-
ment center to Badung Regency, just outside the traditional urban core, creates a more
balanced morphology in the DMA. As for the strong monocentricity of the MMA, it
can be mainly explained by the greater employment discrepancy between the metropolis’s
urban core and peripheral area. Thus, the diﬀerences in employment center location
between the MMA and DMA have inﬂuenced its morphological polycentricity levels,
regardless of their similar tendency of centrality.
    We also identiﬁed polycentricity based on the functional connection between settle-
ments from the intra-metropolitan commuting patterns. Having speciﬁed an administra-
tive municipal boundary to limit settlement locations, we found that commuting activity
makes up only a small part of daily commutes. Table 3 shows that the share of commuters
in the three metropolitan areas only accounts for less than 20% of the total working popu-
lation. Nevertheless, commuter ﬂows can be quite signiﬁcant if we would consider the
absolute number of the labor force. In the JMA, for instance, the number of daily commu-
ters reaches 3.5 million people. Compared to the MMA or the DMA, which have only 315
and 187 thousand commuters, respectively, the JMA has a more complex commuting
14         E. SADEWO ET AL.


pattern. Although the traditional commuters from the suburbs to JMA’s core still domi-
nate the travel patterns, the ﬂow to the suburban areas is sizeable. There were 10.2%
reverse commuters and 33.39% cross commuting activities, which indicate the importance
of suburban economic activities. Such complexities do not occur in the MMA, while in
Denpasar, the reverse and cross commuting ﬂows together make up a greater value of
59.89%. Nevertheless, this situation cannot be interpreted as an increase in complexity
since the CBD is located in the suburban area contiguous to the DMA core (Figure 2(f)).
   We veriﬁed the importance of suburban economic activity using the general functional
polycentricity index (Pgf) proposed by Green (2007). Although the special functional
polycentricity index (Psf) of the MMA is quite balanced between in- and out-commuting,
the overall Pgf value was low compared to Hall and Pain’s (2006, 52) benchmark. Table 4
shows an unbalanced Psf for the DMA, whereas its out-commuting ﬂow is more poly-
centric than the in-commuting ﬂow. Nevertheless, its Pgf value is equal to that of the
MMA. Thus, the MMA and DMA are not polycentric in terms of a functional network
between settlements. The JMA, on the other hand, has a larger out-commuting Psf
value but the daily travel pattern is spread more evenly between traditional and cross com-
muting, the resulting Pgf value of 0.11 imply that the JMA currently has a medium level of
functional polycentricity.


The contextual perspective
At some point, the contrast between the three metropolises urban forms may be largely
determined by the striking diﬀerence in their population size. A review of the subcenter
formation theory by McMillen and Smith (2003) emphasized the importance of popu-
lation numbers and commuting costs in determining the urban spatial equilibrium. Never-
theless, previous studies have also shown that neither population nor urban size could
guarantee polycentricity. To illustrate, Hong Kong was monocentric in terms of popu-
lation concentration but polycentric when it comes to employment locations (Walls
and Wang 1997). Griﬃth and Wong (2007) identiﬁed the New York Metropolitan
Area, ranked ﬁrst in population size in the United States, as monocentric. Meanwhile,
they identiﬁed other metropolises such as Atlanta or Cleveland, which ranked 11th and
16th, respectively, as polycentric. Polycentric structures could also be found in almost
all European urban regions, regardless of their size (Meijers, Hoogerbrugge, and
Cardoso 2018). In China, most mega-city regions are surprisingly lacking in polycentricity
(Mu and Yeh 2016). This suggests that in several cases, population size is not the primary
determinant. Thus, we might need to consider other factors, since it becomes increasingly
diﬃcult to separate history and geography, time and space (Soja and Hadjimichalis 1979).

    The MMA has grown with the development of Medan City, as a trading center for
various plantation commodities produced in the surrounding areas such as rubber,
tobacco, palm oil, tea, and coﬀee. The development of Medan begun in the nineteenth
century, when the Sultan of Deli invited Dutch businessmen to open tobacco plantations
and granted them concession rights (Elida 2018). The success of the tobacco plantation
encouraged many entrepreneurs to expand the plantation business in the region to
rubber and palm oil. Following the massive capital inﬂows, the Dutch moved their govern-
ment center from Bengkalis to Medan (Nurhabsyah 2019). Supported by the capital and
management system from the Netherlands and various European companies, a 200-kilo-
meter rubber belt emerged during the ﬁrst four decades of the 1900s, equipped by the Deli
railway system (Kaur 2004, 86–87). The rapid growth of plantations by the early of the
twentieth century increased the demand for labor. To fulﬁll this demand, the Coolie Ordi-
nance policy by the Deli Planters Association (DPU) facilitated the migration of workers
from Java, India and China, creating a multicultural society. However, many elements of
the migrant worker’s native cultures have not been practiced again today, so that outsiders
tend to see the present population as a homogeneous identity (Nurhabsyah 2019).
    The shift to rubber plantation of today North Sumatra Province began in the middle of the
twentieth century in anticipation of the needs of the automotive industry in the western
countries. To date, rubber product-related industries generate the largest employment and
second-largest output value in the Province. Meanwhile, palm oil-related industries rank
second in employment and ﬁrst in total output share. Within the last two decades, there has
been an increasing role of both commodities in global trade. Data from Indonesia’s Central
Statistics Agency (BPS) shows that in 1997, palm oil and rubber products from North
Sumatra made up 45.7% of the region’s total export. In 2017, the share of these commodities
in the total export of North Sumatra had increased to 62.4% and one-ﬁfth of the total national
palm oil and rubber product exports. One of the biggest palm tree plantation concessions
managed by the 2nd State-Owned Plantation Holding (PTPN 2), and located in the Lubukpa-
kam Sub-district of Deli Serdang, has operated from the early 1970s. In 2016, palm oil products
and its derivatives contributed nearly 73% of Deli Serdang’s capital turnover.
    In the post-independence era, the growth of Medan City led to changes in its spatial
pattern (Tarigan et al. 2017). Subsequently, the city’s economic focus shifted from the
plantation-based primary economic activities to industry and services, reﬂected by the
establishment of the ﬁrst industrial estate with an area of 514 hectares in the southern
part of Medan in 1988. To accommodate the changing nature of the economy, the munici-
pal government has implemented multicentered spatial planning policies since 2011.
However, these policies are still limited to Medan City. Under the spatial masterplan
developed by the government of Medan City, the city is expected to have two services-
based centers and eight subcenters with each having speciﬁc functions (Medan City
Spatial Planning 2011–2031, 2011).
    The JMA has been managed and contested by various forces over time until it came
under the control of the Dutch East India Company (VOC) in 1619. The transition
between ruling power from the VOC to the Dutch colonial government transformed
the area from the company headquarters to a capital city. As such, Jakarta became the
center of the Dutch colonial government in Indonesia, while its future expanding metro-
politan area (the JMA) developed as a base for securing and expanding their commercial
activities in Asia (Indraprahasta and Derudder 2019). The Indonesian government
16      E. SADEWO ET AL.


repeated this strategy after the country’s independence. Since the implementation of lib-
eralization policies in the mid-1980s, the region has seen an increase in capital inﬂow,
especially in the manufacturing sectors.
    Industrialization is the key factor for the rapid urbanization in the region (Hudalah
et al. 2013). Notably, the urban transformation of the JMA is driven mainly by economic
expansion manifested in the rapid construction of industrial complexes and new satellite
cities. This resulted in a spatial expansion with mixed land use on the outskirts of Jakarta
(Rustiadi and Panuju 2002). To avoid social discomfort, gated communities developed
rapidly along with various amenities, reﬂecting American-styled metropolitan expansion
(Dick and Rimmer 1998). During the “New Order” era of 1966–1997 (named so to dis-
tinguish it from the economic collapse and political instability of the preceding “old
order” period), Indonesia’s development process was marked by the absence of planning
and regulations (Rukmana 2015). Meanwhile, liberal economic policies resulted in
planned as well as unplanned urban forms (Winarso 2011). This so-called urban
duality was a consequence of the increased demand for land, which encouraged massive
land banking practice by the private sector, causing largely uncontrolled development.
    The DMA has maintained its status as the center of Bali Island’s economic activities, the
largest tourist destination in Indonesia and one of the most popular holiday locations in
the world for many years. Over the last two decades, Bali has accounted for 32% of Indo-
nesia’s total foreign tourist visits. The tourism-related economy such as accommodation
and restaurants contribute 22% of the regional GDP. Almost 66% of Bali’s GDP originates
from the DMA (BPS 2016). The 2016 Economic Census shows that the hotel business has
generated the highest employment and second-largest annual output. Overall, tourism-
related activities such as accommodation, food and beverages, and leisure share 33% of
the total employment in the DMA.
    The tourism development in Bali is inseparable from the island’s open culture and atti-
tude towards the outside world (Picard 2006, 21). Before the twentieth century, most area
of Bali is controlled by several Hindu kingdoms, with the major economic activities revol-
ving around slavery trade. Having conquered the island, the Dutch government began to
build infrastructure and opened a shipping lane to Bali in the early twentieth century. This
attracted a ﬂow of tourism to Bali which continues to date. However, the mushrooming of
hotels and accommodations did not start until the 1990s around leisure activities that con-
centrated in Badung Regency. The increase in tourist arrivals has made the DMA the main
location for foreign investment in various accommodation facilities (Yuliandari, Martha,
and Widodo 2014). In fact, half of the foreign direct investment in the DMA is related to
global hotel chains. The massive development of tourism facilities contributed to a rapid
rise in employment, especially in Denpasar City and Badung Regency, allowing the DMA
to grow into a cosmopolitan area consisting of local migrants and many foreign residents.
Denpasar and Kuta have been planned as the urban core, while other places such as
Gianyar, Tabanan and Ubud are arranged as local centers for trade and services
(Rahayu, Haigh, and Amaratunga 2018). The uncontrolled property growth in the
DMA has motivated Bali’s provincial government to introduce a moratorium on hotel
development. However, due to its failure to prevent new massive development, the mor-
atorium is considered ineﬀective (Yuliandari, Martha, and Widodo 2014).
    Although the linkage between the three foregoing metropolitan areas to the global
capital has begun since the Dutch occupation, each of them had diﬀerent moments of

local economic leaps. By considering MLEs as long-term planned capital accumulation,
the acceleration of the JMA’s economy began two decades earlier than in the MMA
and the DMA (Figure 3). The establishment numbers in the JMA have grown sharply

strongly related to the policies of economic openness to foreign direct investment in the

Discussion
Kloosterman and Musterd (2001) argued that the reasons for urban restructuring could be
summarized as economic, technological, or societal changes. However, they emphasized
that research on polycentricity should not merely focus on physical or spatial form of
an urban area and its functional relationship, but also on political entities and cultural
identity. In other words, the context of development could oﬀer a better understanding
of urban spatial outcomes. Empirical evidence shows that most current polycentric
urban regions such as San Francisco, Tuscany, Emilia Romagna, Mexico City, Barcelona,
Tokyo, Beijing, and Hangzhou are on the scent of manufacturing development trajectory
in their urban history (Burgalassi 2010; Cowell 2010; Fernández-Maldonado et al. 2014;
Garcia-López and Muñiz 2010; Huang et al. 2017; Sorensen 2001; Yue, Liu, and Fan
2010). Other polycentric urban regions which primarily developed from the rise of
18      E. SADEWO ET AL.


non-manufacturing industry, such as the Randstad in the Netherland, are located in proxi-
mity to specialized cities, forming an urban network (Cowell 2010). Even if the manufac-
turing sector has shrunk or the economic structure has shifted towards services,
manufacturing industry would still exist in the suburban area and be of signiﬁcant
importance.
    The history of the three examined metropolitan areas portrays a long-term globalized
urban development with diﬀerent trajectories. Following Dick and Rimmer (1998), urban
form divergences between the 1940s-1970s should have been re-converged after the 1980s
due to the increase of trade and investment in global cities. Yet, the trade liberalization
policy taken by the Indonesian government could not decrease the spatial concentration
of industry. Industrial activities did not disperse to other regions because of the manufac-
turing industry’s need to locate close to the main ports, decisions by policymakers and the
situation of dictatorship and political instability (Sjöberg and Sjöholm 2004). Thus, the
industry-led polycentric development argument seems to apply only to the JMA. The
shift of the industries from the core of the JMA to the periphery triggered the post-sub-
urbanization process featuring a fragmented and polycentric structure (Hudalah and
Firman 2012). In fact, the recently formed subcenters in the JMA periphery specialize
in manufacturing sectors (Sadewo, Syabri, and Pradono 2018).
    Although the MMA has followed the same strategy by establishing the Medan Indus-
trial Parks in 1988, its industrialization process seems to be going nowhere as the indus-
trial land development trends stalled. The specialized location quotient of Deli Serdang’s
industry, for instance, indicates that the MMA has experienced a decentralization process.
Yet, it seems too insuﬃcient in scale to compete with the JMA because Deli Serdang as the
main hinterland for the MMA could not escape its status as an underdeveloped region
(Akhir et al. 2016). Meanwhile, the dependency on tourism activities has a double
impact on urban development in the DMA. First, eﬀorts to preserve the local culture
have prevented the growth of manufacturing industries. Yet, this situation provides a con-
ducive environment for the growth of cultural creative industries, which are less sensitive
to distance. Second, the tourist attractions that are scattered around the other parts of Bali
can mostly be reached within several hours from the current CBD of the DMA. This con-
tributes to prevent the emergence of other subcenters away from the CBD.
    The institutional factor plays an important role in steering these historical and econ-
omic developments of metropolitan areas and triggers their transformation into poly-
centric structures. Cowell (2010) argued that a polycentric region with a high level of
economic complementarities would form under weak regional governments; in places
with a strong regional identity; and in regions that have actively pursued polycentrism
as a development strategy. The importance of planning eﬀorts was found to be substantial
in Tokyo in its satellite city development (Sorensen 2001) and in the industrial relocation
and hukou (i.e. household registration) policy in China (Huang et al. 2017; Yue, Liu, and
Fan 2010). Another important factor that encourages polycentric development is the avail-
ability of transportation infrastructure. The cases of Barcelona, Paris, Beijing, and Tokyo
substantiate the importance of transportation system to encourage the employment relo-
cation process (Garcia-López and Muñiz 2010; Garcia-López, Hémet, and Viladecans-
Marsal 2017; Huang et al. 2017; Sorensen 2001). Under the inﬂuence of globalization,
the factors of governance and accessibility could play a key role in achieving polycentric
development (Sweet, Bullivant, and Kanaroglou 2016).
                                                                      ASIAN GEOGRAPHER       19


    Although it was not the intention of the strategy, infrastructure biases may have also
played an important role in the polycentric development of the JMA. The MLEs in the
JMA were largely concentrated in the urban core until the extensive development of a
toll road network facilitated its decentralization. As the national center of politics and econ-
omics, the JMA has become the center of infrastructure investments, thus surpassing other
metropolitan areas in Indonesia. The JMA’s toll road and rail networks cover a large area,
allowing for the relocation of industry to its periphery. Furthermore, a diverse regional iden-
tity and complex institutional setting with many stakeholders have weakened the role of the
local government. Industrial land development in the metropolitan outskirts is heavily con-
trolled by the private sector (Hudalah and Firman 2012; Hudalah et al. 2013). The uncon-
trolled land development creates the contrast of planned and unplanned space in JMA’s
periphery, repeating what Winarso (2011) termed as “urban dualism”.
    The MMA and the DMA have relatively strong regional identities but less governmen-
tal support, as evident from their late infrastructure development. The Belawan-Medan-
Tanjung Morawa toll road in the MMA was operational in 1986, two years before the
Jakarta-Cikampek toll road. However, it took almost three decades for the government
to expand the toll road coverage in the MMA. In the DMA, the Bali-Mandara toll road
has only been operational since 2013 within a limited range from the airport to the
inner city. While the MMA is already equipped with a passenger train system, the
DMA has no train system at all. This lack of infrastructure makes it diﬃcult to attract
investment in manufacturing and encourage the establishment of new subcenters.


Conclusion
This paper contributes to the literature on polycentric urban development in Southeast
Asia by describing the causes and consequences of polycentric development in three
metropolitan areas in Indonesia, namely Medan, Jakarta, and Denpasar. We have selected
these metropolitan areas because of their long historical engagement with global capital-
ism regardless of their striking diﬀerences. An individual examination of their subcenters
gives insight into the current metropolitan spatial structure in Indonesia. While the
employment centers in the JMA have spread to several locations in the periphery, we
found only a single employment center in the MMA and DMA. This indicates that the
economic activities in both metropolitan areas are still strongly concentrated. However,
in contrast to the employment center of the MMA, which is located close to the traditional
CBD, the employment center in the DMA is located outside the traditional CBD and the
urban core. The contrast in CBD location ﬁndings implies that the DMA has diﬀerent
monocentric characteristics, compared to the MMA.
   The supposition is conﬁrmed by assessing the comparative polycentricity level of the
three metropolitan areas; while the JMA and the MMA have a polarized morphology,
the DMA’s urban structure is in-between these two metropolitan areas. In addition, the
commuter travel patterns reveal that the functional polycentricity of the MMA and
DMA is focused on the core and the CBD. Although both regions have a similar functional
polycentricity value, their in- and out-functional polycentricity are quite diﬀerent. The
DMA shows a more reversed commuting pattern to the supposedly suburban area
while commuting in the MMA is dominated by the traditional pattern from suburban
areas to the core. In the JMA, the multiple subcenters create a more balanced commuting
20      E. SADEWO ET AL.


direction and the general functional polycentricity value indicates a moderate level of
polycentricity. The variation of functional polycentricity between the three areas parallels
the diﬀerence of their morphology; i.e. the location of CBD strongly corresponds to the
commuting pattern. These ﬁndings highlight the need to incorporate both aspects in
future polycentricity studies.
   Regarding the speciﬁc contextual and historical development, this study argues that
polycentricity is not necessarily dependent on population, nor economic size. What dis-
tinguishes the JMA and other established polycentric metropolises in the foreign
countries, from the MMA and the DMA is the background of its economic development.
Compared to agricultural or tourism-related industries, the manufacturing sector has
proven to eﬀectively boost urban transformations in Indonesia. Such diﬀerence in regional
economic bases of urban development is likely to determine an urban form, but more evi-
dence is needed to support this hypothesis. However, it should be noted that the existence
of multiple subcenters from industrial complexes development in the JMA was not driven
by the government’s planning strategy. For years, manufacturing industries may have
developed in the JMA because the region has been the focus of government investments.
But industrial complex expansion and land conversion in the JMA’s peripheral areas was
largely controlled by private sectors. The provision of transportation infrastructure might
help to pursue a desired urban form, yet, other regions seeking similar manufacturing
development have failed to do so because national policies favored Jakarta as the capital
and they lack the JMA’s strategic location.
   This study concludes that among the three Indonesian metropolitan areas that are the
focus of this research, only the JMA has a polycentric spatial structure. This study
conﬁrms the previous ﬁndings of a polycentric structure in the JMA, but not in the
other metropolitan areas. Despite using the latest data, this research has several shortcom-
ings. First, the identiﬁcation of urban morphology is limited to MLEs. Despite their sig-
niﬁcant contribution to employment, the total number of MLEs is less than the total
amount of micro and small establishments. Thus, the conclusions about morphology
are only part of the whole story. Second, the study only compared three metropolitan
areas. For a more comprehensive conclusion, future studies should also compare Indone-
sian metropolises to similar-sized metropolises in other developing countries to control for
more variation of urban development. Third, most studies in this ﬁeld have analyzed the
level of polycentricity (both functional and morphological) of the given city-regions, but
relatively little is known about how a polycentric city-region has developed. Therefore, as
Li and Phelps (2019) have indicated, future research could pay more attention to the com-
bination of quantitative and qualitative empirical methods especially to explain the micro–
macro level mechanisms behind the development of polycentric urban regions.
