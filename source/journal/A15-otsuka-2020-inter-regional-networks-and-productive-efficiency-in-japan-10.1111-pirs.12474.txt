1 | IN TR O DU C T ION

In Japan, the concentration of economic activities in Tokyo is increasing. Tokyo has economic advantages, enabling it
to overcome high land prices and congestion costs. Thus, Tokyo exhibits a high level of agglomeration of economic
activities. The expansion of city size through such centralization increases the productivity and productive efficiency
of cities and achieves sustained growth. The role of agglomeration in improving productivity and productive efficiency
is well known in the context of agglomeration economies (Marshall, 1890). Traditional empirical studies have revealed

the importance of economies of scale and transportation costs as factors that are conducive to the creation of
agglomeration economies (Combes & Gobillon, 2015; Rosenthal & Strange, 2004). Geographic links between firms
facilitate research and development and innovation through exchange of ideas, information, and technological
knowledge (Carlino & Kerr, 2015; Fujita & Thisse, 2002). An advantage of agglomeration is that geographic proximity
to the centralized location of firms increases and improves the information available to all market participants.
Through the process of information interchange, market participants can develop more appropriate and accurate
forecasts of the market environment and improve their ability to compete in the market. Knowledge accumulated
by a firm is transmitted to other firms in various ways. This knowledge spillover leads to the development of new
technologies and products, thereby increasing competitiveness in the region.
      These traditional agglomeration theories predict the permanent growth of large cities at the expense of local
(peripheral) areas. Furthermore, if agglomeration's lock‐in effect works, the disparities between large cities and local
areas will inevitably expand. In Japan, however, the concentration of economic activities in Tokyo has accelerated
since the mid‐1990s, but regional disparities have not increased (see Appendix A). Rather, the economic power of
local areas is catching up, and economic disparities between large cities and local areas are narrowing (Otsuka & Goto,
2016).1 Traditional agglomeration theory cannot explain these realities satisfactorily.
      One potential explanation is the scope of agglomeration economies. High‐speed transportation networks have
been developed nationwide in Japan, with Tokyo as the hub of the network. The formation of inter‐regional networks
as a result of investment in transportation infrastructure has expanded the scope of innovation and knowledge spill-
overs in agglomeration areas. The development of high‐speed railways and airports reduces the time costs of travel
and increases opportunities for people to come into contact. This development not only increases the productive effi-
ciency of a particular firm but also increases that of other firms by increasing the frequency of information exchange.
Firms that are not located in agglomeration areas can increase productive efficiency by easily accessing the agglom-
eration economies of agglomeration areas. The knowledge spillover beyond the region suggests that the scope of the
agglomeration economies expands. The economic impact of expanding agglomeration geographic coverage through
transportation networks has been verified by many studies for many countries (Graham, 2007; Holl, 2012; Lall, Shalizi,
& Deichmann, 2004; Melo, Graham, Levinson, & Aarabi, 2016; Montolio & Solé‐Ollé, 2009; Rice, Venables, &
Patacchini, 2006; Stelder, 2016). However, research on Japan, to date, has been insufficient.
      This study analyses the impact of agglomeration and inter‐regional networks on the productive efficiency of the
regional economy. Several studies have identified the relationship between agglomeration and productive efficiency
but they have tended to focus on specific economic activities rather than on the entire national economy (Beeson &
Husted, 1989; Driffield & Munday, 2001; Fritsch & Slavtchev, 2011; Ke & Yu, 2014; McCoy & Moomaw, 1995; Mitra,
1999, 2000; Tsekeris & Papaioannou, 2017). Otsuka, Goto, and Sueyoshi (2010) and Otsuka and Goto (2013) show
that agglomeration and improved inter‐regional networks have a positive impact on the productive efficiency of
the Japanese industries. This finding indicates that productive efficiency increases with decreasing inter‐regional dis-
tances to large cities. However, decreasing distances to large cities also has a negative effect of increasing inter‐
regional competition. This is described as “agglomeration shadows” in the new economic geography (NEG) theory.
This study clarifies whether positive or negative effects of the inter‐regional network dominate by using Japan's latest
data and a renewed market access index.
      In particular, previous studies have not examined the impact of inter‐regional networks on regional disparities of
productive efficiency at all. In Japan, certain large metropolitan areas, such as the Greater Tokyo area and Kansai, are
densely populated urban centres. On the one hand, in these large metropolitan areas, the agglomeration force is
strengthened, and there are large agglomeration economies. On the other hand, in many local areas other than large
cities, population density is declining, and agglomeration force is weakening. In local areas, however, improved trans-
portation infrastructure has reduced transportation costs and attracted investment in factories. This has enabled the
vertical division of the manufacturing industry across the country and helped to achieve high cost‐efficiency. In addi-

tion, local areas have succeeded in shortening the travel time to large cities through the development of high‐speed
transportation networks, and local areas have achieved strong economic growth by “borrowing” agglomeration econ-

   Furthermore, this study focuses on the differences between industries in inter‐regional network effects. Tradition-
ally, for regional economies, the manufacturing industry has been regarded as the basic (export) industry, and the non‐
manufacturing industry has been regarded as the non‐basic industry (McCann, 2001). Because of the relatively large
number of transactions outside the region in the manufacturing industry, which is the export industry, product plan-

manufacturing industry is centred on the local service sector, economic activity across regions may be less frequent
than for the manufacturing industry. This study clarifies whether the economic effects of inter‐regional networks in
the manufacturing industry outweigh those in the non‐manufacturing industry.
   The analysis of this study on inter‐regional networks has great policy significance. Japan's national land plan aims
to make cities more compact and to strengthen intercity networks as a means to counter the shrinking economy
caused by population decline (see Appendix B for a detailed description of Japan's national land plan). The govern-
ment aims to achieve sustainable growth by forming a “super‐megaregion,” in which several large metropolitan areas
integrate into a single economic area, and by dealing with contracting economies using the large agglomeration
effects generated there. However, there is no empirical evidence to support this type of government policy. There-
fore, this study provides valuable insights into the validity of government policies on inter‐regional networking.
   The structure of the rest of this paper is as follows. Section 2 provides theoretical background on inter‐regional
networks. Section 3 describes the methods of analysis. Section 4 presents the analysis results and interprets them.
Section 5 concludes and discusses policy implications.




2 | R E L A T ED S T U D I E S : T H E O R E T I C A L B A C K G R O U N D

In traditional agglomeration theory, transportation cost and economies of scale are the primary forces shaping urban
functions and spatial organizations. Thus, urban scale is characterized by high efficiency, and higher efficiency is con-
sidered to lead to higher growth. However, it is impossible for the traditional theory to explain the reality that local
areas' growth exceeds that of large cities and is catching up to that of large cities. The city network theory, by con-
trast, solves this contradiction (Camagni, 1993). The city network theory introduces the notion that cities can borrow
the functions of other cities, not only because of their physical proximity to other cities but also because of their hor-
izontal and non‐hierarchical relationships (Boix & Trullen, 2007; Camagni, 1993; Camagni & Capello, 2004; Capello,
2000). Known as “urban network externalities,” this concept has been demonstrated in the literature (Boix & Trullen,
2007; Camagni, 1993; Hall & Pain, 2006).
   The starting point for this concept is agglomeration theory, which is developed on the basis of Alonso's (1973, p.,
200) concept of “borrowed size” (Burger, Meijers, Hoogerbrugge, & Tresserra, 2015; Meijers, Burger, & Hoogerbrugge,
2016). The traditional concept of borrowed size mixes demographics, functionality, and critical mass effects for demand
and supply and in small cities, “people can use the shopping and entertainment facilities of other cities to complement
their own, businessmen can share such facilities as warehousing and business services, and labour markets enjoy a wider
and more flexible range of demand and supply” (Alonso, 1973, p.200). Thus, agglomeration theory purports that small
cities can borrow some of the benefits of agglomeration, while avoiding congestion. This approach underscores the fact
that the agglomeration effects of cities are not necessarily limited to their physical boundaries but may spread to sur-
rounding areas. Thus, it can explain why local areas are more efficient than larger cities and why efficient urban struc-
tures exist in a local area where agglomeration economies are borrowed from the entire urban system. The concepts
of “externality fields” (Phelps, Fallon, & Williams, 2001) and “regional externalities” (Parr, 2002) were proposed to high-
light the spatial scope of urban advantage that extends far beyond city boundaries.

      Traditionally, agglomeration economies have been described in the context of urban economics (Fujita & Thisse,
2002). The agglomeration of population and firms explains the cumulative development of urban economies. Expan-
sion of the scope of agglomeration means that the external economies of urban agglomeration, which have been lim-
ited to urban spaces up to now, expands widely beyond the boundaries of cities. In other words, the concept of
borrowing size has the potential to be one basis for explaining the development of the entire regional economy
beyond cities.2 Put differently, the theoretical concept of borrowed size can overcome the limitations of traditional
geographic approaches, which assume that only cities adjacent to large cities accrue efficiency gains. The concept
of borrowed size also overcomes the limitations of geographic approaches that assume size is the only determinant
of productive efficiency and agglomeration economies. Incorporating long‐distance cooperation agreements across
the regional network can improve a region's productive efficiency, even if it is a small local region.
      According to Camagni, Capello, and Caragliu (2015, 2016), the traditional measure of borrowed size is calculated
as the population of neighboring cities discounted by distance:

networks. However, it should also be noted that network connectivity can have negative economic effects because
of competitive processes. This negative economic impact is captured by the notion of agglomeration shadows, which
is captured by NEG theory. NEG models predict the shadow effects of surrounding agglomeration (Dobkins &
Ioannides, 2001). The negative effects of competition through network connectivity are well known in Japan as
the “pull‐in phenomenon.” In other words, there are positive and negative effects on the effects of the inter‐regional
network. When the positive effects outweigh the negative effects for a region, that region enjoys the economic ben-
efits of regional network formation. This study clarifies the net impact of agglomeration areas on inter‐regional
networks.



3 | METHOD

3.1 | Stochastic frontier analysis

This study undertakes a stochastic frontier analysis (SFA) to estimate the effects of agglomeration and inter‐regional
network on productive efficiency. The SFA employed in this study conforms to the stochastic frontier function con-
ditions proposed by Aigner, Lovell, and Schmidt (1977). Furthermore, this study utilizes the one‐step approach of
Battese and Coelli (1995). It simultaneously estimates the production frontier function and the productive inefficiency
term's determinant. Traditionally, a two‐step estimation method is used, in which inefficiency is first obtained by esti-
mation of the stochastic frontier function, and the value of inefficiency is subsequently regressed to the determinant.
However, in this case, there is a discrepancy between the hypothesis regarding the distribution of the stochastic fron-
tier function's inefficiency term and the hypothesis regarding the regression equation that analyses the inefficiency
determinant. Thus, consistency of estimation results cannot be guaranteed under the two‐step approach (Coelli,
1995). By adopting the one‐step approach, we are able to avoid this problem. This SFA model approximates the eco-

4 | RESULTS

Data used in the analysis are panel data for Japan's 47 prefectures from 1980 to 2014. A description of the data
source is given in Appendix C. The software used for the estimation is Frontier 4.1 (Coelli, 1996). In this software
package, stochastic frontier models with fixed effects can be estimated for panel data. Table 1 presents the results
of the maximum likelihood estimation using (2) and (3). The estimation equation for the determinants of efficiency
is estimated at the same time as the production frontier function. In this study, two models are estimated from the
perspective of introducing time effects in terms of population density and market access. In models A, C, and E, no
time effects are introduced, while in models B, D, and F, time effects are introduced.
    To determine whether the deviations from the estimated frontier were due to the effects of inefficiency, the null
hypothesis γ = 0 was tested against the alternative hypothesis γ > 0. The null hypothesis was rejected at the 1% sig-
nificance level and γ was statistically distinct from zero. This suggests that there is an efficiency effect, and thus, we
confirm the estimates of the variables on the determinants of efficiency. There is no significant difference in the esti-
mation results with or without consideration of the time effect. Models B, D, and F are selected from the log likeli-
hood sizes, and the results are evaluated as follows.
    The coefficient sign of population density and market access, which are variables explaining productive efficiency,
are positive and statistically significant. This result indicates that agglomeration and inter‐regional networks positively
influence productive efficiency. In both the manufacturing and non‐manufacturing industries, market access parame-
ters exceed population density parameters. In particular, the market access parameters are larger in the manufacturing
industry than in the non‐manufacturing industry. This indicates that the borrowed size impact of inter‐regional net-
working is greater in the manufacturing industry than in the non‐manufacturing industry. In other words, in terms
of passenger travel, the effects of inter‐regional networks are large in the manufacturing industry. This result is as
expected in this study. In other words, this result implies that the geographical scope of production activities in the
manufacturing industry extends beyond the region and further, suggesting that the manufacturing industry is the
basic (export) industry of the regional economy.

5 | DISCUSSION AND CONCLUSION

The current dynamics in Japanese regional economic systems are consistent with traditional literature, which empha-
sizes the importance of agglomeration economies for economic growth. Population agglomeration is progressing in
the three large metropolitan areas of the Greater Tokyo area, Kansai, and Chubu, achieving high economic growth.
Urbanization is relatively stable. The Greater Tokyo area's share of the population is increasing at the expense of local
areas (see Appendix A). These trends follow global trends. However, there is no proof in recent studies that regional
disparities have been expanding steadily. The manufacturing industry is predominantly located outside of urban areas
and continues to grow, despite economic globalization, and this supports Japan's local economies. Therefore, an
explanation that goes beyond the conventional agglomeration theory is required for Japan. In this study, we show
that inter‐regional networks enhance the effectiveness of borrowed size and complement lost links between real
regional economic systems and agglomeration theory.
      Network economies are paradigms that have entered a stage in which they are generally more complementary to
the concepts of agglomeration economies. This study explores the roles of inter‐regional networks and draws new
analogies by focusing on the new roles of agglomeration economies. Agglomeration economies are known to have
traditionally relied heavily on the sheer size of a region, but this study examines whether inter‐regional networks
complement the size of the region as a factor of regional economic growth. In particular, this study focused on
upgrading passenger networks through the three major networks of roads, railways, and aviation. The empirical anal-
ysis of this study has produced new knowledge. The main findings can be summarized as follows.
      First, the improvement of inter‐regional networks increases the “borrowing” effect, whereby the benefits of
agglomeration in large cities spills over to increase the productive efficiency of the local economy. The impact of
the “borrowing size” effect is greater than the agglomeration benefit of local size. Positive effects in inter‐regional
networks are strongly evident, and in that sense, the effects of agglomeration shadows are not apparent.
      Second, the impact of the increase in productive efficiency due to the improvement of inter‐regional networks is
greater for the manufacturing industry than for the non‐manufacturing industry. The manufacturing industry plays a
role in the basic (export) industry in regional economies. Therefore, a network with the outside area is important for
production activities in the manufacturing industry.
      Third, in large metropolitan areas, such as the Greater Tokyo area and Kansai, the economic benefits by region size
were large. This means that the large metropolitan areas enjoyed a high level of agglomeration economies, driven by
population agglomeration. If a positive lock‐in effect operates in agglomeration, the large metropolitan area's sustain-
able growth is guaranteed.
      Fourth, in local areas other than large metropolitan areas, borrowed size from large city agglomeration was highly
effective in enhancing productive efficiency. The shortening of travel times to large metropolitan areas engendered
by high‐speed transportation networks through investment in transportation infrastructure greatly contributed to
the growth of the local economy.
OTSUKA                                                                                                                  127


   Fifth, the strengthening of transportation networks contributed to the convergence of regional disparities on pro-
ductive efficiency. The development of transportation networks has enabled local areas to catch up to the large met-
ropolitan areas due to the effects of borrowed size in local areas.
   These findings have substantial theoretical and policy implications. Theoretically, the observation that the
borrowed size effect occurs in regional networks calls for the geographic base of agglomeration theory to be re‐built.
It may be necessary to re‐consider the appropriateness of the term “agglomeration economies” if the economic phe-
nomenon can be shared not only within the agglomeration itself but also across regional networks. Ignoring the role
of inter‐regional networks means that regional development policies may be directed toward narrowly defined spatial
scales. The existence of an inter‐regional network economy affirms wider regional development and suggests that it
can be an effective means of putting under‐developed areas on a growth track.
   Japan is aiming to build a giant economic zone, called a “super‐megaregion,” through the formation of inter‐
regional networks, as defined in the national land plan. The goal is to create a national land structure that can deal
with the effects of population decline. The specific aim is to achieve sustainable economic growth by shortening
the travel time between regions through investment in high‐speed transportation networks, namely, the Linear Chuo
Shinkansen (magnetic levitation train), and increasing the borrowed size effect. The results of this study provide
insights into the foundations of Japanese land policies and enable predictions of regional disparities. Specifically,
the results of this study suggest that the development of high‐speed transportation networks may further reduce
regional disparities. This effect is particularly significant for the manufacturing industry.
   Future research should deepen our consideration of these findings from a micro perspective. In other words, the
mechanism of the effects of borrowed size should be elucidated. With regard to passenger travel, the number of busi-
ness trips is expected to increase as inter‐regional travel time decreases. However, the impact of an increase in the
number of business trips on productive efficiency has not been well explored. In particular, the mechanism by which
a business trip leads to increased productive efficiency needs to be clarified. Furthermore, as globalization progresses,
the regional economy needs to make full use of inter‐country network connectivity, and further research is needed to
explore ways to utilize it. Future research should also focus on analysing and understanding networks between inter-
national regions.

T H E N AT I O N AL LA N D P LA N I N J A PA N
The national land plan is a long‐term, comprehensive spatial plan for the use, development, and conservation of
national land. Since the formulation of the First Comprehensive National Development Plan in 1962, Japan's post‐
war national land planning has been based on the Comprehensive National Land Development Act. Post‐war national
land plans have been revised to resolve various issues in accordance with the needs of each era.
   The latest national land planning period is 10 years, with the 2020 Tokyo Olympic and Paralympic Games as the
interim year. Efforts during this period have been positioned to influence Japan's future development potential. The
latest national land plan defines the basic concept of the plan as the creation of “a country that promotes interaction.”
Interaction is the inter‐regional movement of people, goods, money, and information arising from the mutual cooper-
ation of various regions with diverse personalities. It is believed that dynamic interaction between regions enables
sustainable development. The means of creating this interaction is the “compactization” of the region and the forma-
tion of “inter‐regional networks.”
   In order to provide services efficiently although the population decline defines the economy, regional integration
(downsizing) is essential. The aim is to maintain the size of the markets by networking each region to prevent the mar-
kets from shrinking. The important issue here is the individuality of each region as a compact entity. Differences in
individuality between regions arise when regions independently identify their own local resources and refine their
attractiveness. This difference in individuality creates interaction between regions, and this interaction is thought
to be the source of regional vitality. Through these efforts, Japan aims to create a national land structure that will
increase the productivity and productive efficiency of the nation as a whole by providing high‐quality services effi-
ciently and creating new value, even in the face of population decline.
   An important role in creating inter‐regional interaction is the formation of inter‐regional networks through high‐
speed transportation systems. The new national land plan aims to form a “super‐megaregion” through the Linear Chuo
Shinkansen, a high‐speed transportation system, to gain high competitiveness by creating significant regional agglomer-
ation economy effects. The Linear Chuo Shinkansen is a 438‐kilometre high‐speed transportation line connecting
Tokyo, Nagoya, and Osaka. The development plan for the Linear Chuo Shinkansen was officially decided in 2011. The
Linear Chuo Shinkansen is scheduled to open in 2027 between Tokyo and Nagoya, and in 2045 between Nagoya and
Osaka. The superconducting magnetic levitation system (maximum speed: 505 kilometres/hour) has been adopted as
a driving method. It is planned to connect Tokyo and Nagoya in about 40 minutes, and Tokyo and Osaka in about 1 hour.
   The opening of the Linear Chuo Shinkansen will bring about a significant reduction in travel time. With this short-
ened travel time, Japan's major large metropolitan areas will be able to work closely together as an integrated eco-
nomic zone, creating a world‐class “super‐megaregion” on an unparalleled economic scale. This dramatic reduction
in travel time is expected to stimulate face‐to‐face communication between firms further. In other words, when
the Linear Chuo Shinkansen connects Tokyo, Nagoya, and Osaka in approximately 1 hour, information exchanges
are expected to increase, and new joint research and development and innovation may be created among firms.

Resumen. Las teorías tradicionales sobre la aglomeración no pueden explicar la reducción continua de las
disparidades regionales en el Japón. Este estudio emplea un análisis de frontera estocástica para investigar el papel
de las redes interregionales en la mejora de la eficiencia productiva por medio del concepto de “tamaño prestado”.
La mejora de las redes interregionales es una fuerza motriz para el fortalecimiento de las economías locales y la
convergencia de las disparidades regionales. El tamaño prestado de la aglomeración de las grandes ciudades fue
muy efectivo en las áreas locales. El desarrollo de las redes de transporte ha permitido a las zonas locales ponerse
a la par de las grandes áreas metropolitanas. Los resultados sugieren que el desarrollo de las redes de transporte
de alta velocidad puede ayudar a reducir las disparidades regionales en la economía japonesa.



抄録: 伝統的な集積の理論では、日本の持続的な格差の縮小を説明するのは不可能である。本稿では、確率フロン
ティア分析により、生産効率の改善における地域間ネットワークの役割を「借用したサイズ」の概念を用いて検
討した。地域間ネットワークの改善は、地域経済を強化し、地域格差を収束させる原動力である。大都市の集積
から借用したサイズは地方では高い効果を示した。交通ネットワークの開発は、地方の地域が大都市地域に追い
つくことを可能にしている。結果から、高速交通ネットワークの開発が日本経済における地域間格差の縮小の一
助となることが示唆される。
