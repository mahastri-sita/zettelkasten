Borrowed size and agglomeration shadows in Japan:

Abstract
This study examined the influence of spatial interdependencies on regional total fac-
tor productivity in Japan by focusing on the contrasting effects of borrowed size and
agglomeration shadows. Using panel data from 47 Japanese prefectures between
2000 and 2018, we compared the manufacturing and non-manufacturing sectors
by estimating the Spatial Durbin Model and the Spatial Durbin–SARAR Model.
These models enable a detailed decomposition of productivity effects into direct
(within region) and indirect (spillover) components across space. The results indi-
cated that industrial agglomeration and transport capital positively influence local
productivity in both sectors. However, negative spillovers, interpreted as agglom-
eration shadow effects, were also observed, where the concentration in neighboring
regions suppressed local performance. Human capital exerted a consistently posi-
tive impact on manufacturing, with evidence of interregional spillover effects. These
findings highlight the sector-specific spatial dynamics and indicate the need for dif-
ferentiating regional policy responses. For the manufacturing sector, promoting net-
worked industrial clustering and labor mobility can enhance productivity but must
be balanced against the risks of overconcentration. In the non-manufacturing sector,
decentralized service economies and spatially equitable infrastructure investments
are critical for reducing competitive displacement from urban cores. By clarifying
the conditions under which borrowed size and agglomeration shadow effects prevail,
this study contributes to spatial productivity theory and offers actionable insights for
designing regionally differentiated development strategies.

1 Introduction

This study investigates the influence of interregional spillover effects on regional
total factor productivity (RTFP). Two contrasting spatial mechanisms mediate
these effects: borrowed size and agglomeration shadows.
   Borrowed size refers to the phenomenon in which a region experiences pro-
ductivity gains by leveraging the external influence of economic resources avail-
able in neighboring regions, such as industrial agglomeration, human capital, and
transport capital. These three elements are conceptualized as the core compo-
nents of borrowed size, and their spatial spillover effects on RTFP are empirically
examined using spatial econometric models.
   Rooted in the theory of external economies introduced by Alonso (1973, p.
200), Burger et al. (2015) further developed the concept of borrowed size. This
view is based on the premise that small cities can transcend their physical con-
straints and achieve growth by leveraging the economic benefits of nearby large
cities, including population size, critical market mass, and functional diversity.
This perspective suggests that agglomeration economies can transcend adminis-
trative boundaries into broader regional spaces (Parr 2002).
   This outlook provides a theoretical foundation for urban growth mechanisms
that do not rely on conventional factors such as population size or administra-
tive status. Several studies have shown that extensive interregional networks and
infrastructure can enhance functional specialization and increase productivity,
overcoming the limitations of geography (Camagni 1993; Capello 2000; Hall and
Pain 2006; Boix and Trullén 2007; Burger and Meijers 2016). Moreover, agglom-
eration economies have been shown to foster innovation and productivity growth,
particularly in urban settings, where dense interactions promote knowledge spill-
overs (Carlino and Kerr 2015).
   Advanced transportation infrastructure, such as high-speed rail networks and
airports, can stimulate interregional knowledge exchange, thereby promoting
knowledge diffusion and expanding the spatial scope of external economies (Ma
and Liu 2022; Tian et al. 2022). These high-quality networks enhance the speed
and frequency of knowledge flows, generating productivity gains for individual
economic agents and through positive spillovers to surrounding regions.
   However, interregional network formation does not always yield beneficial
outcomes. As Hesse (2014) argues, network-based urban growth may strengthen
the concentration of resources in large cities, creating a siphoning effect on the
periphery. Echoing this concern, Dijkstra et al. (2013) suggested that interur-
ban competition can widen growth disparities and undermine overall economic
efficiency.
   This dynamic was formalized in the concept of the agglomeration shadow
introduced by Fujita et al. (1999). This describes a situation in which large,
highly agglomerated urban centers absorb talent and resources from neighbor-
ing small- and medium-sized cities, thereby hindering their autonomous devel-
opment (Fujita and Thisse 2002). Although borrowed size and agglomeration
shadows seem to be opposing forces, they can operate either complementarily or
Asia-Pacific Journal of Regional Science (2025) 9:1115–1144                         1117


competitively, shaping the spatial dynamics of regional economies (Phelps and
Ozawa 2003; Meijers 2005; Burger and Meijers 2016).
   The simultaneous presence of benefits and constraints is a common phenomenon
in real-world regional development, where proximity to large cities either promotes
or inhibits local economic activity. This duality reflects the distinction between static
and dynamic agglomeration economies, where immediate productivity gains may
be accompanied by long-term structural disparities, depending on the spatial con-
text (Camagni et al. 2015). When borrowed size effects are strong, they may offset
the negative impacts of agglomeration shadows and support autonomous regional
growth. Conversely, when agglomeration shadows dominate, they may restrain the
borrowed size effects and exacerbate regional disparities.
   However, this dual mechanism has gained scholarly attention, as empirical
research on the simultaneous operation of borrowed size and agglomeration shadow
effects remains limited. Most existing studies tend to analyze these mechanisms in
isolation, often focusing on either positive externalities from urban networks or neg-
ative spillovers from concentrated agglomerations. This fragmented approach has
hampered the comprehensive understanding of how these forces interact in space.
   Despite the increasing interest in these concepts, the operationalization of bor-
rowed size remains inconsistent and ambiguous. Recent empirical studies explored
how spatial externalities and structural embeddedness shape regional economic out-
comes in the context of urbanization and systemic shocks. Crescenzi et al. (2022)
emphasized the role of territorial embeddedness in fostering local development,
showing how region-specific assets can amplify or mediate external effects. Sim-
ilarly, Capello and Caragliu (2023) examined the economic impact of COVID-19
in cross-border regions, revealing the uneven spatial vulnerability of regions and
the importance of structural conditions in regional resilience. Guo (2025) offered
a novel perspective on urban productivity by investigating the relationship between
urban wage premiums and spatial equilibrium, contributing to a broader understand-
ing of scale effects and agglomeration benefits.
   These recent contributions reinforce the relevance of analyzing positive and nega-
tive spatial spillovers in productivity, particularly through frameworks that capture
complex spatial interdependencies, such as those employed in this study. Some
studies equate this with functional connectivity or urban performance (Meijers and
Burger 2015; Meijers et al. 2015; Volgmann and Rusche 2020), whereas others
emphasize specific spillover channels, such as commuting patterns or infrastructure
networks (Partridge et al. 2009; Hesse 2014). These analyses frequently adopt sin-
gle-dimensional frameworks and are largely centered on European urban systems.
Conversely, empirical evidence from non-Western, mature regional systems, such as
Japan, is limited despite rich regional heterogeneity and established regional hierar-
chies. As Hornbeck et al. (2024) noted, the conditions under which borrowed size
dominates agglomeration shadows remain largely unresolved.
   This study addresses these gaps by employing spatial econometric models to
identify the pathways through which industrial specialization, human capital, and
transport capital mediate spatial spillovers. By jointly estimating the borrowed
size and agglomeration shadow effects, this analysis offers a multidimensional
1118                                   Asia-Pacific Journal of Regional Science (2025) 9:1115–1144


and contextually grounded contribution to our understanding of regional produc-
tivity dynamics in Japan.
   Recent studies in regional science have clarified the roles of these three pillars
in shaping local productivity. Regarding industrial specialization, de Souza Silva
et al. (2024) analyzed the links between occupational specialization and wage
premiums in Brazil, highlighting the productivity implications of skill matching
within urban labor markets. Leitão et al. (2024) revisited the structure of Mar-
shallian districts in Portugal, offering new methodological insights into the spa-
tial organization of industries.
   Regarding human capital, a growing body of literature emphasizes cross-
border spillovers and place-specific dynamics. For instance, Jardon et al. (2024)
showed how human capital accumulation interacts with administrative bounda-
ries to shape regional performance, whereas Biedka et al. (2022) provided evi-
dence from Poland regarding the impact of EU cohesion policy investments on
local human capital and development outcomes. Raya and Resosudarmo (2024)
further highlighted the institutional roots of human capital disparities, illustrating
how historical legacies continue to shape regional capacity building.
   Finally, recent research on transport capital focused on investment volumes
and spatial impact asymmetries. Arachovas et al. (2025) examined the timing and
spatial distribution of public capital spikes in Greece, underscoring the uneven
benefits of infrastructure across regions. Similarly, Centurião et al. (2024) evalu-
ated road infrastructure investments along the Latin American Integration Route,
highlighting differentiated regional gains depending on the network position and
accessibility profiles.
   These recent contributions strengthen the conceptual underpinnings of this
analysis and provide a broader empirical backdrop for understanding the struc-
tural sources of RTFP variation.
   This study makes a threefold contribution to the existing literature.
   First, while previous studies examined borrowed size or agglomeration shad-
ows individually, this study is among the first to jointly identify and quantify both
effects within a unified spatial econometric framework. By applying the Spatial
Durbin Model (SDM) and the Spatial Durbin–SARAR Model (SDM-SARAR),
the analysis disentangled the positive and negative spatial spillovers associated
with industrial specialization, human capital, and transport capital, enabling
the structural identification of borrowed size and agglomeration shadow effects
across space.
   Second, unlike studies that operationalize borrowed size through regional con-
nectivity or functional centrality (e.g., Meijers and Burger 2015; Meijers et al. 2015;
Volgmann and Rusche 2020), this study decomposes spatial externalities into three
distinct channels—industrial specialization, human capital, and transport capital—
to offer a granular understanding of spillover pathways.
   Third, existing empirical literature focuses primarily on European or North
American regional systems, whereas this study provides novel evidence from Japan,
a non-Western context characterized by a high urbanization rate and polycentric
regional structure. The applicability and limitations of established spatial economic
theories in different institutional and geographical settings were critically assessed.

2.2 Strategy for model selection

To account for spatial economic interdependence, this study adopted the SDM and
SDM-SARAR models. These models are suitable for analyzing regional produc-
tivity patterns where observable characteristics and unobserved spatial factors are
likely to generate interregional spillovers.
   Traditional spatial econometric approaches, such as the Spatial Autoregressive
Model (SAR), which introduces a spatial lag of the dependent variable, and the
Spatial Error Model (SEM), which incorporates spatial autocorrelation in the error
term, have been widely used. However, each model captures only one aspect of spa-
tial dependence and thus has limitations.
   The SAR model is useful for modeling situations in which the RTFP in neighbor-
ing regions directly affects the local RTFP. However, it does not account for spatial
spillovers that operate through explanatory variables, such as the influence of neigh-
boring regions’ human capital or industrial structures. Conversely, the SEM model
addresses spatially correlated unobserved factors but does not capture the impact of
the observed variables in neighboring regions.
   The SDM overcomes these limitations by including the spatial lags of both
dependent and explanatory variables. This structure enables simultaneous estima-
tion of the direct (within-region) and indirect (between-region) effects of industrial
specialization (LQ), human capital (HC), and transport capital (TC) on regional
Asia-Pacific Journal of Regional Science (2025) 9:1115–1144                         1121


productivity. Moreover, by encompassing spatially lagged regressors, the SDM
nests both SAR and SEM models as special cases under specific parameter restric-
tions, positioning it as a generalized and robust spatial model (Anselin 1988; Elhorst
2014).
   While the SDM can address the observed spatial heterogeneity, it does not fully
capture the latent sources of spatial dependence. The SDM-SARAR model extends
the SDM by introducing spatial autocorrelation in the error term via a spatial autore-
gressive parameter (λ). This addition enables the model to account for unobserved
spatial dependence arising from the institutional context, cultural characteristics, or
omitted geographic factors. By modeling observable and unobservable sources of
spatial dependence, SDM-SARAR enhances identification and improves the consist-
ency of parameter estimation.
   Overall, the SDM and SDM-SARAR models offer the following methodological
advantages over conventional spatial econometric approaches: (1) they support the
explicit modeling of spatial spillover effects; (2) they jointly consider both observ-
able and latent spatial dependence; and (3) they are general and inclusive frame-
works that nest SAR and SEM models. Given these strengths, they are well suited
for capturing the nuanced spatial interdependencies that shape regional productivity.


2.3 Estimation models

1. Spatial Durbin Model (SDM)

   The SDM enables the explicit estimation of spatial spillover effects by incorpo-
rating the spatial lags of dependent and independent variables. This feature is use-
ful for analyzing regional productivity, which may be influenced by local conditions
and neighboring characteristics. The model is expressed as follows:

where Y k is a vector[ of the RTFP   ] for industry k (manufacturing or non-manufac-
turing), and X k = LQk , HC, TC is the matrix of explanatory variables, including
location quotient (LQ), human capital (HC), and transport capital (TC). W denotes
the spatial weight matrix; ρ is the spatial autoregressive coefficient; β captures
the impact of local explanatory variables; θ represents the effects of neighboring
regions’ explanatory variables (that is, spillover effects); μ is a vector of region-spe-
cific fixed effects; and ε is the error term.
   The coefficients in θ reflect the impact of “borrowed size” from the neighboring
regions. A positive value (θ > 0) indicates positive spillovers, namely, that external
resources concentrated in nearby areas enhance local productivity. Conversely, a
negative value (θ < 0) implies that economic resources are drawn away toward neigh-
boring regions, resulting in a reduction in local productivity due to an “agglomera-
tion shadow.”

   The SDM nests the SAR and SEM as special cases under specific parameter
constraints (Anselin 1988), making it a general and flexible framework for cap-
turing different types of spatial dependence (Elhorst 2014).

2. Spatial Durbin–SARAR model (SDM-SARAR)

   The SDM-SARAR Model extends the SDM by incorporating spatial autocor-
relation into the error term. Assuming that the error follows a spatial autoregres-
sive process, the model is specified as follows:

where λ is the spatial autoregressive coefficient in the error term, capturing unob-
served spatial dependence arising from institutional settings, cultural background,
or unmeasured geographic features. Thus, the SDM-SARAR allows for a richer rep-
resentation of spatial dependence by modeling observable spillovers (via WX) and
latent dependence (via λWu).
    As noted by Elhorst (2010), the SDM-SARAR model may be subject to identi-
fication issues under specific conditions. However, this study addresses these con-
cerns by employing the full Durbin specification (that is, applying spatial lags to
all explanatory variables) and using a panel dataset with sufficient spatiotemporal
variation to ensure a stable estimation. The empirical performance of the model
is further examined in the results section.
    The SDM and SDM-SARAR models were estimated by applying maximum-
likelihood (ML) methods based on prefecture-level panel data with regional fixed
effects. ML has been widely adopted in spatial econometrics (Ord 1975; Haining
1990, 2003), and the balanced panel used in this study provides adequate obser-
vations for consistent estimation.
    These models incorporate three common sources of spatial correlation: spatial
lag of the dependent variable (WY), spatial lag of explanatory variables (WX),
and spatial autocorrelation in the error term (λWu). Each of these sources can
introduce endogeneity. Specifically, a spatial lag of the dependent variable may
result in simultaneity bias. However, the ML estimator was designed to account
for this endogeneity, and yielded consistent estimates.
    Moreover, the Durbin specification facilitates the identification of spatial spill-
over effects through WX, whereas the SARAR structure helps control for spa-
tially correlated omitted variables. This combination enhanced the credibility of
the estimated relationships between regional productivity and spatial drivers.
    In summary, estimating the SDM and SDM-SARAR using ML provides a the-
oretically grounded and empirically robust approach for evaluating spatial exter-
nalities. These models enable a statistically reliable analysis of how borrowed
size mechanisms, industrial agglomeration, human capital, and transport infra-
structure affect regional productivity across Japanese prefectures.

3. Impact evaluation

   In spatial econometric models with lag structures, changes in an explanatory vari-
able in one region can affect that region as well as other regions through spatial link-
ages. This feature introduces complexity in interpreting model coefficients, as the
estimated parameters (such as β and θ) do not directly represent the full magnitude
of influence across space.
   To address this problem, LeSage and Pace (2009) proposed decomposing the
estimated effects into three components: direct, indirect, and total. Currently,
this approach is the standard for analyzing spatial models, such as SDM and
SDM-SARAR.
   The direct effect refers to the average impact of a change in a region’s explanatory
variable on its dependent variable (RTFP). This reflects localized mechanisms, such
as agglomeration economies and internal policy interventions.
   The indirect effect (that is, the spatial spillover effect) measures the average influ-
ence of changes in explanatory variables in neighboring regions on the RTFP of a
given region. This component captures the interregional externalities that are central
to evaluating the balance between positive and negative agglomeration shadows.
   The total effect is the sum of the direct and indirect effects and represents the
overall spatial impact of a given variable.
   In the general SDM-SARAR framework, these effects are computed numerically
using the following impact matrix:

where 𝛽X and 𝜃X are the coefficients associated with the local and spatially lagged
explanatory variables, respectively. The average of the diagonal elements represents
the direct effect, the average of the off-diagonal elements represents the indirect
effect, and the overall mean represents the total effect.
   For instance, if all three effects are positive, this suggests that the variable in
question, such as human capital or transport capital, not only improves local produc-
tivity but also generates positive spillovers to other regions. These findings support
the presence of borrowed size effects through interregional connectivity.
   Conversely, if a variable exhibits a significantly negative indirect effect, it indi-
cates the presence of an agglomerated shadow. In this case, strong concentrations
of industrial specialization (LQ), human capital (HC), or transport capital (TC) in
neighboring areas drain productive resources, thereby reducing RTFP in adjacent
regions despite local efforts to improve productivity.
   This impact decomposition provides a rigorous analytical basis for understand-
ing the role of uneven spatial distribution of economic resources in shaping regional
development outcomes. By distinguishing between local and spillover effects, this
analysis highlights the dual role of agglomeration: it can either facilitate or constrain
regional growth, depending on how benefits and burdens are spatially distributed.
   Thus, the SDM-SARAR model is a powerful tool for quantifying these dynam-
ics and enables a comprehensive empirical investigation of the spatial propagation
mechanisms that drive regional productivity in Japan.

2.4 Spatial weight matrix

This study constructed a binary queen-type contiguity matrix to represent the geo-
graphical proximity between Japan’s prefectures (Anselin 1988). In this matrix, two
prefectures are considered neighbors if they share either a common border or a sin-
gle point of contact. The matrix is square and symmetric, with a value of 1 assigned
to adjacent pairs and 0 otherwise. The diagonal elements are set to zero, indicating
that a prefecture is not its own neighbor.
   Compared to the rook-type matrix, which only includes edge-sharing units, the
queen-type matrix incorporates corner contiguity, providing an inclusive representa-
tion of spatial connectivity. This is particularly relevant for Japan, where prefectural
boundaries are often irregular and fragmented. The queen approach better reflects
real-world spatial interactions, including broad-area effects such as resource alloca-
tion pressures and spillover mechanisms, which are the central themes of this study.
   A specific adjustment was applied to Okinawa Prefecture, which is geographi-
cally isolated and does not share direct borders with the other prefectures. To avoid
numerical issues caused by zero-row isolation, Okinawa is considered adjacent to
Kagoshima Prefecture, which is its geographically nearest mainland counterpart.
This procedure follows established practices in applied spatial econometrics (LeS-
age and Pace 2009; Elhorst 2014), in which spatial islands are connected to the clos-
est unit to ensure full participation in spatial structures.
   The adjacency matrix was subsequently row-standardized to form the spa-
tial weight matrix used in the estimation models (LeSage and Pace 2009). In this
process, each non-zero element is divided by the number of neighbors for the cor-
responding row unit, such that all row sums are equal to one. This normalization
allows for comparability across regions and prevents regions with more neighbors
from exerting a disproportionately large influence.


2.5 Data

This study analyzes the determinants of regional productivity in the manufactur-
ing and non-manufacturing sectors using panel data for all 47 prefectures in Japan
between 2000 and 2018. All data sources are publicly available and based on official
national statistics.
   The dependent variable, RTFP, was calculated using the Regional-Level Japan
Industrial Productivity Database (R-JIP) published by the Research Institute of
Economy, Trade, and Industry (RIETI). This database was developed to overcome
the limitations of regional and industry-consistent data availability in Japan. In this
study, prefecture- and industry-level RTFP were estimated under consistent defini-
tions for the full sample period.
   The analysis period ended in 2018, which is the latest year available in the R-JIP
database. Other key explanatory variables, including public capital stock, were
obtained from official Cabinet Office data, with the most recent available year being
2020. These limitations stem from the deliberate emphasis on research transparency
Asia-Pacific Journal of Regional Science (2025) 9:1115–1144                      1125


and reproducibility using reliable open datasets. Therefore, the absence of post-
COVID observations reflects current institutional constraints rather than a lack of
theoretical or policy relevance.
   In the RTFP calculation, output (Y) was measured as the real value added. Input
(X) comprises labor and capital, with labor measured in hours worked and capital as
the real capital stock. Cost shares (S) were derived from labor and capital cost data
included in the R-JIP.
   The LQ was constructed using real value added by industry and prefecture and
was sourced from the R-JIP. HC was calculated using employment and wage data
disaggregated by educational attainment from the Basic Survey on Employment
Structure (Statistics Bureau of Japan) and the Basic Survey on Wage Structure
(Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare). TC, which serves as a proxy for infra-
structure investment, is based on the Cabinet Office’s Statistics on Social Capital
Stock. Table 1 summarizes the descriptive statistics for all the variables.
   A comparison of the mean RTFP values indicates that manufacturing sectors
exhibit higher productivity than non-manufacturing sectors, indicating pronounced
technological advancement and efficiency gains. The standard deviation is also
greater for manufacturing, reflecting stronger regional disparities.
   The average LQ in manufacturing slightly exceeded 1.0, indicating regional
concentration, whereas the non-manufacturing LQ was closer to 1.0, suggesting a
uniform distribution across regions. The standard deviation of the LQ was notably
higher in the manufacturing sector, reinforcing the presence of spatially clustered
industrial activities.
   The HC index showed relatively stable averages but substantial variation across
regions. The gap between the maximum and minimum values reflects a spa-
tial imbalance in educational attainment, with potential implications for local
productivity.
   TC, which represents infrastructure investment, exhibited a moderate upward
trend in its mean value. However, a high standard deviation suggests uneven infra-
structure development, with some regions benefiting from substantial transport
investments.
   Overall, the descriptive statistics reveal sectoral contrasts in productivity and
significant regional imbalances in economic structure and resource allocation. The
combination of high variance in RTFP and LQ in manufacturing underscores the

3.1 Spatial autocorrelation

A preliminary step in spatial econometric analysis is to assess the presence of spatial
autocorrelation in the dependent variable, RTFP. This assessment clarifies whether
productivity levels exhibit geographic clustering, which suggests spatial spillovers,
concentration effects, or random distributions.
   This study employed Moran’s I (Moran 1950) as a measure of spatial autocorrela-
tion. Moran’s I is a spatial analog of Pearson’s correlation coefficient and is used to
measure the similarity of values among neighboring regions. This measure is par-
ticularly useful, because it is sensitive to spatial lags in the dependent variable, spa-
tial autocorrelation in the error term, and heteroscedasticity (Anselin and Griffith
1988; Anselin and Rey 1991; Florax and de Graaff 2004).
   A positive Moran’s I value indicates that neighboring regions tend to exhibit sim-
ilar levels of industrial activity, implying spatial clustering. Conversely, a negative

regional productivity dynamics. Section 4 applies spatial econometric models to

3.2 Testing for region‑specific effects

Accounting for unobserved region-specific heterogeneity is vital for spatial
econometric analysis at the prefectural level. Time-invariant characteristics, such
as geographic conditions, industrial composition, labor market structures, and
local policy environments, may systematically influence RTFP. If such heteroge-
neity is ignored, the model estimates may suffer from omitted variable biases and
inconsistencies.
   To address this issue, this study evaluates whether fixed effects (FE) or random
effects (RE) should be adopted in a panel data framework. Specifically, an F test
for individual effects and the Hausman test for model consistency were conducted
to guide the specification choice. These tests help determine whether unobserved
regional differences should be treated as correlated with explanatory variables
(FE) or as uncorrelated (RE), thereby improving estimation validity. The results
are summarized in Table 3.
   In the manufacturing and non-manufacturing sectors, F-statistics are highly
significant, rejecting the null hypothesis of homogeneous regional effects. This
confirms the presence of non-negligible cross-sectional heterogeneity and sup-
ports the need for region-specific effects.
   The Hausman test results confirmed this conclusion. In both sectors, the test
statistics are sufficiently large to reject the null hypothesis that the RE model pro-
vides consistent estimates. This outcome indicates that unobserved regional char-
acteristics are likely to correlate with the explanatory variables, thereby validat-
ing the use of the FE model.
   Overall, the test results strongly favor the fixed-effects specification. Therefore,
Eqs. (4) and (5) are estimated under the fixed-effects framework in the spatial
econometric analysis presented in Sect. 4.

agglomeration shadow mechanism. The nature and strength of the borrowed size
effects vary across sectors, with adverse spatial spillovers observed in the non-man-
ufacturing sector. The negative spatial spillovers from transport capital highlight the
need for careful regional coordination of infrastructure investment policies.
    Although the SDM-SARAR model is theoretically flexible, it raises potential
identification concerns. Particularly, when spatially lagged explanatory variables
and spatial error terms are constructed using the same spatial weight matrix, it can
be difficult to distinguish their impacts because of possible multicollinearity (Elhorst
2010).
    To mitigate this risk, this study estimated the full SDM-SARAR specification
using the sacsarlm() function in the spatialreg package in R by applying a maxi-
mum-likelihood estimation. Durbin terms were included for all explanatory vari-
ables, and the panel data structure ensured sufficient information richness across the
spatial and temporal dimensions. The estimation was numerically stable for manu-
facturing and non-manufacturing models, and most of the key parameters were sta-
tistically significant.
    These results suggest that appropriate model specifications, the use of rich spatial
panel data, and the adoption of a Queen-type contiguity matrix contribute to stable
estimation performance. Despite theoretical concerns regarding collinearity, empiri-
cal evidence supports the validity of the SDM-SARAR model in this context.
    In conclusion, this specification successfully captures complex spatial interac-
tions, including observable spillovers and unobserved spatial dependencies, without
encountering convergence or instability. These findings underscore the practical util-
ity of the model in analyzing regional productivity under spatial interdependence.

    Regarding indirect effects, industrial agglomeration exerts a significant negative
influence, indicating the presence of an agglomeration shadow—a dynamic in which
neighboring industrial hubs siphon away labor, capital, and technological inputs,
thereby suppressing local productivity. In contrast, human capital demonstrates a
significant positive spillover, implying the existence of interregional labor market
integration and knowledge diffusion. The indirect effect of transport capital is nega-
tive and significant, implying that infrastructure in neighboring regions may inten-
sify competition for mobile inputs or markets.
    In terms of total effects, agglomeration and human capital contribute positively
overall. However, the negative spatial spillover from industrial agglomeration is par-
ticularly pronounced, underscoring the importance of accounting for external pres-
sure when formulating regional industrial strategies.
    Next, we consider the non-manufacturing sector. Direct effects show that indus-
trial agglomeration has a positive but weaker impact than in manufacturing, imply-
ing that while clustering supports productivity, it does not produce the same scale of
technological spillovers. Human capital has an insignificant direct effect, suggesting
that labor quality is less central in non-skill-intensive activities. In contrast, TC has a
strong and direct effect, signifying that infrastructure development facilitates access
to services and consumer markets in this sector.
    As for the indirect effects, industrial agglomeration exhibits a negative effect,
consistent with agglomeration shadows attributable to consumer and labor migra-
tion to urban centers. The indirect effect of human capital is positive but not statisti-
cally significant, whereas transport capital shows a significantly negative spillover,
indicating that investment in core areas may draw economic activity away from the
surrounding regions.
1138                                    Asia-Pacific Journal of Regional Science (2025) 9:1115–1144


   Regarding the total effects, industrial agglomeration and TC contribute positively
to the non-manufacturing sector; however, the role of human capital remains mar-
ginal. The agglomeration shadow appears to be more binding in the non-manufac-
turing sector, where local compensating mechanisms, such as endogenous knowl-
edge accumulation, are weaker and exposure to interregional competition is greater.
   In summary, the decomposition of the spatial effects revealed sector-specific spa-
tial dynamics. In manufacturing, endogenous growth drivers and positive spillovers
from skilled labor strengthen resilience to spatial competition. In contrast, non-man-
ufacturing sectors appear vulnerable to negative spillovers, as productivity depends
on internal demand and physical infrastructure rather than on localized innovation
or skill accumulation.


4.4 Policy implications

The findings suggest that effective spatial policies should be sector-specific and
spatially adaptive. Given the observed asymmetries in spillover effects between the
manufacturing and non-manufacturing sectors, a uniform approach is unlikely to
achieve balanced regional development. Instead, different strategies tailored to the
spatial dynamics of each sector are required.
   The direct positive effects of agglomeration and human capital in the manufac-
turing sector imply that regional productivity can be enhanced by reinforcing local
clustering and skill accumulation. This underscores the need for industrial policies
that foster geographical collaboration and innovation. Simultaneously, negative
spillovers from neighboring industrial hubs and evidence of agglomeration shadow
effects highlight the risk of overconcentration. Therefore, policies should aim to pro-
mote regionally distributed cluster networks rather than singular industrial poles.
   The agglomeration shadow effects identified in this study are likely to reflect spe-
cific structural mechanisms in Japan. For example, intensified interregional competi-
tion for skilled labor may drain talent from peripheral areas, whereas market siphon-
ing effects concentrate demand in urban centers at the expense of nearby regions.
Additionally, technological dependence on core regions may inhibit the development
of independent innovation capabilities in less-developed prefectures. These mecha-
nisms suggest that shadow effects are not merely statistical artifacts but manifesta-
tions of unequal regional interdependencies that merit targeted policy interventions.
   To support this, regional growth strategies should include investments in mid-
sized regional cities, which can function as secondary cores and help retain human
capital. Educational and labor policies must also emphasize interregional mobility
and integration, building broader functional labor markets that facilitate efficient tal-
ent matching across space.
   Regarding transport infrastructure, the finding of negative spatial spillovers in
manufacturing suggests that mere expansion may unintentionally weaken peripheral
productivity. Thus, infrastructure planning should prioritize connectivity and com-
plementarities over scale, with an emphasis on regional accessibility, multimodal
integration, and the minimization of resource diversion.
Asia-Pacific Journal of Regional Science (2025) 9:1115–1144                         1139


   In the non-manufacturing sector, policy challenges are acute. Spatial competition
is pronounced, and the benefits of local agglomeration are weaker. The results high-
light the need for policies that decentralize economic activity and strengthen place-
based service economies such as tourism, culture, and local entrepreneurship.
   To reduce dependence on metropolitan centers, peripheral regions should be sup-
ported in developing locally rooted economic identities bolstered by improvements
in institutional capacity, quality of life, and public services. As human capital has a
limited impact on this sector, higher returns can be achieved through strategic infra-
structure investment, market expansion policies, and demand-side interventions,
such as boosting local consumption through income stabilization.
   Moreover, the potentially adverse impacts of metropolitan infrastructure on con-
tiguous areas underscore the importance of evaluating the unintended spatial conse-
quences of major public investments. Transport policies should be designed with a
regionally differentiated lens to prevent the displacement of economic activity and
strengthen economic resilience in non-core regions.
   These implications reinforce the need for a spatially nuanced, sectorally targeted,
and equity-conscious policy framework. Balancing growth with interregional fair-
ness requires an integrated perspective that recognizes the opportunities and con-
straints created by spatial interdependence.



5 Conclusion

This study employed spatial econometric models to examine the influence of geo-
graphic and interregional factors on RTFP, focusing on the differences between the
manufacturing and non-manufacturing sectors. Using panel data for 47 Japanese pre-
fectures, the RTFP was calculated based on consistent measures of output and input
and analyzed with respect to industrial specialization, human capital, and transport
capital. By applying an SDM with spatial error components (SDM-SARAR), this
study identified direct (local) and indirect (spillover) effects that are typically over-
looked by conventional models.
   A key insight from this analysis is that spatial interdependencies manifest dif-
ferently across sectors and shape productivity outcomes through distinct spillover
mechanisms. Industrial agglomeration exerts a positive effect on RTFP in both sec-
tors, while negative spillovers from neighboring regions, interpreted as agglomera-
tion shadow effects, are also statistically significant. Broadly, human capital contrib-
utes positively to productivity, particularly in the manufacturing sector. In contrast,
the effect of transport capital is modest and occasionally negative, especially through
spatial spillovers.
   From theoretical and methodological perspectives, this study contributes to
the literature by operationalizing the concepts of borrowed size and agglomera-
tion shadow within a unified econometric framework. The SDM-SARAR model
enables the simultaneous estimation of spatial spillovers and latent interregional
dependencies, offering a comprehensive overview of how localized and external
forces jointly shape regional productivity. This approach also advances empirical
1140                                  Asia-Pacific Journal of Regional Science (2025) 9:1115–1144


spatial analysis by incorporating sectoral distinctions into the assessment of spa-
tial externalities.
    Several directions for future research must be explored. First, the mechanisms
underlying spatial spillovers warrant further investigation. Although the SDM-
SARAR model improves estimation by capturing spatial error dependence, future
studies should consider identifying channels of interregional influence, namely,
knowledge diffusion, labor mobility, and institutional interactions. Specifically,
the conditions under which agglomeration shadows emerge, such as interre-
gional competition for skilled labor or asymmetric innovation capacity, should be
explored in detail.
    Second, temporal dynamics should be incorporated. As the impacts of agglom-
eration, human capital, and infrastructure may evolve over time, dynamic spatial
models are required to capture lagged adjustments and intertemporal spillover
effects. Such extensions would offer a complete picture of regional development
trajectories.
    Third, a simulation-based policy analysis can enhance the practical relevance
of spatial econometric findings. By modeling the potential effects of changes in
industrial structure, educational attainment, or infrastructure investment, future
studies could provide quantitative policy scenarios to inform spatial planning and
regional development strategies.
    While the present study provides robust empirical evidence of agglomeration
shadow effects across Japanese prefectures, specific transmission mechanisms,
such as labor competition, market centralization, and technological dependence,
cannot be fully identified within the constraints of a macro-level spatial econo-
metric framework. Addressing these channels requires complementary research
designs, including case studies and qualitative institutional analyses, which fall
outside the scope of this study. Nevertheless, the findings presented here lay
an empirical foundation for future investigations by highlighting where and for
which sectors shadow effects are most pronounced.
    Despite the context-specific nature of the Japanese case, future studies should
examine whether these findings can be generalized to other national or subna-
tional settings, especially those with different institutional structures or spatial
hierarchies, to assess the broader relevance of the borrowed size and agglomera-
tion shadow frameworks proposed in this study.
    Although the current analysis was limited to the pre-COVID-19 period due
to data availability, the observed spatial patterns were consistent across multiple
model specifications and weighting schemes. This stability suggests that the iden-
tified mechanisms, borrowed size and agglomeration shadow, are likely to hold
beyond the study period.
    Future studies should extend this framework to the post-2018 period, particu-
larly to explore how regional productivity responded to the disruptions and recov-
ery trajectories of the COVID-19 pandemic. Such extensions will provide deeper
insights into the resilience and adaptability of Japan’s regional economies under
unprecedented conditions.
