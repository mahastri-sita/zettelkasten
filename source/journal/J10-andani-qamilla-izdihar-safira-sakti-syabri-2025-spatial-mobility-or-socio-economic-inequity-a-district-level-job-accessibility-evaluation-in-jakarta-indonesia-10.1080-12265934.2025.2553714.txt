1. Introduction
The increasing rate of urbanization has brought significant mobility challenges, par­
ticularly concerning how urban populations travel, the available transportation oppor­
tunities, and its accessibility level. Hansen (1959) initially defined accessibility as the
potential for opportunities to interact. Subsequently, accessibility has been defined
in a variety of ways. For instance, Dalvi and Martin (1976) defined accessibility as
the convenience with which any activity (land use) can be accessed using the existing
transportation system. Access to activities is the primary goal of transportation,
influenced by factors such as individual mobility, the quality of available transpor­
tation modes, transportation network connectivity, as well as population density
and land use variation (Litman, 2013).
    Among the diverse indicators of accessibility, job accessibility emerges as a central
focus due to its critical role in socio-economic outcomes. Jobs are a fundamental
driver of economic mobility and social inclusion. Access to employment opportunities
directly affects individuals’ economic well-being, making job accessibility a key indicator
for understanding spatial and socio-economic inequalities (Guzman et al., 2017; Lucas,
2012). Although many studies on transportation equity evaluate access to healthcare
or education (e.g. Lardier et al., 2023; Sharma & Patil, 2021; Tiznado-Aitken et al.,
2021), job accessibility is frequently used due to its direct impact on economic outcomes
and daily commuting needs (Bastiaanssen et al., 2021; Lucas et al., 2016; Martens, 2016).
Unlike healthcare, which may be needed less frequently, jobs require consistent and
reliable access, making employment-related trips a critical factor in transportation
planning.
    Numerous studies have demonstrated the uneven distribution of job accessibility in
many large cities worldwide. For instance, Dodson et al. (2011) in several Australian
cities, Teunissen et al. (2015) in Bogota, Hernandez (2018) in Montevideo, Kawabata
(2003) in Tokyo, and (Kawabata & Shen, 2007) in the US metropolitan areas. This
unequal job accessibility and its association with limited opportunities in certain areas
and social interaction exacerbate pre-existing socio-economic inequalities (Blanco &
Apaolaza, 2018; Golub & Martens, 2014). Identifying the most affected or marginalized
groups and areas is crucial to evaluating the role of transportation systems in promoting
equity (Lucas et al., 2016). Recent transportation studies have shown interest in measur­
ing differences in job accessibility and its impact on socially marginalized groups (see for
example, Boisjoly et al., 2017; Grisé et al., 2019; Liu & Kwan, 2020).
    Despite this growing body of work, limited research focuses on the Global South – par­
ticularly Southeast Asia – where transport systems are characterized by mixed modal pat­
terns and the dominance of informal and motorcycle-based mobility. Moreover, few
studies have applied multidimensional equity indicators to capture both modal and
socio-economic disparities in job accessibility. In Asian countries, such as Indonesia,
evaluations of job accessibility are not widely conducted or able to explore how
                                                 INTERNATIONAL JOURNAL OF URBAN SCIENCES       3


accessibility is distributed across socio-economic strata and transport modes. The study
by Andani et al. (2021) on accessibility to employment in Indonesia in the context of eval­
uating toll road construction, for example, does not accommodate the aspect of mode
choice. In fact, the characteristics of mobility in Asian countries are very unique and
different from countries on other continents. For example, the use of (semi) automatic
motorbikes with low engine capacity (below 150 cc) is very widespread in Indonesia
and other developing countries such as Vietnam and Thailand (Hsu et al., 2003). This
is in contrast to motorbike users in Europe and the United States. Ride-hailing services
with motorbikes are also more popular in Southeast Asia, and have been found to
improve mobility in urban communities (Irawan et al., 2020; Phun et al., 2019; Wadud,
2020). Ride-hailing services provide a more flexible alternative for low-income commu­
nities that rely heavily on public transport (Qiao & Gar-On Yeh, 2023).
    The government of Indonesia has prioritized the development of mass public transport
in the past years as part of their strategy to boost mobility and accessibility. As of the end of
2019, types of mass public transport such as Bus Rapid Transit, MRT, and LRT were oper­
ational in Jakarta. It is said that transportation services in addition to what existed before
were able to service about 90 percent of the people. However, there has been no analysis of
how the expansion of these services has affected access to employment or reduced socio-
economic inequities in mobility. This study seeks to address that gap by evaluating inequi­
ties in job accessibility across socio-economic groups and transport modes in Jakarta.
    The unequal distribution of resources or opportunities among population groups or
places, such as public transport accessibility between central and periphery districts, is
called inequality in transport (Arellana et al., 2021). Inequity emphasizes fairness and
justice beyond inequality (Litman, 2015). Systemic limitations to transportation services
can prevent disadvantaged populations from participating in critical activities like job, edu­
cation, and healthcare. While inequality can be quantified by statistical measures like the
Gini coefficient, inequity necessitates assessing whether observed discrepancies are fair or
unfair based on ethical and socio-economic factors (van Wee & Geurs, 2011). This study
investigates inequities in job accessibility to identify gaps and offer recommendations that
promote fairness in mobility and opportunity distribution across districts and social groups.
    This research provides critical empirical evidence from Jakarta, a highly relevant case in
the Global South, using equity metrics rarely applied in the region. It examines job acces­
sibility inequity through the combined lens of spatial, socio-economic, and modal dispar­
ities in a city where motorcycles dominate mobility and public transport systems are still
emerging. While previous studies have focused on transit affordability or infrastructure dis­
tribution in other cities, this paper uses the Gini coefficient, Palma ratio, and Theil index to
evaluate job accessibility from a multidimensional perspective. By doing so, the study pro­
vides one of the first empirical analyses of accessibility inequity in Indonesia that considers
the interplay of income levels, geographic location, and mode of transport. It responds to
recent calls in the literature for more nuanced and context-sensitive assessments of trans­
port-related inequality (Lucas, 2019; Pereira et al., 2016; Uteng & Lucas, 2017).
    This paper is organized as follows: Section 2 presents a literature review focused on
transportation equity and job accessibility, highlighting essential metrics employed in
the assessment of inequities. Section 3 outlines the research methodology, including
data collection, travel time analysis, and the computation of accessibility and inequity
indicators. Section 4 presents the results and includes a comprehensive discussion that

compares the findings with existing literature. Section 5 concludes the paper by summar­
izing key insights and providing policy recommendations aimed at enhancing equitable
job access in Jakarta.

2. Literature review
To examine inequity in urban transport systems, this study is guided by a conceptual
framework that integrates three key dimensions of transport-related disparities: spatial
inequity, socio-economic inequity, and mobility inequity (Figure 1). These dimensions
represent distinct but interconnected pathways through which access to employment
is shaped by geographic location, income status, and modal availability. All three
influence individuals’ ability to reach job opportunities, conceptualized in this study
as job accessibility. This framework also links each dimension of inequity with suitable
distributional equity indicators: the Gini coefficient and Lorenz curve for overall
inequality, the Palma ratio for disparities between extremes, and the Theil index for
analysing within- and between-group differences. This multi-metric approach
enables a nuanced evaluation of transport-related exclusion in Jakarta. Each com­
ponent of the framework is discussed in detail in the following subsections.

2.1. Understanding transport inequity and its dimensions
Transport inequity refers to the unfair or unjust distribution of transport services, infra­
structure, and the opportunities they enable. It differs from inequality, which is

                                                INTERNATIONAL JOURNAL OF URBAN SCIENCES       5


descriptive in nature, by introducing a normative evaluation of whether the observed dis­
parities are ethically or socially acceptable (Litman, 2015; van Wee & Geurs, 2011).
Transport equity concerns whether individuals and communities – regardless of
income, location, or demographic characteristics – can access opportunities and partici­
pate fully in society. This study focuses on three key and interrelated dimensions of trans­
port inequity, which are spatial, socio-economic, and mobility inequities. The terms
spatial inequity, socio-economic inequity, and mobility inequity address disparities in
opportunities and resources, each emphasizing distinct dimensions.
    Spatial inequity arises from geographic disparities in the distribution of transport
infrastructure and services. In many cities, central or high-income areas benefit from
dense networks and high service levels, while peripheral or low-density zones often
face limited connectivity and longer travel times (Arellana et al., 2021; Panzera & Post­
iglione, 2020). Spatial inequity is assessed through the analysis of the distribution of
resources, services, and opportunities across various geographical regions. Indicators
of significance encompass access to public services, including healthcare and education,
income inequality, unemployment rates, housing quality, and employment opportunities
(Lawal & Anyiam, 2019; Morasae et al., 2012).
    Socio-economic inequity concerns disparities in access based on income, education,
or employment status. Lower-income groups may face financial barriers, longer commu­
tes, and reduced modal choice, which constrain their access to urban opportunities
(Lucas, 2012). Socioeconomic inequity is assessed by analysing disparities in social and
economic status across different demographic groups, including factors such as
income, education, and occupation. Common measures encompass income distribution,
the percentage of the population residing below the poverty line, and variations in
employment rates or educational attainment (Hertel & Groh-Samberg, 2019).
    Mobility inequity entails two primary domains: socioeconomic mobility and geo­
graphic or physical mobility. Socioeconomic mobility examines the ease with which indi­
viduals can enhance their social and economic status throughout their lives or across
generations, shaped by elements such as educational access, job quality, and inheritances
(Chetty et al., 2014; Corak, 2013). In contrast, geographic or physical mobility refers to
the capacity of individuals to navigate within a city or region, influenced by factors such
as the availability of transport options (Brockmann et al., 2006). Limited access to reliable
public transit or car ownership can restrict geographic and physical mobility, which in
turn limits individuals’ access to job opportunities, education, and essential services,
thereby impeding socioeconomic mobility. Assessment is conducted based on factors
including the availability, affordability, and quality of transport, average commuting
times, vehicle ownership rates, and the accessibility of destinations through walking,
cycling, or public transport. This study examines mobility inequity as a type of transpor­
tation-related disparity.
    Spatial inequity, socioeconomic inequity, and physical mobility inequity are inter­
related concepts that influence one another in intricate manners. Spatial inequity influ­
ences socio-economic inequity by determining the distribution of resources and
opportunities among various social groups, often resulting in the concentration of
wealth and services in particular regions while leaving others underserved (Gezici &
Hewings, 2007; J. J. Shen et al., 2022). Spatial inequity restricts physical mobility, as indi­
viduals in regions with inadequate transport access encounter difficulties in accessing
6      I. G. A. ANDANI ET AL.


employment opportunities or essential services, thereby perpetuating socio-economic
disparities (Petersen et al., 2010).
   Socioeconomic inequity significantly influences the disparities in spatial and physical
mobility. Individuals from lower socioeconomic backgrounds often experience restricted
access to reliable transport, which impedes their mobility within urban or regional areas
(Tomasiello et al., 2022). The limitation of physical mobility can exacerbate socio-econ­
omic disparities by hindering access to education, healthcare, and employment opportu­
nities, thereby obstructing socio-economic mobility (Morasae et al., 2012).
   In addition, disparities in physical mobility can exacerbate spatial and socioeconomic
inequalities. Disparities in access to transport infrastructure can result in spatial divides,
leading to the marginalization of specific areas due to inadequate connectivity (Sun et al.,
2020). The absence of physical mobility options can isolate communities from economic
opportunities and essential services, thereby perpetuating socio-economic disparities and
spatial inequalities. These three forms of inequity are interconnected and function in a
cyclical manner. Spatial inequity restricts physical mobility and intensifies socio-econ­
omic inequity, whereas socio-economic inequity can hinder physical mobility and
sustain spatial inequity.


2.2. Accessibility and job accessibility as analytical lenses
Accessibility is a key concept in transport planning and equity analysis. It refers to the ease
with which people can reach desired destinations, such as jobs, education, healthcare, or
other essential services (Dalvi & Martin, 1976; Hansen, 1959). Unlike mobility – which
focuses on the capacity to move – accessibility highlights the relationship between trans­
port systems, land use, and the spatial distribution of opportunities (Litman, 2013). In
equity evaluations, accessibility offers a more outcome-oriented perspective, as it reflects
people’s ability to participate in socio-economic life.
    Among different types of accessibility, job accessibility is especially significant due to
its direct implications for economic welfare and inclusion. Employment access is a daily
necessity, and the quality of this access can influence income levels, time poverty, and
long-term socio-economic mobility (El-Geneidy et al., 2016; Lucas et al., 2016). While
many transport equity studies have examined access to healthcare (e.g. Jin et al., 2022;
Sharma & Patil, 2021; J. J. Shen et al., 2022) or education (e.g. Pizzol et al., 2021;
Tiznado-Aitken et al., 2021), job accessibility is more sensitive to daily transport con­
ditions and often exposes deep-rooted spatial and socio-economic divides. For
example, Boisjoly et al. (2017) examined job accessibility in São Paulo, Brazil, found
that access is predominantly concentrated in high-income areas, primarily attributable
to employment informality and the unequal distribution of public transport services.
    Similar other studies also indicates that low-income populations in big cities in Aus­
tralia, Brazil, Uruguay, Great Britain, the United States, and many more face extended
commutes, elevated transport costs, and restricted access to public transit, which
limits their employment opportunities (see, for example, El-Geneidy et al., 2016; Fan
et al., 2012; Guzman et al., 2017; Kawabata & Shen, 2007; Sharma & Patil, 2021; Q.
Shen, 2001; Smith et al., 2020; Teunissen et al., 2015). These studies address spatial mis­
match and modal inequity, while also highlight the significance of affordable transport
and high-quality network infrastructure.
                                               INTERNATIONAL JOURNAL OF URBAN SCIENCES       7


   In cities of the Global South – particularly in Southeast Asia – there is a relative scar­
city of research that examines job accessibility in a comprehensive, multi-dimensional
way. Urban mobility in these contexts is shaped by unique modal mixes, including
motorcycles and informal transport services, and rapidly evolving infrastructure
systems. This study addresses these gaps by assessing job accessibility across income
groups and transport modes in Jakarta, where motorcycles and ride-hailing services
are widely used and public transport remains unevenly distributed.


2.3. Operationalizing accessibility-based inequity: metrics and indicators
Accessibility measures are crucial for analysing transportation inequity as they reveal the
varying effects of transportation systems on different socio-economic groups (Golub &
Martens, 2014). Nonetheless, they possess certain limitations. Metrics like the Gini coeffi­
cient and Lorenz curve (Gini, 1936) offer general evaluations of inequality; however, they
may fail to reflect nuanced disparities among particular subgroups. These measures do
not consider individuals’ socioeconomic status, making it impossible to determine
whether inequalities disproportionately affect disadvantaged populations. They also
lack directionality, meaning they cannot distinguish between progressive and regressive
changes in accessibility. To address this issue, various metrics such as the Theil index
(Conceição & Ferreira, 2000) and Palma ratio (Palma, 2011) can be integrated to offer
a more comprehensive understanding of inequities related to accessibility.
   The Gini coefficient is used to assess overall inequality in the distribution of job acces­
sibility. It provides a single summary statistic that reflects the concentration of access
among the population, where higher values indicate greater inequity (Delbosc &
Currie, 2011). The Palma ratio assesses the accessibility distribution between the top
10% and the bottom 40% of the population, emphasizing disparities at the extremes
(Guzman & Oviedo, 2018). The Theil index decomposes overall inequity into within-
group and between-group components, facilitating an analysis of inequity variation
within and between categories, such as population density groups (Jin et al., 2022).
   These indicators translate abstract inequity concepts into observable and comparable
patterns across transport modes, zones, and population groups. By applying them to job
accessibility outcomes – disaggregated by income group, transport mode, and spatial
zone – the study offers a structured and quantifiable assessment of transport-related
exclusion. This operational framework builds on global best practices while adapting
to the specific urban and modal characteristics of a Southeast Asian megacity.


3. Data and methods
The analytical framework of this study integrates spatial and temporal travel data with
job distribution metrics to evaluate job accessibility and its inequity in Jakarta. As illus­
trated in Figure 2, the methodology comprises two main stages: accessibility estimation
and inequity measurement. In the first stage, accessibility is calculated using the cumu­
lative opportunities method within a 41.2-minute travel time threshold (Jakarta’s average
commuting time – JICA, 2019). This involves combining travel time data – parametrized
by mode and collected via Google Maps API on November 2, 2020 at 10:00 AM – with
job distribution data derived from origin-destination (OD) matrices. The second stage

3.1. Study area and traffic analysis zone (TAZ)
Jakarta was selected as the case study for this research due to its complex urban mor­
phology, rapidly growing population, and persistent challenges related to transport
inequity. As Indonesia’s capital and largest city, Jakarta presents a highly relevant
context for investigating accessibility disparities, particularly in light of its evolving trans­
port landscape, which includes recent investments in mass public transport such as the
MRT, LRT, and BRT (Abiad et al., 2019; Hasibuan & Mulyani, 2022; Martinez & Masron,
2020). The city is also characterized by a high degree of modal heterogeneity, where
formal systems coexist with informal and motorcycle-based travel. These characteristics
make Jakarta a representative and policy-relevant case for understanding how geography,
infrastructure distribution, and socio-economic diversity shape job accessibility out­
comes in a Global South megacity.
   Jakarta Province, comprising five administrative cities: North Jakarta, South Jakarta,
East Jakarta, West Jakarta, and Central Jakarta. The study area (Figure 3) is segmented
into Traffic Analysis Zones (TAZs), facilitating a comprehensive evaluation of travel
behaviour throughout the region. The definition of TAZs follows the methodology estab­
lished by the Japan International Cooperation Agency (JICA, 2019).
   It indicates that the distribution of framework data for formulating traffic analysis

(population/km2).

3.4. Accessibility measurement
Infrastructure-based, location-based, person-based, and utility-based accessibility
measures are usually used (Geurs & van Wee, 2004). Infrastructure-based measures
focus on transport infrastructure and can include supply-oriented metrics like the dis­
tance to the next toll gate or demand-and-supply indicators like road network travel
times (van Wee et al., 2001). Location-based accessibility estimates cumulative opportu­
nities within a specified travel time or distance or evaluates opportunities at destination
locations from an origin, weighted by distance decay. This study employs both infrastruc­
ture-based and location-based measures. The infrastructure-based approach assesses the
performance of transport infrastructure, focusing on service levels, such as travel times

   However, the threshold-based cumulative accessibility approach has certain limit­
ations. First, it imposes a rigid cut-off, which may oversimplify the gradual decline in
willingness to travel beyond the threshold. In reality, individuals’ tolerance for travel
time may vary depending on income, trip purpose, and transport availability. Second,
the binary nature of the measure does not account for the qualitative experience of
travel – such as reliability, comfort, or safety – factors which may influence accessibility
but are not captured in this model. Lastly, the threshold does not consider time-of-day
variation or congestion effects, which could lead to under- or over-estimation of
actual accessibility during peak hours. Despite these limitations, the threshold method
remains a widely used and interpretable measure for highlighting spatial disparities in
access and guiding comparative equity assessments.
   For the purpose of this research, job accessibility will be calculated for each zone and
assessed across transportation modes and income levels, spanning low to high income.
Accessibility maps will be categorized into five classes (‘very low’ to ‘very high’) using
the natural breaks classification in ArcGIS Pro software. Natural breaks classification
(Jenks) organizes data into categories based on natural groupings within the dataset.
Class breaks are determined to group similar values effectively while maximizing the dis­
tinctions between different categories. The categorization method was chosen as it effec­
tively categorizes accessibility into distinct classes based on significant differences in data
values, maximizing interpretability with standardized classification for easier comparison
across different variables compared to other methods.


3.5. Travel time between zones
This study obtained travel time data for Jakarta’s 133 Traffic Analysis Zones (TAZs)

(1) Zone Identification and Centroid Mapping

The study utilized 133 TAZs covering the Jakarta Province, with centroid coordinates

framework (JICA, 2019). These centroids were used as origin and destination points
for travel time estimation.

(2) Google Maps API Configuration and Access

A valid API key from the Google Maps Platform was acquired, enabling access to the Dis­
tance Matrix API, which provides travel time estimates incorporating both real-time and
historical traffic data.

(3) OD Pair Construction

All possible origin-destination pairs between zones were constructed, yielding 17,689
unique combinations (133 × 133). For each pair, travel time data were retrieved for
three transport modes: car, motorcycle, and public transport.

3.6. Inequity metrics
3.6.1. Gini coefficient and Lorenz curve
The Gini coefficient measures inequality by comparing the area between the Lorenz curve
and the line of perfect equality (a 45-degree line) to the total area beneath it. On a scale of
0 to 1, 0 means everyone has the same share and 1 means all values are concentrated in
one person or group.
   The Lorenz curve, developed by Lorenz (1905), shows the population’s cumulative
distribution of a variable like wealth or income. The Gini coefficient quantifies inequality,
while the Lorenz curve illustrates it (see Figure 7). Area A between the Lorenz curve and
the line of equality is divided by the entire area under the line of equality (A + B) to deter­
mine the coefficient. This method permits mathematical inequality comparison across
distributions. Lorenz curves and the Gini coefficient can be used on any cumulative dis­
tribution, including accessibility levels vs population proportion, as in this case. They are
usually employed for income.

4. Results and discussion
4.1. Patterns of job accessibility in Jakarta
Job accessibility will be quantified for each Traffic Analysis Zone (TAZ) by determin­
ing the number of jobs reachable within a specified time travel threshold by the
population residing in the corresponding TAZ. The threshold used for this analysis
is determined by averaging the time travel data across all transport modes (car,
motorcycle, and public transport), resulting in a threshold of 41.2 min. Utilizing
the number of jobs available in a TAZ by trip attraction data, as discussed in
section 3.4, we further calculate the number of jobs that each TAZ, serving as an
origin, can access within the established 41.2-minute time frame. Figure 8 shows
the compilation of maps providing a general comparison of job access levels across
various means of travel and income levels.

4.1.1. Spatial distribution of job accessibility
The spatial distribution of job accessibility in Jakarta reveals pronounced centrality, with
the highest accessibility concentrated in the city’s urban core, particularly in Central,
South, and parts of West Jakarta. These areas host Jakarta’s primary employment clus­
ters, commercial centres, and administrative districts, making them critical focal
points for commuting flows. Accessibility consistently declines toward the northern,
eastern, and peripheral southern areas, especially in zones where infrastructure is
inadequate or disconnected from public transport networks.
   Among all transport modes, motorcycles offer the widest spatial coverage, enabling
access to a broad range of jobs from both central and peripheral areas. This spatial
                                               INTERNATIONAL JOURNAL OF URBAN SCIENCES        17

levels in Jakarta.


flexibility allows motorcycles to reach job centres not only in well-developed districts
but also in semi-urban or informal settlements where public transport coverage is
weak. This pattern reflects broader trends in Global South cities, where informal
or two-wheeler modes bridge spatial gaps in formal transport systems (Uteng &
Lucas, 2017).
   Conversely, public transport accessibility is spatially limited, primarily concentrated
along a few high-capacity transit corridors served by bus rapid transit (BRT) and com­
muter rail lines. While accessibility is relatively high near these corridors, it quickly
declines in areas located beyond the limited reach of trunk lines and transfer points.
The absence of a comprehensive feeder system and inadequate first- and last-mile infra­
structure contribute to the spatially selective nature of public transport access, reinfor­
cing exclusion for residents in underserved peripheral districts.
   Car accessibility exhibits spatial selectivity, concentrated in areas with high-quality
road infrastructure and less congestion. However, Jakarta’s chronic traffic delays and
18      I. G. A. ANDANI ET AL.


poor road network in dense informal settlements constrain car-based accessibility, par­
ticularly in lower-income neighbourhoods.

4.1.2. Modal contributions to job accessibility
The mode of transport is a critical determinant of job accessibility outcomes. Motorcycles
emerge as the dominant enabler, accounting for 62.5% of all jobs reached in the region.
Their role is particularly important for medium- and low-income populations, who
together rely on motorcycles to access over 7 million jobs. The efficiency, affordability,
and adaptability of motorcycles in Jakarta’s congested and often fragmented urban
fabric make them a cornerstone of urban mobility (Joewono & Kubota, 2007).
   This study demonstrates that motorcycles markedly improve job accessibility for all
socio-economic groups, supporting the findings of Irawan et al. (2020), which indicate
that motorcycle ride-hailing services enhance urban mobility for lower-income house­
holds. However, this equity comes at a cost. Motorcycles are also associated with high
emissions, road safety risks, and unregulated traffic behaviours, raising critical questions
about the sustainability of equity gains achieved through informal transport (Gutierrez &
Mohan, 2020; Kalifa et al., 2024; Mahesh et al., 2019; Yousif et al., 2020). This duality
shows the complex trade-offs facing urban transport policy in rapidly motorizing cities
– where motorcycles function both as an equalizer and a sustainability challenge.
   The heavy reliance on motorcycles also reflects systemic deficiencies in the formal
transport system, particularly in delivering broad, inclusive access to employment.
Motorcycles fill the vacuum left by inadequate public transport coverage, especially in
areas beyond the reach of trunk routes.
   In contrast, public transport plays a minimal role, facilitating access to only 9.27% of
total jobs. Even for the most advantaged group (medium-income), public transport
allows access to just 692,000 jobs (4.93%). The system’s limited geographic coverage,
weak integration, and low service frequency constrain its potential to equitably
connect residents with economic opportunities (as also argued by Basso et al., 2020;
Bueno Rezendede Castro et al., 2022). These issues disproportionately affect low-
income residents who are most dependent on affordable public transit options.
   Car-based accessibility, while more substantial than public transport in absolute
numbers, also reveals inequality. Medium-income users benefit the most (2.16 million
jobs), but high-income users access significantly fewer jobs (737,000) despite greater
ownership rates (Sefriyadi et al., 2023). This counterintuitive pattern likely stems from
spatial mismatch between elite residential enclaves and job centres, along with the limit­
ations of Jakarta’s car-oriented infrastructure in accommodating efficient, long-distance
commuting (Oviedo & Guzman, 2020; Saraiva & Barros, 2022).

4.1.3. Income-level differences in job accessibility
Income level is closely intertwined with both spatial and modal patterns of job accessi­
bility. Medium-income individuals consistently demonstrate the highest levels of job
accessibility, reaching over 7.67 million jobs (54.59%). This outcome is attributable to
both spatial self-selection and greater modal flexibility. Unlike high-income groups,
medium-income individuals are often willing to reside in modest housing conditions
closer to employment centres, striking a balance between affordability and accessibility
(Adeniran et al., 2024; Friedman et al., 2020; Levine, 1998). Their neighbourhoods are
                                             INTERNATIONAL JOURNAL OF URBAN SCIENCES      19


frequently located in older, mid-density areas that remain well-connected to road and
transport networks.
   High-income residents, although financially advantaged, access fewer jobs (18.56%)
due to residing in low-density, exclusive zones, often on the periphery or in gated com­
munities. Aritenang (2022)observed that residents with higher incomes, despite living in
peripheral zones, often commute to central urban areas. These locations prioritize
privacy and housing quality over proximity to work and are often disconnected from
both public and informal transport routes. As a result, their effective job accessibility
remains constrained.
   Low-income residents – while potentially located closer to the city core – face barriers
of a different kind. Limited financial capacity, inadequate infrastructure, and dependence
on low-cost, informal transport restrict their job access to 3.77 million jobs (26.85%). The
minimal contribution of public transport for this group further reinforces structural dis­
advantages, as they are priced out of formal alternatives and often face longer travel
times, safety issues, and regulatory uncertainties (Bergman et al., 2024).


4.2. Distributional assessment using inequity metrics
While the previous section detailed variations in job accessibility by mode, income group,
and location, such descriptive analysis alone is insufficient to understand the deeper pat­
terns of transport-related disadvantage. Not all differences in accessibility are inherently
unfair; therefore, to assess whether these disparities reflect systemic inequities, it is
necessary to apply formal distributional metrics. Inequity indicators offer a quantitative
lens to measure the extent to which job accessibility is unevenly distributed across
different population groups and spatial units, and to identify whether these differences
are disproportionately concentrated among certain social or geographic categories.
   The measurement of inequity in job accessibility is based on three indices: the Gini
coefficient, the Palma ratio, and the Theil index. Analysis of these metrics reveals that
access to employment via public transport demonstrates the greatest degree of inequality
relative to other transportation modes.
   The Gini coefficient and Palma ratio are calculated for each income level, allowing for
an analysis of accessibility distribution across different socioeconomic strata. The metrics
highlight ongoing challenges in attaining equitable job access, with public transport
demonstrating the greatest level of inequality across all income brackets. Furthermore,
the Theil index utilizes population density groupings to examine access disparities, dis­
tinguishing between within-group and between-group inequalities.

trated in a small subset of zones.

networks often serve dense, high-income corridors while peripheral or informal

4.2.2. Palma ratio
The Palma ratio provides additional insight into distributional extremes by comparing
the share of job accessibility held by the top 10% of zones to that of the bottom 40%.
As shown in Table 3, the Palma ratios for car (1.610), motorcycle (1.442), and public
transport (7.361) all exceed 1.0, indicating that accessibility is concentrated among the
most advantaged zones across all modes. However, the disparity is most severe for
public transport, where the top 10% of zones capture more than seven times the acces­
sibility of the bottom 40%.
   This finding is consistent with spatial mismatch theory (Andersson et al., 2011), which
posits that low-income households in peripheral or low-access zones are spatially discon­
nected from employment opportunities, especially when high-quality public transport is
concentrated in central districts. In the case of Jakarta, the spatial mismatch is not necess­
arily due to cost barriers, as observed in cities like Bogota (Guzman & Oviedo, 2018), but
rather due to limited network coverage and inadequate integration between transit
modes. While public transport may be nominally affordable, its geographic inaccessibility
renders it functionally exclusionary for large segments of the population.
   Moreover, these results align with those from the Gini coefficient analysis, reinforcing
that public transport is the most unequal mode in terms of accessibility distribution. In
contrast, motorcycle accessibility is the most evenly distributed, with a relatively low
Palma ratio and Gini coefficient. This again emphasizes the unique role motorcycles
play in expanding access in areas poorly served by formal transport modes.
   Similar with the Gini results, the Palma ratios for all three income groups (low,
medium, and high) show minimal variation, suggesting that inequities in accessibility
are not strongly differentiated by income strata. Rather, they reflect a broader pattern
of modal and spatial exclusion, confirming earlier observations that transport inequity
in Jakarta is shaped more by geography and network design than by socio-economic cat­
egory alone.

4.2.3. Theil index: within-group vs. between-group inequity
The Theil index adds another layer of insight by decomposing inequity into within-group
and between-group disparities. As shown in Table 4 and Figure 10, 96% of total inequal­
ity in public transport access arises within population density groups, compared to only
4% between them. Similar patterns are observed for cars (91% within-group) and motor­
cycles (92% within-group).
   This shows that inequality cannot solely be caused by variations between high- and
low-density locations; it’s also caused by variances within each group. This means that
some neighbourhoods in dense areas remain without access to jobs because of inadequate
connectivity or gaps in infrastructure. Thus, broad spatial categories like ‘central vs. per­
ipheral’ or ‘dense vs. sparse’ may obscure crucial differences that are only found in certain
areas. City-wide or corridor-scale changes alone are unlikely to render mobility more
fair. Fine-grained improvements at the district or subdistrict level, including feeder
routes, pedestrian access to stations, or micro-mobility infrastructure, may work better
to reduce spatial exclusion.
   These results go against the popular belief in urban planning that density alone assures
access and show how important it is to make changes at the neighbourhood level. Policies
for public transit in cities frequently focus on expanding the network. However, this
study implies that making access easier and consolidating it in places that are already
‘served’ is just as important. This entails making stations work better together,

5. Conclusion
This study offers a comprehensive, multi-dimensional assessment of job accessibility in
Jakarta, analysing spatial, modal, and income-related disparities through the use of a
cumulative opportunities framework and equity metrics, including the Gini coefficient,
Palma ratio, and Theil index. The results indicate a significant spatial centrality in job
accessibility, with residents of Jakarta’s urban core having considerably more access to
employment opportunities than those in peripheral and low-density areas and remaining
underserved. Transportation mode plays a critical role in this divide.
    Motorcycles offer the most comprehensive and equitable employment accessibility
among all modes. They are a critical means of access for medium- and low-income
groups due to their flexibility and reach into both central and peripheral zones. Never­
theless, this dependence on motorcycles also underscores the systemic deficiencies in
Jakarta’s formal transportation infrastructure and raises long-term concerns regarding
safety, sustainability, and regulation.
    Conversely, public transportation exhibits the most severe inequality. Accessibility via
public transport is spatially concentrated in a small number of well-served zones and
does not extend effectively to underserved areas. This is evidenced by its high Gini coeffi­
cient (0.417), extreme Palma ratio (7.361), and Theil index, which suggests that over 90%
of inequality is concentrated within population density groupings rather than between
them. These findings suggest that spatial exclusion in Jakarta is not only a result of
large-scale urban structure but also of neighbourhood-level service deficiencies, reinfor­
cing the need for localized, fine-grained improvements such as feeder services, pedestrian
access, and micro-mobility infrastructure.
    While medium-income individuals enjoy the highest average access, the similarity in
Gini coefficients across income categories indicates that income alone is not the domi­
nant factor shaping inequity. Instead, geographic location and transport mode are
more influential, pointing to spatial and modal exclusion as the primary drivers of job
accessibility disparities in Jakarta.
    Beyond its empirical contributions, this study makes three important additions to the
transport equity literature. First, it presents a replicable framework for evaluating job
accessibility in the Global South using formal, multidimensional equity metrics,
offering one of the first such applications in a Southeast Asian megacity. Second, it inte­
grates spatial, socio-economic, and modal dimensions into a unified analytical approach,
enabling a more comprehensive understanding of transport-related disadvantage. Third,
it draws attention to context-specific mobility patterns – such as motorcycle dependence
and fragmented transit networks – that are often overlooked in global accessibility
research. In doing so, the study responds directly to the need, articulated in the literature,
for context-sensitive and data-driven assessments of transport inequality in emerging
urban regions.
    The findings highlight the urgency of expanding an inclusive and integrated public
transport network, improving first- and last-mile connections, and extending service
coverage to underserved districts. While motorcycles may continue to serve as an
24       I. G. A. ANDANI ET AL.


adaptive mobility solution, long-term transport planning should prioritize the transition
toward safer, low-emission, and more reliable public modes. Embedding equity metrics
into transport planning and investment decisions is essential to identify and address
intra-urban disparities and ensure that accessibility gains are equitably shared.
   Future research should explore how job accessibility and transport equity evolve over
time, especially in response to Jakarta’s ongoing investments in MRT, LRT, and BRT
systems. Longitudinal studies comparing pre- and post-intervention conditions would
provide evidence on whether infrastructure investments effectively reduce inequity.
Further research should also incorporate more granular socio-demographic variables –
such as gender, age, employment type, and disability status – to examine how different
groups experience transport exclusion. Methodologically, the development of dynamic
accessibility models that reflect real-time service variability could enhance the realism
of future assessments. Lastly, future work should explore the intersection of housing
affordability, land use, and transport accessibility, particularly to assess the equity
impacts of urban regeneration and transit-oriented development strategies in Jakarta.
