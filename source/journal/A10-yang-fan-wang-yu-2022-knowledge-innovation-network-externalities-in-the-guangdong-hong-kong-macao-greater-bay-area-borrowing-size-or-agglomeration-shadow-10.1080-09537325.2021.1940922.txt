1. Introduction
In the knowledge economy, urban agglomerations have become incubators for regional innovation.
With the transition from ‘space of place’ to ‘space of ﬂow’ (Castells 1996a, 1996b), the innovative
spatial pattern of urban agglomerations has gradually evolved from traditional hierarchies to net-
works. Cities have transformed from independent points in space to hubs in networks, and inter-
actions between cities have become important factors aﬀecting innovation performance (Cooke
et al. 2011).
    The Guangdong–Hong Kong–Macao Greater Bay Area (GBA) is one of the regions with the highest
degree of openness and the most economic vitality in China. To maintain its competitiveness, the
Chinese government issued the ‘GBA Planning Outline’ in 2019, aiming to build the area into a
world-class urban agglomeration and international innovation highland, which considers Guangz-
hou, Shenzhen, Hong Kong and Macao as four innovation centres.
    However, it is unclear whether the innovation network of GBA has formed a multi-centre pattern,
and what its network externality is. Most research on urban networks in GBA analyses from the per-
spective of economic factor ﬂows (Qiu et al. 2019; Wang et al. 2020). Although some studies have
examined innovation in this region, including exploring the innovation development path (Gu,

2. Literature review
2.1 From agglomeration externality to network externality
Agglomeration and diﬀusion are contradictory phenomena in the spatial evolution of urban agglom-
erations. The classic ‘backwash-spread’ theory, proposed by Myrdal, and ‘trickle down’ eﬀect pro-
posed by Hirschman (1958), both explain the imbalance of regional development. The spatial
spillover eﬀect of a central place gradually decreases under the constraint of distance. While
driving the development of surrounding areas, the central place causes peripheral areas to fall
into a trough (Zhang and Zhuang 2000) and form a ‘core-periphery’ structure. When Friedmann
(1972) created the core–periphery theory, drawing on Schumpeter’s innovative ideas, he believed
that development could be regarded as a discontinuous accumulation process from basic innovation
groups to large-scale innovation systems. Large cities with favourable conditions for innovation
activities often become the core area of regional innovation, while the surrounding areas are
reduced to a subordinate position; then, the two together form a complete innovation space
system. Focusing on the relationship between cities, Alonso (1973) proposed the concept of ‘bor-
rowed size’. He pointed out that small cities close to metropolitan areas have higher per capita
income levels than cities of the same scale and independent development. This is because these
small cities can realise some of the advantages of large cities while avoiding their disadvantages
by borrowing size. Krugman and Fujita (1995) introduced the concept of ‘aggregation shadow’,
referring to the situation in which some small cities do not beneﬁt from large neighbouring cities
but are instead disadvantaged by them (Meijers, Burger, and Hoogerbrugge 2016).
    Above theories have led many researchers to verify the two-sided externalities of agglomeration.
Some scholars divided agglomeration economies into localised and urbanised economies and dis-
cussed their external eﬀects on industries and urban economies (Tao et al. 2019; Wu, Wei, and
Chen 2020). Other scholars have veriﬁed borrowing size as a positive agglomeration externality (Mar-
tinus et al. 2021). The essence of borrowing size is the complementarity between the local advan-
tages of small cities (such as lower rents and less traﬃc congestion) and the advantages of big
cities (such as a broad market, labour pool and diverse consumption/cultural facilities) (Phelps,
1998). However, some scholars have also found that big cities have an agglomeration shadow
eﬀect on small cities. Speciﬁcally, there are signiﬁcant diﬀerences in land use types between metro-
polises and their shadow areas (Wu, Zhou, and Liu 2020). Per capita income in large cities is much
higher than surrounding areas, resulting in imbalances in the distribution of labour and the
spatial pattern of the economy (Au and Henderson 2006; Sun et al. 2019). A 2004 research report
by the Asian Development Bank introduced the ‘Beijing-Tianjin Poverty Belt’, which refers to a C-
shaped belt poverty area around Beijing and Tianjin (Luo and Ma 2015). This prompted scholars

to research the formation mechanism of the agglomeration shadow and poverty reduction measures
(He, Fan, and Li 2018).
    The progress of technology and the advent of the network society have made various factors,
such as population, capital, information etc., ﬂow between cities convenient and frequent, and
mechanisms of interaction between cities are also changing. Cities and the companies within
them not only beneﬁt from local exchanges, but also increasingly aﬀected by external regions
(Wang et al. 2020). Access to agglomeration advantages is suﬃcient to the company’s operation
without having to exist in or be close to the agglomeration area (Speldekamp, Knoben, and Saka-
Helmhout 2020). However, the perspective of agglomeration externality, based on the centre periph-
ery theory, cannot explain the agglomeration phenomenon on a larger scale because it conﬁnes the
city’s inﬂuence on its neighbouring and ignores cross-regional connections (Fan et al. 2021). In view
of this, Castells (1996a, 1996b) introduced the concept of ‘space of ﬂows’, referring to the spatial
pattern formed by the ﬂow of various elements. Taylor, Hoyler, and Verbruggen (2010) proposed
the central ﬂow theory based on the external urban relations to criticise central place theory
based on interurban hierarchical relations. In the context of space of ﬂow, the intercity ﬂow of
various elements has shaped connections between cities, shifting the study of regional spatial struc-
ture from the internal attributes of cities to the external connections (Inostroza and Zepp 2021).
    Capello introduced ‘network externality’ and found that it improves with an increase of nodes in
the network. By participating in network, cities can achieve functional complementarity through
cooperation, obtain economies of scale and reduce uncertainty. By joining the network, cities can
obtain two eﬀects (Meijers 2005): (1) the horizontal synergy eﬀect, which refers to the scale eﬀect
brought about by simple cooperation between cities; (2) vertical synergy eﬀect, which refers to
the division of labour and specialised production between cities. In this context, the realisation of
borrowing size is no longer based on geographical proximity but through embedded in networks
(Meijers, Burger, and Hoogerbrugge 2016). Therefore, a city’s ability to establish a network connec-
tion has become important in realising the borrowing size.
    In general, the agglomeration economy causes externalities. No matter how the regional spatial
structure changes, borrowing size and agglomeration shadow together comprise the pros and cons
of externalities (Meijers, Burger, and Hoogerbrugge 2016). The ﬁnal eﬀect of externalities depends on
the tradeoﬀ between the two eﬀects. Therefore, analysing the role of externalities is of great signiﬁ-
cance for objectively evaluating and optimising regional spatial structures.


2.2 Innovation network externality
Generally, the development of innovative activities begins in a non-equilibrium state due to the het-
erogeneous distribution of innovative resources (Mohnen, DeBresson, and Shiqing 2002). Under the
eﬀect of path dependence, innovation resources continue to be highly concentrated in a certain
region ultimately making it an innovation pole. While areas with less innovative resources are inno-
vation holes, which together with the innovation pole constitute the spatial pattern of innovation
(Gonçalves, de Matos, and de Araújo 2019). As the connections and interactions involved in inno-
vation continue to strengthen within the innovation pole, innovation networks are generated and
continuously expanded. Driven by the promotion of globalisation and knowledge economy, inno-
vation activities continue to expand, eventually forming an innovation network that brings together
local and cross-regional interactions (Fan, Lian, and Wang 2020). This type of network structure has
the characteristics of coexistence, with obvious agglomeration boundaries and openness, making
the spatial eﬀect of the innovation network uncertain.
   From the perspective of internal factors, the structure of the innovation network signiﬁcantly
aﬀects its eﬀectiveness (Taalbi, 2020). The density and centrality of network are positively corre-
lated with the innovation performance (Bai et al. 2021). Enterprises embedded in innovation
network have more innovation output than those outside the network (Fan, Lian, and Wang
2020). The innovation cooperation relationship can be divided into strong and weak connections.
4      W. YANG ET AL.


Strong connections can enhance trust and cooperation and promote resource ﬂow and knowl-
edge sharing within the network (Hansen 1999). However, some scholars believe that strong con-
nections will reduce the diversity of members in innovation network, thereby reducing the
sources of new ideas and technologies. A weak connection has low-cost advantages and
higher openness, which can extend the connection outside the network and promote cross-
border communication (Baron and Gomez 2013). Rowley, Behrens, and Krackhardt (2000) found
that strong ties encourage incremental technological innovation, while weak ties encourage
breakthrough technological innovation. Capaldo (2007) emphasised innovation networks with
only strong or weak connections are insuﬃcient for producing external eﬀects, and a two-layer
network structure with strong internal structures and weak external connections should be con-
structed. Eisingerich, Bell, and Tracey (2010) measured innovation network structure from two
aspects of network strength and openness and found that network strength’s positive eﬀect
on innovation performance decreases with increasing environmental uncertainty, while the posi-
tive eﬀect of network openness is positively correlated with environmental uncertainty. These
views mean that the external environment should be considered when discussing the innovation
network eﬀectiveness.
    From the perspective of external factors, Boschma and Frenken (2010) demonstrated that cog-
nitive proximity, social proximity, organisational proximity, institutional proximity and geographi-
cal proximity are the main factors aﬀecting the spatial evolution of innovation network. Except
that the spatial eﬀect of network attenuates with distance increasing (Foxon 2014), the spatial
concentration of innovation is likely to form a stable and rigid closed relationship network, result-
ing in a negative eﬀect of geographical proximity ‘lock-in’, which is unfavourable to innovation
(Bathelt, Feldman, and Kogler 2012). However, some scholars believe that the development and
popularisation of the Internet will lead to ‘a ﬂat world’, thus redeﬁning the spatial network in
terms of soft innovation (Howells and Bessant 2012). Social and cultural factors are playing
increasing roles on innovation. There will be more interaction and trust between innovative
members with closer cultural customs, thereby promoting innovative cooperation (Porter 1998).
However, the excessive singularity of cultural customs may produce xenophobia and discourage
innovation diversiﬁcation. Furthermore, institutional proximity reﬂects the degree of similarity of
enterprises constrained by formal or informal rules. On the one hand, it can reduce the uncer-
tainty and conﬂict of innovation cooperation (Balland, Belso-Martínez, and Morrison 2016). On
the other hand, excessive institutional proximity may lead to excessive dependence on organis-
ations, threaten the protection of intellectual property rights and reduce innovation incentives
(Molina-Morales et al. 2015).
    With the advancement of globalisation and technology, innovation cooperation can break
through geographical boundaries and expand outward. High density and openness have become
the source of spatial eﬀect of the innovation network, and soft environment factors, such as
culture and institution, impose signiﬁcant inﬂuences. In China, GBA is one of the most vigorous inno-
vation regions. However, the special environment of ‘one country, two systems and three customs
areas’ means its innovation faces more cultural and institutional challenges than other regions. The
Chinese government plans to establish a polycentric innovation network centred on Guangzhou,
Shenzhen, Hong Kong and Macao in here. What is the current situation of innovation in this
region? Is the externality of innovation network a positive borrowing size or negative agglomeration
shadow? Previous studies have focused on the evolution of network spatial structure (Li, Li, and Qiu
2019; Ma and Zeng 2020), innovation performance (Wang, Zhang, and Fan 2019; Fan and Lian 2021)
and inﬂuencing factors (Wen, Deng, and Chen 2017), while ignoring network externalities, so they
cannot ﬁnd out the truly spatial eﬀect of innovation networks. In view of this, this study focuses
on analysing the externalities of innovation network under the inﬂuence of distance, cultural and
institutional diﬀerences to clarify the impact of complex external environments on network
eﬀects and make up for the existing literatures.

3. Data and methods
3.1 Research scope
The GBA was ﬁrst formally proposed in China’s plan for the ‘One Belt and One Road’ in 2015 and was
subsequently included in the national ‘13th Five-Year Plan’ in 2016. The GBA consists of two special
administrative regions of Hong Kong and Macau and nine cities of Guangzhou, Foshan, Zhaoqing,
Jiangmen, Zhongshan, Zhuhai, Shenzhen, Dongguan and Huizhou in Guangdong Province. It oper-
ates two political systems and three separate customs territories, giving it a unique and complex soft
environment diﬀerent from other regions in China and bay areas around the world (Figure 1).



3.2 Data sources
Indicators of innovation include papers and patents. Paper data can be used to reﬂect knowledge
innovation, while patent data is more used to represent technological innovation. Therefore, this
study uses the number of all research papers published in city to reﬂect its knowledge innovation.
Correspondingly, the collaborative paper between cities can be used to measure the intercity knowl-
edge innovation cooperation, and papers without cooperation with other cities reﬂect the city’s
independent knowledge innovation ability. Above three types of paper data comes from the
‘web of science’. The study period is set to 2001–2019, the reasons are as follows (1) Hong Kong
and Macau became the two special administrative regions of China in 1997 and 1999 respectively,
and China joined the World Trade Organization in 2001, all of which greatly promoted cross-border
cooperation. (2) Although it was not until 2019 that the Chinese government issued the plan of GBA,
the local governments had already conceived and promoted cooperation at the beginning of the
twenty-ﬁrst century. Therefore, it is reasonable to set the starting year as 2001.
   We set up two dummy variables to measure the impact of the GBA’s cultural and institutional
environment on innovation cooperation. First is the cultural diﬀerence dummy variable (cultureij ),

two political systems in the GBA: the socialist system is implemented in nine cities in Guangdong

Kong and Macau have their own customs territories. The administrative levels of cities are
divided into three levels: Guangzhou and Shenzhen are sub-provincial cities, other seven cities
are prefecture-level cities, Hong Kong and Macau are special administrative regions (equivalent
to provincial level). Accordingly, the dummy variables of institutional diﬀerences between
every two cities are set from 1 to 4.
    This study also uses socio-economic indicators as control variables to measure the innovation
network externalities, which all come from statistical yearbooks of Guangdong Province and each
city.1



3.3 Methods
3.3.1 Social network analysis
This study uses the social network analysis method and UCINET software to calculate network cen-
trality and density. The indicator of degree centrality reﬂects the status and inﬂuence of a city in the
network, which is the sum of the direct connections between one node and other nodes. Density
reﬂects the closeness of innovation cooperation between cities. The knowledge innovation
network in this study is an undirected network. Therefore, the relative degree centrality and
network density formula are as follows:
                                  Degree centrality = d(i)/(n − 1)                                   (1)
where d(i) is the number of relationships owned by any node i in the network, and (n − 1) is the
maximum number of relationships that a node has in the network.
                                      Density = m/n(n − 1)/2                                         (2)
   The m represents the total number of relationships contained in the network, and the denomi-
nator, n(n − 1)/2, is the maximum number relationships of theoretically possible in the network.

4. Results and analysis
4.1 Knowledge innovation network spatial pattern
We examined the knowledge innovation development trend in the GBA using the total papers, local
papers and intercity collaborative papers. As Figures 2–4 show, the total number of papers published
in the GBA from 2001 to 2019 kept increasing. Before 2011, Hong Kong ranked ﬁrst in total papers. In
2013, Guangzhou’s total and local papers both exceeded Hong Kong; Guangzhou maintained ﬁrst in
the cooperative papers throughout the study period. After 2012, Shenzhen ranked third in total
papers, and the gap between it and Hong Kong continued shrinking. However, the number of
papers in Macao has been relatively small. Therefore, Guangzhou, Hong Kong and Shenzhen are
the three central cities in the current knowledge innovation network, while Macao has not yet
shown a high central position in knowledge innovation.
    The results of social network analysis show that the overall density of GBA’s knowledge inno-
vation network improved from 2001 to 2019, indicating that the innovation cooperation connections
among cities are increasing (Figure 5). The overall centrality was calculated using the total number of
papers published in each city. Hong Kong maintained the highest before 2012, was surpassed by
Guangzhou after 2013, and then shows a declining trend. Guangzhou showed a rapid growth
before 2012, but its growth trend slowed after 2013. Shenzhen surpassed Zhongshan in 2013 and
ranked third. In 2019, Shenzhen’s overall centrality was close to that of Hong Kong. The overall cen-
trality of other cities is relatively low (Figure 6).
    Cooperative centrality was calculated using the cooperative papers between every two cities.
Guangzhou maintained the highest cooperative centrality. Zhongshan ranked second before
2018, but behind Hong Kong in 2019. Shenzhen ranked fourth in the GBA (Figure 7).

4.3 Robustness and endogenous tests
The possible endogenous problem of above models is that there may be a causal relationship
between the city’s cooperative centrality and its knowledge innovation capabilities. Speciﬁcally,
the more knowledge innovation cooperation, the more knowledge innovation output; on the con-
trary, the stronger the knowledge innovation ability of cities, the more innovative cooperation
relationships there will be. In view of this, we use the lag term of cooperative centrality as an instru-
mental variable to revise the model. Results are shown in Table 3. The lagging term of urban coop-
erative centrality also has a negative impact on urban knowledge innovation. Under the distance
spatial weight matrix, the negative impact of centrality is relatively small and insigniﬁcant. Under
the spatial weight matrix of cultural and institutional diﬀerences, cooperative centrality both have
a signiﬁcant negative impact on cities’ overall, and cooperative innovation. These results are consist-
ent with the previous results, indicating that they are robust and reliable.

small and medium-sized cities do not enjoy the same favourable terms, which intensiﬁes the

5. Conclusion and discussion
The Chinese government is trying to build GBA into an international polycentric innovation hub.
Analysis in this paper shows that the knowledge innovation capability and network density of the
GBA have continuously improved, but the four-centre innovation network pattern in the govern-
ment plan has not yet been formed. Guangzhou and Hong Kong have always been innovation
centres in this region. Since 2012, Shenzhen has become another innovation centre. However,
Macao is not strong enough to be an innovation centre in the network. Although Hong Kong has
superior innovation strength, under the inﬂuence of institutional and cultural diﬀerences, its inno-
vation cooperation with mainland cities is relatively limited except for its cooperation with Guangz-
hou and Shenzhen. Most of the intercity innovation cooperation takes place within Guangdong
province, especially between Guangzhou and Shenzhen. Consistent with the conclusion of other
studies, this article also proves that there exists a multi-centre network structure in knowledge inno-
vation in GBA (Ma and Zeng 2020). The diﬀerence of conclusion lies in that Hong Kong does not have
a central position if technological innovation is considered (Li and Zhou 2020).
    Results of spatial econometric models show that the impact of cities’ innovation centrality on sur-
rounding areas is negative. Small and medium-sized cities do not obtain borrowing size but are in
the agglomeration shadow of central cities. In terms of inﬂuencing factors, the cultural and insti-
tutional diﬀerences between cities have far greater negative impacts on innovation than distance.
Inspired by this, improving soft environment is a prerequisite for reducing the spatial gaps and pro-
moting regional innovation. On the basis of the above conclusions, we propose the following sug-
gestions for optimising the spatial eﬀect of knowledge innovation network in GBA.
    First, it is necessary to reduce the institutional barriers in innovation cooperation between main-
land cities and Hong Kong/Macao. Hong Kong and Macau are special administrative regions in China
with diﬀerent political systems compared with the mainland. The current cross-border cooperation
mechanism is not sound, which hinders the development of innovative cooperation and the free
ﬂow of elements. In this regard, city managers need to formulate more convenient policies from
the aspects of personnel mobility, innovation resource sharing and cooperation projects to
promote the development of innovation cooperation. For small and medium-sized cities, it is necess-
ary to increase investment in knowledge innovation and institutional reforms to narrow the gap with
central cities.
    Second, create a more tolerant social environment that can be compatible with diverse cultures
and personalities. The interests of immigrant should be safeguarded and make them enjoy the same
treatment as the local residents, which is a key measure to eliminate the negative eﬀects of cultural
diﬀerences. Besides, improving the education level of residents will help to enhance the social inclu-
siveness and acceptance of cultural diﬀerences so as to exert the positive inﬂuence of cultural diver-
sity on stimulating innovation.
16         W. YANG ET AL.


    Third, reducing the obstacles caused by inadequate infrastructure, such as transportation and
Internet facilities, is still a task that cannot be ignored. Although great progress has been achieved
in the construction of transportation networks in recent years, distance still has a certain negative
impact on the ﬂow of factors. Cities can jointly promote the construction of transportation in
border areas by carrying out transportation cooperation projects. Among them, the intercity
traﬃc between mainland cities and Hong Kong/Macao is the most important. In addition to facilitat-
ing the movement of people, construction of Internet facility is a precondition for information
exchange and innovation cooperation, which is especially important for small and medium-sized
cities.
    It is worth noting that compared with Hong Kong, Guangzhou and Shenzhen, Macau’s knowl-
edge innovation ability is relatively weak. The development of higher education began late in
Macau is one of the reasons for this phenomenon. Macau established its ﬁrst modern university,
the University of Macau, in 1981, which is far later than Hong Kong and Guangzhou. Given this,
Macao should strengthen scientiﬁc research investment and talent exchange with Hong Kong
and Guangzhou to strengthen its basic research.
    Finally, strengthening the protection of property rights is an important measure to safeguard the
legitimate interests of researchers and stimulate innovation. Hong Kong’s experience and advan-
tages in intellectual property protection should be fully utilised to establish a regional intellectual
property protection and transaction centre. At the same time, an intellectual property information
exchange mechanism and sharing platform should be established in the GBA to promote the
legal and eﬀective circulation of intellectual property.
    Future research can be advanced from the following aspects. First, the measurement method of
network structure can be improved. For example, locally weighted regression is a cutting-edge
method that can accurately identify the centre of the network by ﬁtting the urban density
surface. Therefore, it can measure and identify urban innovation network centres more precisely.
Second, the spatial eﬀect of innovation network structure needs to be deeply analysed. In addition
to inﬂuencing innovation itself, the innovation network structure will also have an impact on other
aspects of the economy and society, as well as the urban spatial layout, which provides a wide range
of topics for future research. Finally, the situation and mechanism of knowledge innovation and tech-
nological innovation are signiﬁcantly diﬀerent. Therefore, analysing the network externalities from
the perspective of technological innovation is one of the contents of future work.
