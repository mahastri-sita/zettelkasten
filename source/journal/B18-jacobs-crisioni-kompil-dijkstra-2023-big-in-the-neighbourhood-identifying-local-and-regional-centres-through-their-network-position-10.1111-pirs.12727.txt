While much of the urbanism and economic geography literature focuses on the world's largest cities
(Bell & Jayne, 2009; ESPON, 2014), a sizeable portion of the world's population lives in small cities, towns
and rural areas. Counting gridded population counts (Batista e Silva et al., 2021) by degrees of urbanization

(European Commission, 2020) yields that 63% of the population in the EU and UK do not live in a city (i.e., urban
centre). Furthermore, according to the functional urban area definition (Dijkstra et al., 2019), some 38% of the EU
population lives in the vast territory outside these metropolitan areas. The lives of people living outside these metro-
politan areas are likely to revolve around small towns and villages that provide a cluster of services. Through the
flows of goods and services to and from those service clusters, those small settlements have a functional tie with
their hinterland (Klemmer, 1978). Thus, even if their role in labour markets may be very limited, some small settle-
ments can have a value that considerably exceeds their population size because of their role as hubs for services of
general interest (Rondinelli, 1983).
      The provision and delivery of social services of general interest, such as education and health, is one of the main
mandates of regional, national and supranational governments. In Europe, adequate access to such services is
guaranteed in the EU pillar of social rights (EU, 2018) and greatly promotes territorial cohesion (EC, 2022; Franco &
da Costa, 2023; Rauhut et al., 2013). Together with economic services of general interest such as retailers and banks,
they play an important role in shaping the socio-economic and spatial character of settlements (ESPON, 2017a).
However the allocation of scarce resources for service provision obliges private and public investors to manage costs

costs inter alia (Kompil et al., 2019, 2022). Furthermore, these costs may grow as a consequence of ageing and popu-

      Using population grids and universal and harmonized methods (Dijkstra & Poelman, 2014) we can identify
almost 50,000 settlements across the EU and UK, composed of cities, towns and villages. Some attention has already
been given to the functional dependencies of those settlements on, for instance, agglomerated labour and housing
markets (e.g., Dijkstra et al., 2019; Vanderstraeten & Van Hecke, 2019). Much less is known about the value of those
settlements as central service hubs. According to ESPON (2014) only a part of the EU member states even recog-
nizes that small settlements can have a significant territorial role. This is problematic because, arguably, policies may
be much more effective if they are targeted on settlements that “punch above their weight.” Among others, Powe
and Shaw (2004) confirm that policies that support small market towns can be effective to maintain rural service pro-
vision. Such policies would require comprehensive and harmonized information on all the public and private services
provided by different settlements across the EU territory, which to our best knowledge is currently not available.
      To fill the knowledge gap, this paper proposes a hierarchical classification of centres based on their position in
settlement networks. It ranks settlements based on their population size compared to other settlements in the vicin-
ity. A settlement is considered higher-ranking if it takes more time to reach a settlement that has more population,
so that a settlement that is the largest in a 10-minute car drive is considered lower on the hierarchy than a settle-
ment that is the largest in a two-hour drive. This local hierarchy ranking has been calculated comprehensively for all
known settlements across the EU and UK. Combinations with local service provision estimates confirm that local
hierarchies are a relevant ordering mechanism to understand social and economic service provision throughout
Europe's territory. In addition, clear hierarchical preferences are identified for several service types, after controlling
for population size.
      The objectives of this paper are to introduce a method based on road travel times and settlement population
counts, in order to infer a hierarchical classification of centrality in settlements, and to present its results for the
EU27 and UK; to validate its merits by analysing how well the captured hierarchy helps explain the endowment of
service locations; and to verify whether differences in transport-network efficiency coincide with our ranking and
endowment of services. For service endowment, we consider both economic and social services of general interest.
We consider the introduced approach a pragmatic heuristic that allows us to capture a Christallerian hierarchy of
central places in Europe (Christaller, 1934). It relies only on estimated settlement sizes and road network connectiv-
ity, so that it can be applied globally in principle. The classification method has been executed using spatial data and
fast shortest-path algorithms available in the open-source GeoDMS software (ObjectVision, 2014). Statistical

2     |       S E T T L E M E N T H I E RA R C H I E S I N T H E L I T E RA T U R E

Arbitrary settlement hierarchies abound in regional and national systems, owing to, for instance, political or clerical
decisions, but those have a limited usage for a universally applicable method. There have been numerous efforts to
rank settlements objectively, based on their relative global or national sizes, mainly focusing on city size distribution
(see e.g. Gabaix, 1999; Wang et al., 2021) and returns to scale (see e.g., Bettencourt et al., 2007). Other classification
attempts focus on global or continental city networks (Hall, 2004; Taylor & Derudder, 2016). However, none of
these approaches give a satisfying answer on what the service providing functionality is of local settlements for their
immediate hinterlands, while nevertheless hierarchies do exist among all settlements, not only major cities
(Batty, 2006).
      The functional hierarchy of settlements has been a topic of research since the introduction of central place the-
ory (CPT) by Christaller (1934) and Lösch (1940). For a comprehensive recent overview of research on CPT we refer
to Mulligan et al. (2012). Essentially, CPT entails a number of assumptions and propositions by which a spatially
repeating system of retail and service providing clusters can be understood. Those clusters vary in complexity and
level of specialization, with low complexity and low-specialization clusters occurring often across space, while high
complexity, high-specialization clusters occur scarcely. The processes that cause these repeated hierarchical systems
can for instance be described as a spatial evolution (Allen & Sanglier, 1979), or through micro-economics
(Hotelling, 1929; Mulligan, 1984). Regardless of the underlying processes, service provider roles come about because
of differentials in consumer choices and service provider decisions (Mulligan et al., 2012). Both aspects feed into a
set of characteristics by which central settlement hierarchies can possibly be identified.



2.1       |     Consumer choices

Consumer choices follows a trade-off between closer distances and richer choices. Consumers tend to prefer dis-
tance minimization for goods that are for example standardized and frequently demanded, while they prefer sizeable
diverse clusters for goods that are differentiated and demanded less frequently. These consumer choices would lead
to a spatial system in which services that need to be acquired often are offered in local centres at a high spatial fre-
quency, while more specialized services tend to cluster in major centres at a low spatial frequency. Some argue that
small local centres are jeopardized by the ubiquity of automobiles (Jacobs, 2007; Powe & Shaw, 2004); nevertheless,
local centres are still relevant commercial service providers (Van Leeuwen & Rietveld, 2011).



2.2       |     Economic service provider decisions

From an economic service-provider perspective follows a trade-off with, on the one hand, the need to be close to
potential users and maximize distance to potential competitors; and on the other hand, the need to accumulate
424                                                                                                 JACOBS-CRISIONI ET AL.



sufficient critical consumer mass to provide the service efficiently or profitably. For public services, returns to scale
have been shown repeatedly (Kenny, 1982; OECD & JRC, 2021). For commercial services, agglomeration benefits
may play out through productivity, diversity and competition benefits (De Groot et al., 2016; Jacobs, 1969). Given
that acceptable distances, needed market size and other agglomeration benefits play out differently depending on
the provided service or good (Van Leeuwen & Rietveld, 2011), different service providers will have diverging location
preferences.



2.3      |   Social service provider decisions

Market considerations also play out in social service provision; Kompil et al. (2019) have recently shown that indeed
social service provision across Europe can be simulated accurately using multiple service tiers with different thresh-
olds regarding minimum market size and maximum spatial extents. However, equity considerations also come into
play when considering social services. Public intervention is likely to maintain spatial access to local facilities despite
the presumably high cost of maintaining service locations in sparsely populated areas (OECD & JRC, 2021). In fact a
sizeable strand of literature focuses on finding societally optimal locations for social services such as postal, health
and education facilities (see e.g., Baik & Murray, 2022; Fortney, 1996; Mercier et al., 2021; Pacheco &
Casado, 2005). It may therefore be expected that, compared with economic services, social service facilities are
somewhat less responsive to local market sizes to ensure adequate spatial accessibility.



2.4      |   Identifying settlement roles in service provision

The most straightforward method to infer settlement roles in service provision would be through observed spatial
interactions or service provision levels. Such an approach has been applied using commuting data (ESPON, 2014;
Sýkora & Mulíček, 2009), but taking into account only spatial interactions for employment may bias results, as com-
muting is not uniform among labour market sizes and educational attainment (Groot et al., 2012), and in any case is
subject to gender and age-specific labour market participation rates. Ideally, settlement functionality is established
by employing comprehensive, harmonized and spatially sufficiently detailed data on the acquirement of goods and
services across the European territory. Unfortunately, currently such data remains evasive for academic inquiries.
Even currently available data on the provision of services e.g., (e.g., ESPON, 2017b) would be of limited use, as pub-
licly available data that describes European service providers has a limited scope, and, because many such service
location data are crowdsourced with considerable spatial variation in degrees of participation, likely suffer from clas-
sification, commission and omission biases (Johnson et al., 2016; Yeow et al., 2021).
      Alternatively, settlement functionality may be inferred from modelling, for example through the dynamic model-
ling approaches that were particularly popular in the 1970s and 1980s. However, models that set out to simulate the
emergence of functional hierarchies are very complex even when assuming uniform population distributions, and
accounting for a limited number of sectors or offered goods (Mulligan et al., 2012; White, 1978). Europe's population
is not distributed uniformly, and in practice, the number of public and commercial goods and services that a
European settlement may offer is almost boundless, so that a sector-based simulation procedure is impractical for
our identification aim.
      A pragmatic solution would be to identify settlement functionality based on population size. Christaller (1934)
for example offers a rigid set of bounding conditions on market size that supposedly govern the degree of centrality
of settlements (see Bell & Jayne, 2009, for an overview) and thus could be used for identification. However, these
static market size estimates do not take into account spatial and temporal dynamics in agglomeration and travel cost,
which are known to be relevant for the establishment of settlement hierarchies (White, 1977). In addition, Van
Leeuwen and Rietveld (2011) confirm that the importance of towns with a given population size for shopping differs
JACOBS-CRISIONI ET AL.                                                                                                        425



considerably between European countries, seemingly depending on population densities in the towns' broader spa-
tial contexts. This is in line with the notion that the economic performance of European cities depends partially on
their capacity to “borrow size” from connected cities (Meijers et al., 2016). In the remainder of this paper we will
confirm that local size and neighbourhood context are relevant when identifying settlement service provision in a
hierarchical structure, and extend downwards to the smallest settlements.



3          |   I D E N T I F Y I N G PO T E N T I A L L O C A L C E N T R E S

We propose to use the relative (population) size of a settlement within a prespecified travel time radius as a proxy of
centrality. We use our approach to evaluate whether the settlement is a centre for any service of general interest for
which acceptable travel times coincide with the imposed travel time radius. Travel-time bands thus function as a sim-
plification of the distance-decay functions often imposed in CPT models (Allen & Sanglier, 1979; White, 1978). We
establish whether the origin settlement is locally dominant, by verifying whether it is larger than any destination set-
tlement in the subset. This is done using a travel-time matrix T ij and settlement population counts P considering set-
tlements of origin i and destination j. Whether the settlement i is the largest locally, given radius t, is then evaluated
per origin for all the destination settlements in J through L by a product so that:

      The logic behind this classification of locally largest settlements consists of two assumptions that revolve around
services with a universal demand. The first assumption is that if services have a maximum acceptable travel time
t for the majority of consumers, there will most likely be a service provider within that radius. If there is insufficient
market potential within acceptable travel time, providers can diversify, or adjust their consumer prices, or rely on
public transfers to be maintained. On the other hand, if there is ample market potential, multiple providers may settle
within the market either in agglomerations or dispersed, depending on characteristics of the offered service and on
provider strategy (Mulligan et al., 2012).
      The second assumption is that services likely settle in the largest settlement within a market. There are numer-
ous conceivable reasons why a service provider would settle in the locally largest settlement. As it has the largest
population, it would have the biggest direct market, which is attractive given potential spatial competition. In reason-
ably regular spatial systems, settling in the largest settlement often minimizes aggregate travel times for the entire
market (OECD & JRC, 2021). In the case of public services, local democratic processes are relevant, which benefits
the largest settlement as it likely has the most voting power (Bjarnason, 2021). In case of spatial competition,
Hotelling's (1929) central location considerations may apply as well. And lastly, inertia may be relevant, as in the
absence of major technological shocks, positive feedback loops apply (Allen & Sanglier, 1979), so that largest settle-
ments presumably maintain their historical advantage by accumulating population and service providers.



3.1      |   Data sources and identification in practice

The accurate representation of settlement centrality hinges on the correct identification of settlements as nodes in
which service providers can plausibly settle. We use settlements that have been identified in the process of esta-
blishing degrees of urbanization in Europe. This has been done using 1  1km population grids from the 2011 EU
census, applying universal and harmonized methods that were introduced by Dijkstra and Poelman (2014), computed
globally and expanded in thematic resolution by Dijkstra et al. (2018, 2021), and recently endorsed by the United
Nations (European Commission, 2020). The settlements analysed in this paper encompass all physically separate,
spatially contiguous grid-based population clusters that have sufficient population mass and population density to be
considered a separate city, town or village. Areas that, due to their low population densities, may be considered sub-
urban, sparsely populated or uninhabited, are not included. All settlements are described as single areal units that
were generalized from grid units into vector polygons. Finally, all the cities and towns in the used settlements data-
base have recently been named (Lecomte & Poelman, 2020), while villages were named generically for the purposes
of this paper. By means of example, all settlements classified by degree of urbanization in Belgium are shown in
Figure 2. Belgium is considered a useful example here because it manifests sizeable differences in regional popula-
tion densities in a limited geography.
      For the purpose of this study, travel time matrices were calculated for every analysed country separately. Thus,
central settlements are not expected to impact service endowment across national borders. This is in line with the
finding that, despite the far-reaching international integration in the EU, cross-border interactions are at a disadvan-
tage (Jacobs-Crisioni & Koomen, 2017; Rodrik, 2000). On the other hand, discontinuities in for instance regulation or
taxation across borders may spur cross-border service usage in particular. In addition, cross-border commutes are
often linked with other cross-border activities, for instance between Belgium and Luxembourg (Carpentier, 2012).
We therefore verify the impact of being close to a border on service endowment.
      Fast travel time matrix operations in GeoDMS were taken advantage of to obtain travel times from every settle-
ment to every other domestic settlement within 2 hours driving. Travel times in T ij were obtained from information

        The results from the hierarchy ranking are shown comprehensively in Table 3 by settlement size class. Clearly,
very large settlements are much more likely to be the largest in the neighbourhood than very small settlements. As
can be expected, at small values of t, a slightly higher thresholds likely have a much bigger impact on classification

small islands including Tranebjerg in Denmark and Korissia in Greece; with connections from the mainland either

        An attractive feature of the proposed method is that it can help identify local centres even in contexts with low
population densities. For Figure 4, total population in all settlements within a 45 minute drive are compared with set-
tlement size for settlements that are, or are not, the largest in 45 minutes. We consider total settlement population
within 45 minutes an adequate proxy for local population density. Consistently, only the settlements that are at the
low end of the graph are the largest within 45 minutes, so that the method identifies likely local centres even in very
low density contexts, while classification approaches based only on national size rankings would presumably omit
those.



4       |    I N F E R RE D S E R V I C E P R O V I D E R R O L E A N D S E R V I C E E N D O W M E N T

In order to verify the usefulness of the proposed inference method, service endowment has been analysed for
ten services of general interest. The aim is to correlate our local centrality classification as comprehensively as
possible with service locations. The only criterion for selection of service location has been availability of relevant
points of interest with Europe-wide coverage. The full list of included service types, and some general statistics,
is given in Table 4. The selected service types include very locally served, for example primary schools and retail;
and much higher order services, for example universities and cinemas. Some of these services may be considered
social services (for instance, schools and healthcare facilities) and others economic services (such as retail and cin-
emas). We also include train stations as a coarse indication of public transport provision in settlements. Train sta-
tions potentially provide an interesting contrast with the other included services, as network economics instead

4.1           |   Measuring service endowment

In order to verify endowment, service locations from POI databases need to be attributed to settlements. However,
facility locations that serve a settlement are regularly not placed within the bounds of analysed settlements. Services
have therefore been attributed to the settlement by means of Euclidean distance to the closest grid cell inside a
selected settlement. These linkages have been stored to visualize the attribution connections. For the sake of visuali-
zation, those connections are stored as connecting the population-weighted centroid of a settlement, so that these
do not explicitly visualize the established linkage between facility and grid cell. The attribution and visualization pro-
cess are shown schematically in Figures 5 and 6.
        Some observed service locations are in remote locations, fairly distant from any settlement. To ensure that iso-
lated service locations are not falsely attributed to a settlement, any service that is more than 2.5 km away from the
closest settlement is not accounted for. In most countries and for most services, this has a limited impact on the ser-
vice counts in settlements, as Table 4 shows that the vast majority of services could be accounted for through attri-
bution to the closest settlement. Train stations perform worse here, with only 78% of all observed train stations in
Europe being within 2.5 km from a settlement border. Closer inspection has shown that many rail stops in, for

4.2        |   Different settlement types and sizes differ in service endowment

The information gap that this paper tries to bridge is based on the assumption that service endowments are
not uniformly distributed over different settlement types and sizes. To verify this, we tested whether service
endowment per 100,000 inhabitants differs significantly between settlement types and between settlement size
classes. Tests were performed using ANOVA and Tukey comparisons, and results are considered statistically sig-
nificant at the 95% confidence interval. Our null hypotheses are that there is no significant difference in ser-
vices per capita between settlement types, or between settlement sizes. Settlement types distinguish villages,
towns and urban centres, and are inherent to the settlement creation procedure. Settlement sizes are based on
population and discern four size classes from 25,000 to over 250,000 inhabitants, which were chosen on an ad
hoc basis.
      The results of this analysis are discussed in detail in Appendix B. For all included types of services, we can
reject the null hypothesis. There are statistically significant differences between urban centres, towns and villages
in terms of average number of services per 100,000 inhabitants. For most services, towns and villages have more
services per capita. Universities are an exception, as there are significantly more universities per capita in cities. Ser-
vice endowment is also heterogenous when discerning settlements by population size, for example, settlements
above 25.000 inhabitants, as for most service types there are significantly more facility locations per capita in the
smallest discerned settlement category. Surprisingly, the provision of doctors and universities does not show statis-
tically significant differences between settlements of different size classes. For universities, this is possibly due to
the very limited number of universities across the territory studied. For the presence of doctors, an explanation
remains evasive. Potentially data omissions and/or classification issues have a sizeable impact on the results for this
category.

4.3        |   Inferred centrality and service endowment

Service endowment is not uniform across settlement types and sizes. In Table 6 we list results from the three settle-
ments indicated in Figure 3. Arlon overshoots Antwerp and Brussels in terms of per capita service endowment. This
may partly be because of issues with population counts. The population counts only reflect the population within
settlement clusters, which is less than half of the municipal population in Arlon (12,714 vs roughly 32,000), while the
Antwerp and Brussels settlement boundaries encompass multiple municipalities, so that the population sizes of the
latter settlements are probably a better approximation of actual settlement market size. Nevertheless, even when
taking the Arlon municipality population, its per capita service endowment considerably overshoots the two cities. In
addition, despite its much bigger size, Brussels has higher per capita service endowment for all service types except
doctors, which lends some credibility to the earlier suggestion that Antwerp has a slightly smaller service provider
role due to its proximity to Brussels.
      As the example with selected Belgian settlement already indicates, the service provider roles inference that is
proposed in this paper is useful to explain some of the heterogeneity found. Figure 7 clearly shows that villages that
are the largest settlement within 45 minutes are much more likely to have specific services, with over 50% of all such
villages having a primary school, retail, a pharmacy and a bank, despite having at most 5,000 inhabitants. Clearly, set-
tlements that are the largest in a larger geographical area are much more likely to act as a service provision cluster
for their hinterlands.

to have more service locations. From these results it is also immediately clear that, after controlling for population size,
the proposed centre ranking matters for service provision. In line with central place theories (Christaller, 1934;
Lösch, 1940; Mulligan et al., 2012), the analysed service types have very diverging preferences with regard to spatial
extent. Some service types (retail, primary schools, pharmacies) are more likely present in greater numbers in all settle-
ments that are dominant at low travel time thresholds; while other services (cinemas and universities in particular) are
mostly indifferent to local centrality, and are only likely present in settlements that are central at high travel time
thresholds. Figure 8 shows how the results from Table 7 play out when predicting number of services for a non-isolated
settlement with 10,000 inhabitants, for increasing travel time thresholds at which the settlement is the largest. From
this Figure, it is easy to discern services that operate on different spatial scales, such as proposed by Kompil et al.
(2019). On the local scale (discernible preference for centres up to 30 minutes), primary schools, retail and pharmacies.
On the subregional scale (discernable preference for centres up to 45 minutes), secondary schools, banks and hospitals.
On the regional scale (discernable preference for centres over 60 minutes), cinemas and universities.
        The proximity of borders plays out differently for economic and social services of general interest. In general,
economic services including retail and banks have increased presence close to borders, likely partially to profit from
discontinuities in regulation and taxation across borders. On the other hand social services, which are often
restricted to serve only domestic residents, have less presence close to borders, presumably because national bor-
ders cut into their market. Borders thus disparately affect the quantity of services in border towns in the established
central hierarchy; but they do not seem to affect the hierarchy itself, as regressions yield similar results for the L(t)
variables if we leave out all settlements that are close to national borders.2
        Railway stops are hardly affected by local ranking at all, as travel demand and network path considerations are
presumably the dominant determinant for railway connectivity. This may mean that suburban settlements in metro-
politan regions are much more likely to have a train station, while in regions with relatively small settlements, a train
station is allocated to whichever settlement is on the railway's least cost path between major urban centres. With
Pearson correlation scores between 0.24 and 0.32, there is little correlation between the number of train stations
and other services in Europe's settlements. This signals that, despite evidence of its relevance for historical popula-
tion growth (Koopmans et al., 2012), train accessibility has a limited impact on service provision and thus the
present-day functionality of settlements; and vice versa, train connections provide little spatial access to services.

        Tests shows that, as can be expected, the probability that a settlement is inferred to be a local centre (at e.g., 10 or
20 minute thresholds) decreases with higher effective speeds,3 so that faster networks cause sparser inferred regional
centres. Changes in the road network may thus cause a reshuffling of neighbourhood hierarchies, and service provision
in cascade. To verify if indeed service provision is organized in settlements with a given time extent, despite the Euclid-
ean reach of that settlement, we have fitted Equation (2) on two separate subsets of settlements that have an effective
speed that is at least 11% above or below national average effective speeds. These subsets entail the roughly 15% best
and worst connected settlements across the European territory. If service provision is fully organized on road travel
times rather than on distances, we can expect that the same time thresholds affect services equally in both subsets. In
comparison with overall responses to effective speeds, the results in Table 9 are more mixed. Some services are more
likely affected by speed improvements than others. Secondary schools and cinemas yield significant positive estimators
for roughly identical travel-time thresholds, so that it is likely that these service types are organized fully on travel
times. Services that tend to be close to their user groups, including primary schools, retail and pharmacies, appear much
less sensitive to travel time thresholds, and are more likely organized on a distance basis.



5        |     C O N CL U S I O N S

Through their role as service centres for their hinterland, some small settlements have a value that far exceed their
population count (Bell & Jayne, 2009; Rondinelli, 1983). Existing methods to identify settlement hierarchies are typi-
cally based only on for instance labour market dependencies or population counts, and to some extent fail to capture
the service provider roles of smaller settlements. Indeed, service endowment is heterogenous among different settle-
ment classes and sizes. Through verifying whether a settlement is the most populated in a range of travel time
thresholds, this paper establishes a method to infer a centre-role or a centrality hierarchy for all roughly 50,000 set-
tlements identified across the EU27 and UK. Using POI data on a range of services, we validate whether the
established hierarchies are relevant for explaining the service endowment of settlements.
        Tests show that the proposed hierarchy is meaningful for service provision. Settlements that are inferred to have a
local or regional centre role have significantly more service locations than other settlements of the same size. This con-
firms the notion that European cities depend on both local size and their connected neighbourhood (Meijers
et al., 2016), and extends it to even the smallest settlements across the EU and UK. The proposed method thus adds
useful information on Europe's settlement structure. In line with expectations from central place theory
(Christaller, 1934; Lösch, 1940; Mulligan et al., 2012), we find that different service types operate on different time
bands. We can establish services at roughly three scale levels, in line with Kompil et al. (2019). Local services including
schools, retail and pharmacies, prefer settlements that are the largest in a 20-minute range. Supralocal services, includ-
ing secondary schools, banks and hospitals, prefer the largest settlements in a 30 to 60-minute range. Regional services,
including cinemas and universities, prefer settlements that are dominant for even larger travel time thresholds.

      We consider our classification as complementary to prevailing functional definitions, which typically depend on
labour market agglomerations e.g., (e.g., Dijkstra et al., 2019; Vanderstraeten & Van Hecke, 2019). Our approach can
assist in finding small central settlements that, in terms of service provision, punch above their weight and are essen-
tial for service provision in a more rural context. We therefore believe our approach can be helpful in guiding invest-
ments for territorial cohesion, as well as for prioritization in strategic national and regional spatial plans. By helping
to identify the territorial role of settlements, this approach may even assist in local zoning and traffic regulation. We
must, however, emphasize that the introduced method is an heuristic. It does not take into account many relevant
factors including historical inertia. It also depends on secondary data that describe population sizes and road travel
times, which may deviate in practice. In addition, the validation is mostly based on crowdsourced data with likely
considerable commission and omission errors (Johnson et al., 2016; Yeow et al., 2021). Thus, the presented results
should not be considered as a substitute for local field work.
      The hierarchization of settlements is based on road travel times between settlements. As a consequence, settle-
ments are less likely to maintain a central role in contexts with faster road connections. If services are indeed orga-
nized on travel times, rather than on distances, this implies that road network improvements could cause a further
concentration of services. In that case, speeding up networks becomes an Sisyphean task, as reducing travel times
leads to services moving further away, only increasing the lock-in to fast personal transport (Driscoll, 2014). We con-
firm that almost all service types are affected by faster networks, so that settlements have less services on average if
their connections are faster, presumably due to spatial concentration. However, when looking only at settlements
with relatively slow or relatively fast connections, the picture is more mixed. While some services stick to the same
time bands regardless of network speeds, other services seem to have different time band preferences depending on
network speeds. This may be because of spatial structural elements that are unaccounted for, or because these ser-
vices are not indifferent to distance considerations. More research is needed on this topic to understand the role of,
for instance, spatial structure and location decisions better.

Resumen. Las clasificaciones nacionales de ciudades no describen adecuadamente la relevancia local y regional de
una población. Este estudio introduce un método para clasificar la importancia de las poblaciones en función del
tiempo de viaje y el tamaño de la población. Se investigó la relación entre el tamaño de una población, si es un centro
local o regional, y su dotación de servicios. El enfoque explica adecuadamente las dotaciones de servicios per cápita
en Europa y añade matices que las clasificaciones nacionales no captan plenamente. Además, establece una
caracterización de los tipos de servicio por preferencias discernibles empíricamente en la escala espacial. Por último,
los resultados sugieren que la dotación de servicios en las poblaciones puede reorganizarse debido a la mayor rapidez
de las conexiones por carretera, con servicios que se concentran espacialmente en las zonas mejor conectadas.



抄録: 国の都市ランキングは、集落の地域的な重要性を適切に説明するものではない。移動時間と集落の規模に
基づいて集落の中心性をランク付けする手法を導入した。地域の中心であるか否かにかかわらず、集落の規模と
サービスエンダウメントとの関係を調査する。このアプローチは、ヨーロッパの一人当たりのサービスエンダウ
メントをうまく説明するもので、国別ランキングでは完全には捉えきれていない微妙な部分が示される。さらに、空
間スケールで経験的に認識できる選好性によってサービスタイプの特性評価を確立する。結論として、結果からに集
落のサービスエンダウメントはより速い道路接続によって刷新されることが可能であり、サービスはより良い道路接
続の地域に空間的に集中することが示唆される。
