         Urban Polycentricity and the Costs of

                                       PAOLO VENERI


   ABSTRACT Polycentricity at the metropolitan scale is perhaps the model of spatial organi-
   sation that needs to be investigated more thoroughly as regards its effects on travel. The aim of
   this paper is to test the role of polycentricity—as well as other spatial characteristics, such as
   compactness, functional diversification and size—in the costs of commuting, taking into account
   an external cost component (per-capita CO2 emissions) and a private cost component (time spent
   on travelling). The degree of urban polycentricity has been measured by adopting a dynamic
   approach based on commuting flows and on social network analysis tools. The analysis is carried
   out using a database of 82 Italian metropolitan areas (MAs). Results show that MAs with a
   higher degree of polycentricity are more virtuous both in terms of private and external costs of
   mobility, while the degree of compactness is associated with lower environmental costs but with
   higher private costs. Size is associated with both higher external and private costs, while the role
   of functional diversification turns out to be statistically insignificant. Socio-demographics also
   play a role.

Introduction

T      he spatial organisation of cities and its effects on welfare, economic growth,
       and environmental sustainability has increasingly captured the attention of
economists and other social scientists in the past two decades. In this debate, one
of the most relevant topics is about the relationship between the characteristics of
urban spatial organisation—i.e., polycentricity, dispersion, size, and mixed land
use—and mobility patterns (Ewing and Cervero 2001). Mobility yields relevant
social costs that include both private components (i.e., bus ticket, fuel and time
spent travelling) and external components (i.e., negative environmental externali-
ties like global warming and pollution). While the former directly affects the

commuters’ utility, the latter affects the welfare of the whole society and it is not
considered in the individual’s travel decisions.
   Among the spatial characteristics of cities, the degree of polycentricity is
perhaps the one for which most effort must still be made to shed some light on its
economic effects and externalities, especially those generated by travel behaviour
(Schwanen, Dijst, and Dieleman 2002). The relevance of polycentricity arises
from the fact that polycentric spatial organisation has been playing a relevant
normative role for more than 10 years. In fact, polycentricity is now considered a
planning tool to enhance cities’ competitiveness, social cohesion, and environ-
mental sustainability (Davoudi 2003). However, these hypotheses have not yet
been empirically investigated enough. Moreover, polycentricity itself needs to be
better defined and measured, especially at the metropolitan level. Most of the
attempts to measure urban polycentricity in the literature are based on static and
morphological approaches, which mainly consider the spatial distribution of
employment or of population within metropolitan areas (MAs), without focusing
on the interactions among urban nodes.
   The aim of this paper is to test whether a high degree of urban polycentricity
is virtuous in terms of the costs of mobility, and thus, can be considered as a
desirable planning goal. While trying to answer this question, other relevant
characteristics of urban spatial organisation have also been considered as potential
determinants of the commuting costs. More in depth, the role of urban size,
dispersion, and functional diversity have been assessed.
   The analysis of polycentricity at a metropolitan level requires a definition and
identification of sub-centres. From a morphological perspective, urban sub-
centres are usually identified with employment density thresholds. In this paper,
an attempt is made to identify sub-centres after having defined them from an
interaction perspective rather than from a morphological one. In this way, urban
sub-centres are defined as places with central functions that organise the territory
around them. Their identification is hence carried out using dynamic approaches
based on social network analysis tools, which allow to capture the centrality of
urban nodes and their hierarchic ranking.
   In section 2, a review of the literature on the relationship between urban
structure and mobility patterns is carried out, with particular attention on the role
of polycentricity. Section 3 defines the concept of polycentricity and proposes two
indicators—for reasons of robustness—to measure it appropriately. Section 4
reviews the commuting patterns in the Italian MAs and proposes two indicators to
measure external and private costs related to mobility, respectively. The former
has been measured by focusing on CO2 emissions, while the latter has been
approximated by the time spent on travelling. In section 5, the causal links

between the characteristics of urban spatial structure and costs of mobility are
explained and then tested empirically with an econometric cross-section analysis.
Section 6 concludes.

Literature: A Still Open Debate
   The role of urban structure is now recognised as a factor that facilitates travel
because it provides the framework for the location of houses and firms (Hickman
and Banister 2007). The characteristics of spatial organization that are supposed to
be the most relevant in shaping commuting patterns at a metropolitan level are
basically polycentricity, dispersion, mixing use and size (Tsai 2001).
   Polycentricity and commuting. Polycentricity is perhaps the characteristic
on which knowledge is less consolidated among scholars, regarding its effects on
commuting patterns (Aguilera 2005; Levine 1992; Schwanen, Dijst, and Diele-
man 2002). Some authors (Gordon and Wong 1985; Tsai 2001) argue that poly-
centricity has virtuous effects on mobility patterns. This has been put forward for
several reasons.
   First, polycentric metropolitan organisation favours proximity between work
and home—the so-called “co-location hypothesis” (Gordon and Wong 1985: 666),
reducing the distance travelled (Handy 1996). Distances can be reduced also
because of lower rent near sub-centres if compared with monocentric areas
(Cervero and Wu 1997), encouraging housing near the workplace. However, the
rigidity in residence mobility—especially in the short run—can in part compen-
sate for these effects.
   Second, polycentric areas can favour the competitiveness of mass transit
(Breheny 1995; Susilo and Maat 2007), reducing the use of less sustainable
private means of transport (e.g., cars, motorbikes, etc.). In fact, sub-centres ensure
a minimum dimensional scale as regards the supply of a competitive public
transport system. Moreover, polycentric MAs, which are characterised by two or
more central nodes, are the natural environment for cross-commuting patterns
between such nodes, which can favour the use of the public transport system
(Susilo and Maat: 607).
   Third, polycentricity can also influence the time spent on travelling. Polycentric
urban structures are thought to reduce the excessive congestion that characterises
monocentric areas, especially those of big dimension (Parr 2004: 235–236).
Diseconomies of congestion can be avoided through the distribution of popula-
tion, employment, and urban functions in several sub-centres, without renouncing
the advantages of agglomeration. In other words, polycentricity can be interpreted
as a fair compromise between the need for safe urban compactness and the need
to lighten congestion, while the city is growing.
406    GROWTH AND CHANGE, SEPTEMBER 2010



    Despite the above-mentioned arguments that make polycentricity a poten-
tially desirable planning goal, only a few empirical analyses have been carried
out to corroborate these hypotheses. What is more, the available empirical
works show quite contrasting results, so that the relation between polycentricity
and the costs of mobility still remains unclear. In a work on the MA of San
Francisco, for example, Cervero and Wu (1998) note that decentralisation of
employment in several sub-centres is associated with an increase in commuting
distances and in car drivers. Other scholars have found similar results, even for
European cases (Aguilera 2005; Aguilera and Mignot 2004; Levinson and
Kumar 1994; Næss 2007). Schwanen, Dieleman, and Dijst (2004), in an analy-
sis of the Dutch urban system, show that polycentric cities—if compared with
monocentric ones—are associated with longer commutes, both in terms of time
and distance.
    On the other hand, other studies show that decentralisation of employment
tends to reduce commute duration, both in Europe (Alpkokin et al. 2005; Owens
1986) and in the U.S. (Gordon, Richardson, and Jung 1991; Tsai 2001). Contrast-
ing results can be found even with respect to the share of mass transit users. In
particular, several studies show that the progressive transformation of cities from
monocentric to polycentric is associated with a decrease in public transport users
(Cervero and Wu 1998; Næss 2007; Schwanen, Dijst, and Dieleman 2002), while
others show the contrary (Bento et al. 2005).
    These contrasting results are at least partially due to the way in which the
concept of polycentricity is defined and measured. In almost all the cases, poly-
centricity is intended to be the result of a mere process of decentralisation from the
CBD to the emergent sub-centres. From this perspective, a sub-centre is simply
defined as a place with an employment density that is higher than the surrounding
territory. This interpretation can be sound for North-American cities, while it is
less appropriate for European ones, where urban areas formed mostly through a
process of coalescence rather than decentralisation (García López and Muñiz
2005). As a consequence, the analysis of urban polycentricity in European cities
must take into account a more complex definition of urban centres, which can be
interpreted as high-hierarchy places that supply central functions to the surround-
ing territory. Hence, the simple density-based approach does not seem to be
correct.
    In addition, the fact that in the last 20 years an important phenomenon of
decentralisation has occurred, in tandem with an increase of commuting distance
travelled, does not necessarily mean that polycentricity yields worse commuting
patterns. Once again, the process of decentralisation that occurred in almost all the
European MAs has not necessarily led to polycentricity. This process, in fact, is
                     POLYCENTRICITY AND COMMUTING COSTS                          407



associated with a twofold evolution: polycentric development or simply dispersed
urban areas (Lee 2007). This could be another reason to explain the ambiguity of
the results of empirical works.
    The role of the other spatial characteristics. In addition to polycentricity,
other spatial characteristics of cities can shape commuting patterns and their
effects. First, urban dispersion is considered one of the most important spatial
determinants concerning the increase in commuting distances (Banister 1999),
mass transit inefficiency (Ellison 1995) and energy consumption (Næss 1995;
Newman and Kenworthy 1989). There is already quite a wide consensus among
scholars over the disequilibria and externalities—especially the environmental
ones—generated by low density settlements and scattered patterns of urban devel-
opment, even for Italian cities (Calafati 2003). On the other hand, the effects on
private costs, like the time spent on travelling, are still unclear. Regarding U.S.
cities, for example, Glaeser and Kahn (2004) and Gordon, Kumar, and Richardson
(1989) observe that higher densities are associated with higher travel times. On
the other hand, Levinson and Kumar (1997) find a negative relationship between
density and travel times when density is beyond a given threshold, after which the
sign changes.
    Functional diversification represents another characteristic of urban spatial
organisation that can be important in shaping commuting patterns within cities.
An urban area is functionally diversified if it presents a job-housing balance or,
more generally, a diverse or mixed land-use (Camagni, Gibelli, and Rigamonti
2002; Cervero 1996). Jane Jacobs (1961) was one of the major advocates of such
a model of spatial organisation, even for cities’ economic prosperity and safety.
The empirical literature shows that the higher the level of diversity, the shorter the
distance travelled (Cervero 1996) and the higher the transit usage (Frank and Pivo
1994). In an analysis of six Italian MAs, Travisi and Camagni (2005) show that the
collective costs of mobility decrease in areas with higher density and mixed
land-use.
    Another key factor in shaping commuting patterns is the generalised
deregulation of house building that occurred in Europe during the last 20 years.
This, together with changing individual preferences and the widespread diffu-
sion of private transport, combined to bring about less transit oriented cities
and longer commuting travels. Newly built areas are often planned without
taking enough into account the needs for an efficient mass transit service,
yielding scattered and high land consumption settlements. Hence, the rapidity
of the house building process seems to be associated with less sustainable
mobility patterns (Camagni, Gibelli, and Rigamonti 2002). Cirilli and Veneri
(2007), in an analysis of the major Italian Local Labour Systems, show that

a newer housing stock is associated with higher polluting emissions due to
commuting.

Urban Polycentricity: Concept and Measure
    Polycentricity is emerging as an archetypal model of spatial organisation of
MAs (Anas, Arnott, and Small 1998). Polycentric areas can be conceptualised
based on two main dichotomies, depending on the perspective adopted. On the
one hand, adopting a pure morphological approach, polycentric areas can be
viewed as a model of spatial organisation midway between traditional compact
cities and urban sprawl. They maintain the advantages related to compact cities,
complying with the spontaneous tendencies to dispersion (Camagni, Gibelli, and
Rigamonti 2002: 52). On the other hand, adopting a both functional and morpho-
logical approach, polycentric areas represent an alternative to monocentric areas
(Meijers and Sandberg 2008). Hence, polycentricity is considered as the natural
evolution of monocentric spatial organisation. However, polycentricity can be the
result of two different territorial evolutions. The first consists in the decentralisa-
tion processes from the Central Business District (CBD) to new emerging sub-
centres. This pattern took place with particular intensity in U.S. cities. The second,
which prevails in European cities, consists of a progressive integration of pre-
existing self-contained centres in a single MA, through a coalescence (Calafati
2002) or an incorporation (Champion 2001) process.
    Using a traditional morphological approach, an urban area can be defined
polycentric if its employment is not concentrated in a single centre (Riguelle,
Thomas, and Verhetsel 2007: 195), but it is distributed in two or more centres.
Adopting a functional approach, on the other hand, the focus is not only on the
distribution of employment, but—more generally—on the spatial distribution of
functions (Hall 2009). With the functional approach, territorial units within an
MA must show two or more nodes or “centres” with a higher hierarchical level.
Given that, the definition of polycentricity at the urban level appears quite simple
and should not be a matter of debate. Instead, the concept of sub-centre needs
more attention. While from a pure morphological perspective a centre is simply a
place with a higher employment density than the hinterland, from a wider point of
view a centre is a place that carries out central functions and that organises the
surrounding territory. In order to get hold of these features, it is possible to look
at the way in which each node interacts with the others. Connections among nodes
and their distribution tell a lot about the way in which each place organises its
surrounding territory and about its hierarchic ranking within the MA.
    The first step to measure the degree of urban polycentricity consists of the
identification of sub-centres. For this purpose, there are a lot of studies that use
                     POLYCENTRICITY AND COMMUTING COSTS                         409



density-based approaches, especially in U.S. literature. Some of them use simple
absolute cut-offs of employment density and of total employment for census tracts
within MAs (Anderson and Bogart 2001; Cervero and Wu 1998; Giuliano and
Small 1991). Other more sophisticated studies use relative density-based
approaches, usually through the estimation of the local peaks of a density func-
tion, which often has a non-parametric specification (Craig and Ng 2001;
McDonald and Prather, 1994).
    Another family of approaches for the identification of urban sub-centres—
which has been followed in this work—is based on “dynamic” indicators, which
are built using flow data, such as commuting flows (Bourne 1989). Literature on
these dynamic methodologies has been developing noticeably in the last decade.
In a study of the Spanish MAs, Burns, Boix, and Roca (2001) use a threshold of
net entry flow of commuters (15 percent) above which an area can be thought of
as a sub-centre. They also add an absolute population dimension cut-off of 10,000
residents, similar to Giuliano and Small’s approach (1991). Other similar inter-
action approaches at the intra-urban level are those by Clark and Kuijpers-Linde
(1994) and by Van der Laan (1998). The latter study does not identify sub-centres,
but it divides a set of urban areas into four types of spatial structure looking at
patterns of commuting flows.
    Other dynamic approaches have been used, especially at the inter-urban level,
by the GaWC (Globalization and World Cities) research network (Derudder et al.
2003; Taylor et al. 2002). These approaches start conceptualising the network of
cities from a micro-founded perspective, looking at the location strategies of global
service firms and at the flows they generate. Derudder and Witlox (2008) give
another contribution to the investigation of the spatiality of World City Network
using airline flows. The work of Limtanakool, Dijst, and Schwanen (2007) repre-
sents another step ahead towards the identification and characterisation of urban
spatial structure from an interaction perspective. The authors consider three
dimensions, which they call the “three S” for characterising polycentricity at the
inter-urban level. The first two are the strength and the symmetry of the interac-
tions, while the third one is the structure of the network. The identification of
centres is carried out through the use of some dominance indexes.
    In this work, sub-centres of Italian MAs have been identified using a social
network analysis approach. The methodology starts conceptualising MAs as net-
works of municipalities—composed of nodes (municipalities) and links (flows of
commuting)—and then it uses the in-degree index to characterise the degree of
centrality of each node, as explained in Boix (2002). More in depth, for each
municipality within each MA, the in-degree index has been computed using
commuting flows data in 2001. The in-degree index measures the number of links

   Work commutes in Italy. Before analysing the relationship between spatial
characteristics and commuting costs, it is worth exploring the patterns of mobility
in Italy through simple descriptive statistics. The units of analysis are 82 Italian
MAs, which are identified applying the functional algorithm based on the Federal
Register’s SMA methodology (Office of Management and Budget 1990), with the
adaptations of Clusa and Roca (1997).3

The Spatial Determinants of Commuting Costs
   The aim of this section is to test to what extent the characteristics of urban spatial
structure influence the external (EIM) and private (ACT) costs of mobility. The
hypothesis that has to be tested is that these commuting costs are a function of some
characteristics of spatial structure which can be grouped in four categories.
   The first one is the MAs’ degree of polycentricity. Sub-centres present a
dimensional scale that facilitates the competitiveness of mass transit (Breheny
1995). Hence, a polycentric structure can facilitate a more intense use of public
transport systems and this in turn negatively affects the external costs of
mobility through a minor impact in terms of CO2 emissions (Amici della Terra
2005).
   Polycentricity, according to the co-location hypothesis (Gordon and Wong
1985), also increases job-house proximity. In fact, the presence of several sub-
centres increases the probability of finding a job near the place of residence. This,
in turn, allows a reduction in the distance and time spent on travelling, with a
virtuous effect on commuting costs.
   Urban compactness is also relevant in shaping commuting patterns and their
effects. Compact cities, whose degree of compactness is usually measured with
residential density, are expected to be associated with shorter travel distances,
thanks to a higher spatial concentration of houses and job places. Moreover, urban
compactness enhances the competitiveness of a mass transit system (Ellison
1995). On the other hand, the higher the cities’ compactness, the higher their level
of traffic congestion, which in turn could be associated with an increase in
commuting duration (Glaeser and Kahn 2004; Gordon, Kumar, and Richardson
1989).
                     POLYCENTRICITY AND COMMUTING COSTS                        417



    Compactness can also be analysed in terms of spatial concentration of jobs
within the metropolitan territory. For this purpose, the most applied measure is the
Gini index between jobs and area shares of each municipality within each MA
(Lee 2007). A higher concentration should be associated with shorter distances
travelled, while the effects on travel duration could be ambiguous because of the
congestion problems that may arise in highly concentrated areas.
    Another variable of urban spatial structure that can influence commuting
patterns is the functional diversification of the territory (mixed land use)
(Camagni, Gibelli, and Rigamonti 2002). More specifically, a mixed land use
favours the proximity between places of living and working. MAs with a high
degree of functional diversification are generally characterised by an adequate
equilibrium—within its territory—between residential and economic functions,
integrating, rather than segregating them spatially. Thanks to a reduction in the
distance between jobs and homes, functional diversification reduces the length of
travel and, as a consequence, the emissions of CO2 and of other pollutants.
Functional diversification of the territory has been approximated with a Gini index
between jobs and population shares within each MA (see Table 6). The more even
the distribution of jobs and population within a given MA, the more diversified is
its territory.
    The last urban spatial characteristic that affects travel behaviour is city-size
(Handy 1996). Total city surface area influences the average distance that has to be
travelled, as well as the amount of time spent travelling. Moreover, big cities are
expected to present an advantage in supplying efficient mass transit systems
because it is possible to reach minimum economies of scale that are necessary to
justify big investments and expensive infrastructures.
    The logical relationship among the four main spatial characteristics and the
dependent variables are visually represented in Figure 3, where left and right
rectangles identify exogenous and dependent variables, respectively. Beyond the
major spatial factors, a set of controls have been included in the analysis in order
to take into account non-spatial determinants that can play a role in shaping
commuting patterns. The role of these controls is not represented in the figure in
order to keep it easy enough to be interpreted. The inclusion of such elements
in the estimation, however, permits a better interpretation of the empirical
results and helps avoid, or at least reduce, problems of under-specification of the
model.

(house_age), which has been computed through the number of houses built after
1982 over the total number of houses. The underlying idea is that in the last
decades the speed of house-construction has increased (Muñiz, López, and

   The empirical analysis. In order to test these hypotheses, a cross-section
econometric model has been carried out using the above-mentioned 82 MAs as
units of analysis. I acknowledge that, in order to establish causality, longitudinal
data are to be preferred. However, given the limited availability of data, a cross-
section has been employed. All the independent variables have been computed
drawing on the Istat Population Census (1981, 2001) and the Istat Industry and
Services Census (2001). Dependent variables have been computed as explained in
section 4 (equations 3 and 4). Table 6 lists all the regressors, with a short descrip-
tion and measurement explanation. Regarding collinearity problems, they do not
seem to arise, as shown by the correlation structure of the independent variables
and their variance inflation factor (tables A1 and A2 in the Appendix).
   As regards polycentricity, an additional variable of morphological distribution
of employment within urban territory has been included (gini_area_job). More
specifically, it has been calculated through the Gini index between the share of the
area and employment of each municipality in each MA, as suggested in Tsai
(2005) and Lee (2007). The higher the variable, the more concentrated the
employment within each MA.
   Two cross sectional econometric models have been estimated. The first regards
external (environmental) costs (EIM), while the second adopts private costs (ACT)
as dependent variable. The coefficients of the former model have been estimated
through a spatial lag model, in order to clean the residuals from spatial depen-
dence problems (see tables A3–A4 in the Appendix).4 This estimation is carried
out with a maximum likelihood approach, weighting observation with a spatial
matrix of contiguity. Model 2, on the other hand, has been estimated through an
OLS method with robust standard errors.5 All the variables have been standardised
with zero mean and unit variance so as to give a better interpretation of coeffi-
cients. Results are shown in Table 7.
   As regards model 1, both the coefficients associated to the variables of func-
tional polycentricity are negative and statistically significant. This result is con-
firmed by the coefficient relative to the gini_area_job variable, which tells us that
the more concentrated the population within MAs, the lower the EIM indicator.
These findings corroborate the hypothesis that a higher degree of polycentricity is
associated with lower external costs of commuting in terms of average CO2
emissions per traveller. The effect of polycentricity on the average time duration
is also negative, but not statistically significant. Hence, it cannot be said that a
higher degree of polycentricity makes the journey shorter. This result is in accor-
dance with Levinson (1998), for whom the change in urban structure does not
affect travel times. However, the coefficient of the gini_area_job variable tells us
that the more concentrated the population, the longer the commutes.

    Estimation results show that urban compactness is an important factor in
explaining commuting costs. A higher density is associated with more sustainable
mobility patterns in terms of CO2 emissions, but with a longer travel duration. The
relation between density and commuting time is in accordance with the results of
Glaeser and Kahn (2004) and Gordon, Kumar, and Richardson (1989) for U.S.
MAs. Following these authors, this result is due, at least in part, to a congestion
effect that arises in high-density cities. Hence, a trade-off between external and
internal costs comes out and the net effect of density is still unknown.
    As regards urban functional diversity, its influence on mobility costs remains
unclear. Although the coefficients of the mixed land-use variable (gini_pop_job)
are negative for the external costs model, they lose statistical significance in one
of the two specifications. Thus, it cannot be stressed that functional diversification
has a significant relationship with the external costs of commuting. The same
results hold for private costs specifications, for which coefficients are always
insignificant, though in this case the sign of the relationship is positive.
    Urban size is probably the most important factor that is related to the impact of
mobility. As was expected, larger size is associated with higher CO2 emissions and
with more time spent on travelling. These results can be explained by the longer
distances that on average have to be travelled in larger MAs, which affect both
time and pollutant emissions.
    As far as the other control variables are concerned, it appears that the age of the
housing stock does not have a statistically significant effect. Coefficients of the
dummy variables in relation to the MAs’ geographic localisation are also insig-
nificant, except for the Southern MAs with respect to the external costs specifi-
cations. More specifically, southern cities show a higher impact in terms of CO2
emissions. This can be due to longer distances or to the scarce use of public means
of transport. On the other hand, a higher relative competitiveness of a mass transit
system is associated with a shorter duration of commutes, as shown by the
coefficient of the rel_public_comp variable.
    Among socio-demographics, the share of graduates is associated with a higher
level of CO2 emissions and higher travel times as was expected and explained
above. This result is stable for the two model specifications that have been
estimated. The age of the working population (demo_structure), on the other hand,
does not seem to play a statistically significant role.
    Finally, in the spatial lag model the spatial parameter (rho) is positive and
significant, showing that a proximity effect exists. In other words, neighbouring
MAs have similar characteristics which are not taken on by the model’s regres-
sors, but which can be associated to other factors related to proximity (e.g.,
cultural and technological factors).

Concluding Remarks
    Contemporary MAs are characterised by an increasingly polycentric spatial
structure. The polycentric paradigm promotes this concept as a planning tool to
enhance local competitiveness and sustainability. However, polycentricity is not
yet a well-defined concept and its effects on competitiveness and sustainability
still needs to be investigated both theoretically and empirically.
    The contribution of this work relies firstly on the formal definition of polycen-
tricity at the metropolitan scale and on its quantitative measurement for compara-
tive analysis. Secondly, and in more depth, it sheds some light on the effects of the
degree of urban polycentricity on two main components of commuting costs.
    Polycentricity has been conceptualised from an interaction perspective and it has
been measured with appropriate indicators based on tools of social network
analysis. A novel indicator of polycentricity has been introduced, and another one
has also been computed for reasons of robustness and completeness of the analysis.
    The analysis on 82 Italian MAs shows that polycentricity is a sustainable model
of urban spatial structure, especially from an environmental perspective because it
is associated with a reduction in CO2 emissions due to commuting. The results are
robust for two different measures of polycentricity, and they are also consistent
with the morphological indicators of employment concentration within MAs.
However, the degree of polycentricity does not seem to influence the time spent on
travelling in a significant way.
    Considering other characteristics of urban spatial structure, it comes out that
urban dispersion is associated with higher external costs, but also with smaller
private costs. Hence, the analysis shows a trade-off between the need to contain
collective costs of mobility (such as pollution and greenhouse gases) and the need
to satisfy housing preferences for low-density areas/neighbourhoods.
